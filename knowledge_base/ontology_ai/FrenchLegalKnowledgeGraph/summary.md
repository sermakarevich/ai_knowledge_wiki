# LLM-Assisted Ontology Engineering and Construction of a French Legal Knowledge Graph

**Paper:** [LLM-Assisted Ontology Engineering and Construction of a French Legal Knowledge Graph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24551v1)

## Human Readable TL;DR

French maintenance law is like a giant, scattered instruction manual written in dense legal language that is hard to search when you have a specific broken machine and a specific question about who must fix it and under which rule. This paper builds a kind of smart index for that manual: it first teaches two large language models to discover the recurring relationships in the texts, such as who is responsible for what, where it must be done, and what it applies to, and organizes them into a formal vocabulary grounded in an existing legal ontology. It then uses that vocabulary to read all 6,370 articles and turn them into a structured knowledge graph that can answer practical questions, much like converting a pile of paper manuals into a searchable database where every obligation is linked to its actors, equipment, and legal source.

## TL;DR

The paper presents a two-stage LLM-assisted workflow for French maintenance regulations that enriches a SEMLEG-based core ontology through open extraction, embedding-based fusion, and property induction on a stratified sample of 1,389 articles, then uses that ontology to guide closed triple extraction over the full corpus of 6,370 regulatory articles across 20 topics. The corpus is built from Légifrance texts filtered via Apave regulatory-guide references, and the pipeline is run in parallel with OpenAI and Mistral models to compare how different induced ontologies affect downstream knowledge graph construction. Fusion preserves all extracted relation statements while sharply compacting entities, predicates, signatures, and annotations into a usable ontology-grounded graph that scores 100% JSON validity and near-perfect class coverage but only 50–73% signature compliance. Competency-question tests framed as SPARQL queries confirm the graph can retrieve actor roles and legal justifications for maintenance actions, supporting integration into operational systems such as Computerized Maintenance Management Systems.

## Problem & Motivation

The work targets French maintenance regulations, described as complex legal texts that are difficult to exploit when addressing a specific case and challenging to integrate into operational systems such as Computerized Maintenance Management Systems. Existing LLM work on legal extraction is characterized as largely dataset-driven, without an end-to-end pipeline for moving from legal text to an ontology-grounded knowledge graph. The contribution is therefore a compact workflow that combines LLM flexibility with ontology-based structural constraints, together with a preliminary evaluation of the resulting ontology and knowledge graph. Competency questions focus on concrete maintenance obligations — the actors involved, the artifacts affected, the contextual conditions, and the legal sources — so that the graph can support practical retrieval rather than generic triple collection.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **SEMLEG-grounded two-stage workflow.** The pipeline separates ontology engineering from graph population: it starts from retained SEMLEG classes such as Actor, Action, Artifact, Condition, Source, Location, Reason, Situation, and Time, induces maintenance-specific properties from a stratified sample of about 10% of each domain–document title pair, and then performs closed extraction over the complete corpus guided by the resulting ontology. This keeps LLM creativity constrained by a stable legal core while allowing domain-specific extension.

2. **Open class-guided triple extraction with topic filtering.** Each sampled article is processed with three prompts covering entity extraction typed with retained SEMLEG classes via a compact Turtle Light serialization, open-vocabulary triple generation where relation labels are inferred from context while subject and object classes restrict plausible domain–range patterns, and topic assignment to maintenanceActivity, anotherLegalActivity, or legalCrossReference. Only triples classified as maintenanceActivity are retained for induction, focusing the ontology on maintenance obligations.

3. **Embedding-based fusion of entities and properties.** Entity labels are grouped per ontology class by subject or object role and merged when cosine similarity exceeds 0.7, keeping the most frequent variant as canonical, while properties are grouped by subject–object class pair with the same threshold. Legal references and numeric labels are excluded as unique or context-dependent values, turning noisy raw LLM output into a deduplicated fused triple set ready for induction and population.

4. **Batched property induction formalized into OWL axioms.** The induction sample takes 5% of fused maintenance triples per property with at least one triple per signature, split into 15 batches, where each batch prompt receives triples, the core ontology, and competency questions and returns candidate properties with label, domain, range, textual evidence, definition, aligned question, and confidence score. The outputs are formalized into OWL axioms, yielding two parallel SemLegM variants — 75 properties with 105 signatures for OpenAI versus 44 properties with 59 signatures for Mistral — kept separate to compare downstream effects.

5. **Fusion-aware evaluation of ontology-grounded construction.** The evaluation compares four graph variants (Mistral and OpenAI, each with and without fusion) on structural statistics and compliance metrics including JSON validity, class coverage, property reuse, and signature compliance, complemented by qualitative inspection of inconsistent signatures and SPARQL-based competency-question tests. This makes normalization itself a measured contribution, showing how fusion compacts the graph and where domain–range mismatches persist.

## Key Findings

Fusion preserves all extracted relation statements exactly — 75,870 rdf:Statement instances for Mistral and 51,657 for OpenAI — while substantially reducing duplicated entities, predicates, signatures, and annotations, especially for Mistral, where entities fall from 74,035 to 20,827, object properties from 2,643 to 500, signatures from 4,694 to 1,636, and annotations from 119,063 to 39,055. The OpenAI graph is larger after fusion at 1,311,916 RDF triples versus 1,131,066 for Mistral, but compacts less aggressively, retaining 38,339 entities and 2,031 object properties. Quantitatively, JSON validity is 100% in all settings and class coverage is 99.97% for Mistral and 99.99% for OpenAI, while property reuse rises from 82.51% to 96.42% for Mistral and from 81.52% to 85.11% for OpenAI after fusion, and signature compliance rises from 49.86% to 72.61% for Mistral and from 56.94% to 61.03% for OpenAI. The gap between property and signature scores indicates that fewer than 20% of triples introduce unseen properties, with the remaining non-compliance coming mostly from reuse of existing predicates with new or unexpected domain–range combinations. Qualitatively, the two induced vocabularies share 21 properties and 18 signatures including appliesTo, composedOf, performedAtLocation, and responsibleFor, while OpenAI leans toward fine-grained expressive predicates for purpose, sequencing, and interaction and Mistral toward conservative normative and compliance relations; inconsistent signatures number 39 for Mistral fusion and 52 for OpenAI fusion, driven mainly by entity typing errors such as legal sources typed as Artifact, modality or polarity baked into predicates, and occasional French predicates despite English-only prompts.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The wiki-reported conclusion points to iterative ontology refinement by validating and integrating newly observed domain–range signatures rather than treating the induced ontology as fixed, alongside improved entity and predicate fusion to further reduce duplication without losing meaning. It also calls for formal validation such as SHACL constraints to catch typing and range errors systematically, GraphRAG-based consultation to make the graph directly queryable for maintenance use cases, and mechanisms for updating the knowledge graph as legal provisions evolve. Together these directions aim to close the remaining signature-compliance gap and move from a preliminary evaluated prototype toward a maintainable legal knowledge infrastructure.
