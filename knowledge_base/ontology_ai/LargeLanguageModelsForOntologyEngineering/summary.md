# Large Language Models for Ontology Engineering: A Systematic Literature Review | www.semantic-web-journal.net

**Article:** [Large Language Models for Ontology Engineering: A Systematic Literature Review](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/content/large-language-models-ontology-engineering-systematic-literature-review) — Semantic Web Journal (IOS Press), 04/30/2025

## Human Readable TL;DR

Building an ontology is a bit like writing a legal dictionary for a whole field of knowledge: every concept has to be defined precisely and every relationship has to stay logically consistent, which is why it normally takes trained domain experts a long time. This paper is a systematic survey that asks whether large language models can act as helpful assistants in that slow, expert-heavy work, looking at thirty published papers to see where models like GPT, LLaMA, and T5 fit into the process. Think of the LLMs as versatile apprentices who can play three parts at once: the engineer who drafts the structure, the domain expert who suggests what things mean, and the critic who checks the result. The big takeaway is that while these apprentices are promising, every research team currently gives them different instructions, different tests, and different grades, so it is hard to compare results or reproduce them, and the future will need shared benchmarks plus workflows where humans and AI check each other.

## TL;DR

This Semantic Web Journal survey article presents a systematic literature review of LLMs in ontology engineering, filtering a large initial retrieval set (11,985 results, 2018–2024) down to 30 papers yielding 41 distinct studies, and mapping them across core development activities, input-output characteristics, evaluation methods, and application domains. It finds LLMs applied across requirements specification, implementation, publication, and maintenance, taking on roles as ontology engineers, domain experts, and evaluators, typically using GPT, LLaMA, and T5 families over heterogeneous inputs such as OWL ontologies, text, and competency questions to produce task-specific outputs such as examples, axioms, and documentation. The central diagnosis is fragmentation: task definitions, datasets, metrics, and experimental workflows are not homogenized, and incomplete evaluation protocols and unreleased code undermine reproducibility and transparency. The prescribed direction is standardized benchmarks and hybrid LLM-human workflows, with supporting data files published via GitHub and Zenodo. At the time of the wiki snapshot the article (Tracking #3864-5078) stands at Major Revision, with two solicited reviews calling it comprehensive but asking for structural tightening, clearer novelty beyond prior surveys, deeper domain and human-role analysis, and a unifying taxonomy diagram.

## Problem & Motivation

Ontology engineering is a demanding knowledge-representation task in which domain experts must carefully define concepts and their precise relationships while keeping the resulting ontology logically consistent, and that dependence on scarce expert labor makes every stage from requirements through maintenance slow and expensive. Recent advances in large language models have opened the possibility of automating or augmenting those stages, but the literature is dispersed across many tasks, models, inputs, and evaluation styles, leaving it unclear where LLMs genuinely help and where they merely add noise. The motivation for this review is therefore to consolidate that scattered evidence into a single systematic account organized around four research objectives and matching research questions, covering what development activities LLMs support, what goes in and out of them, how they are evaluated, and in which domains they are tried. Without such a synthesis, the field risks repeating the same pilot experiments with incompatible setups and learning little that transfers between projects.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Four-activity mapping of LLM use in ontology engineering** — the review structures the literature around the core development lifecycle of ontology requirements specification, implementation, publication, and maintenance, so that each paper can be located by which stage it targets rather than treated as an isolated demo.
2. **Triple-role characterization of LLMs** — instead of viewing models only as text generators, the survey casts them as ontology engineers who draft structures, domain experts who supply or interpret meaning, and evaluators who judge outputs, a framing that clarifies what capability each study is actually testing.
3. **Input-output and evaluation systematization** — the paper catalogs heterogeneous inputs (OWL ontologies, free text, competency questions, and similar artifacts) against task-specific outputs (examples, axioms, documentation, and the like), alongside the datasets, metrics, and workflows used, exposing exactly where homogenization is missing.
4. **30-papers-to-41-studies extraction with open resources** — rather than merely listing the 30 included publications, the authors extract 41 distinct studies and publish relatively rich accompanying data files via GitHub (oeg-upm/llm4oe-slr) and Zenodo, making the screening and coding decisions inspectable even though reviewers note a clarifying README is still missing.

## Key Findings

The review finds LLM work spread across all four lifecycle stages, with the GPT, LLaMA, and T5 model families dominant and with inputs and outputs varying widely by task, confirming that there is no shared convention for how an ontology-engineering task should be posed to a model or what a good answer looks like. Evaluation practice is equally fragmented: different studies choose different datasets, metrics, and experimental workflows, and several papers omit complete evaluation protocols or code, which makes results hard to reproduce and methods insufficiently transparent. Human involvement, a natural corrective for expert-dependent work, appears in only a small subset of studies (the second review notes just four studies with human participants), so claims about practical usability rest on thin evidence. The peer reviews corroborate both the value and the limits of the exercise: the first reviewer judges the coverage comprehensive and valuable while flagging scattered structure, redundancy between Sections 4.1 and 4.2.1, a mismatch between the Section 4.2 title and its content with a missing technical "how to do" synthesis, and layout problems in Figure 2; the second reviewer adds that the 11,985-to-30 filtering may under-cover 2018–2021 BERT and T5-era work because of the chosen "Language Model" / "LM" / "LLM*" search terms, that the novelty over the earlier Garijo et al. 2024 survey needs sharper articulation, and that the domain listing and the per-section summaries that repeat the Discussion should be condensed in favor of a single comprehensive taxonomy diagram.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The abstract's own prescription is the development of standardized benchmarks together with hybrid workflows that integrate LLM automation with human expertise, so that speed gains do not come at the cost of logical consistency or domain fidelity. The reviewers' suggestions point in the same direction with concrete editorial steps: tighten the structure to remove redundancy, align section titles with their contents and add a technical synthesis of how tasks are actually performed, flatten and clarify the overview figures, and supply the missing README for the GitHub and Zenodo data deposits. They further recommend broadening or justifying the search strategy to cover earlier language-model generations, deepening the analysis of application domains and of the human role beyond the current four-participant-study base, and consolidating the repetitive section summaries into a unified taxonomic framework diagram that would make the whole landscape graspable at a glance. Taken together, these steps would move the contribution from a valuable catalog toward a reusable reference that future ontology-engineering studies can build on and compare against.
