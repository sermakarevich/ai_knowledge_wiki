# Chapter 6: The Triumph of the Left Hemisphere

This chapter closes Part One by asking what the *working relationship* between the hemispheres is actually like — not just what each does separately, but how they co-operate, compete, and inhibit one another in practice. McGilchrist draws on split-brain research, corpus callosum neurology, and studies of consciousness to argue that the relationship operates on (at least) three distinct levels — millisecond-to-millisecond, phenomenological, and long-term strategic — and that at every level above the most basic, the balance of power tilts toward the left hemisphere (LH) despite the right hemisphere (RH) having the deeper claim to reality. He identifies five specific "asymmetries" that explain why the LH wins this contest even though it shouldn't, and closes with a coda arguing that the LH, like a sleepwalker, exhibits the same denial and self-satisfaction seen pathologically in anosognosia — a warning sign for where Western culture (the subject of Part Two) is headed.

## The corpus callosum: separation, not integration

McGilchrist opens by restating his thesis: the hemispheres have complementary but conflicting tasks and "need to maintain a high degree of mutual ignorance" while still co-operating. The corpus callosum's role is counterintuitive — it is "largely inhibitory in function," and he argues this is not a design flaw but the mechanism of co-operation itself, since "co-operation requires difference, not more of the same." He uses the analogy of a pianist's two hands, or singers in a choir with "fugal elements, discords, cross-rhythms": effective joint action requires each part to *inhibit* itself from mirroring the other, not to synchronize with it. "We must inhibit one in order to inhabit the other."

**Split-brain evidence.** Split-brain patients live remarkably normal lives — one could "go on holiday with them" and notice nothing unusual — because most experience reaches both hemispheres redundantly through the body and senses, and because even in intact brains "no connective pathways, even in the corpus callosum, function all the time" (Joseph Bogen). What the surgery removes is not shared history but ongoing moment-to-moment inhibition.

In the months right after callosotomy, however, patients report **intermanual conflict**: a man "found himself in the unfortunate position of going to embrace his wife with one arm and pushing her away with the other"; a woman's left hand repeatedly grabbed the steering wheel from her right, closed doors the right had opened, snatched money the right had offered a cashier; another patient: "I open the closet door... As I reach for something with my right hand, my left comes up and takes something different. I can't put it down if it's in my left hand. I have to call my daughter." McGilchrist notes the pattern: it is *always the left hand* — i.e., the right hemisphere — that is experienced as "misbehaving." The key point is that these disturbances were not things that stopped happening, but things that "couldn't be prevented from happening" — a failure of inhibition, not of integration. Roger Sperry himself wrote that "both the left and right hemispheres may be conscious simultaneously in different, even in mutually conflicting, mental experiences that run along in parallel."

**Corpus callosum dysfunction and psychiatric illness.** Agenesis of the corpus callosum (failure to develop, affecting up to **1 per cent** of the population) or its incompetence has been linked to **schizophrenia** and schizotypy, where interhemispheric inhibition fails and left-hemisphere modes intrude into right-hemisphere functioning (and vice versa) — producing effects ranging from creative ("flying mathematicians") to disruptive ("inhibited trapeze artists"). Paradoxically, agenesis produces a picture of *increased* interconnectivity, confirming that the callosum's main job is separation, not fusion. Children, too, are "relatively speaking, split-brain subjects": callosal myelination doesn't begin until the end of the first year of life and progresses slowly, so pre-adolescent children find it hard to use their hemispheres separately — independent hemisphere function is "one of the achievements of maturity." McGilchrist's overall view: the corpus callosum acts "principally as the agent of hemisphere differentiation rather than integration, though ultimately differentiation may be in the service of integration" — a paradox he likens to a verse from the Upanishads: "He is the bridge that serves as the boundary to keep the different worlds apart."

## Three levels of the relationship

To organize the question "is the relationship one of harmony or discord?", McGilchrist introduces an extended vignette of two business partners, Franny and Fred, who share day-to-day tasks (**Level One**), have complementary but increasingly resentful roles in running the company (**Level Two**), and — unknown to Franny — one of whom is secretly planning to steal the company's data and split off on his own (**Level Three**). The vignette is explicitly not a literal model of the hemispheres, only an illustration that the answer to "how do they relate?" depends entirely on which level you're asking about.

### Level One: millisecond-to-millisecond

At the most basic level, both hemispheres' "takes" on the world are needed simultaneously — Kant's dictum applies: *"Begriffe ohne Anschauungen sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind"* ("concepts without intuitions are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind"). Yet experiments show the hemispheres' interaction here is not tidy division of labour but "rivalry between departments," and competition between them can actually impair performance. A hemisphere that expects to handle a task "assumes control of processing... and... remains in control even if its performance... is considerably worse than that which could have been produced by the opposite side of the brain" — as if each hemisphere thought, "if this letter looks addressed to me, I'm going to deal with it, even if it turns out... it was really addressed to you." This may be adaptive: sending information across the callosum costs time, so a quick, slightly inferior response can beat a slow, better one. Below this contest sits a "metacontrol" switch, possibly as far down as the brainstem, that apportions work between the hemispheres.

### Level Two: the phenomenological / conscious level

Moving to the level of conscious experience, over longer time spans, McGilchrist reports Marie Banich's finding (Director of the Institute of Cognitive Science, Boulder) that interhemispheric interaction is not mere "photocopying" between hemispheres but produces **emergent functions**: "the nature of processing when both hemispheres are involved cannot be predicted from the parts." When both hemispheres co-operate on a task, wholly different brain regions are recruited — not simply additional ones.

Individuals also show a stable trait called **"hemispheric utilisation bias"** (or "characteristic perceptual asymmetry") — a consistent personality-level leaning toward one hemisphere's style. Experiments on visual attention show this bias is asymmetric in its effects: a left-hemisphere-biased subject's tendency to prioritise the right visual field is *enhanced* by the bias, so irrelevant right-field information interferes with a left-field task; but a right-hemisphere-biased subject shows no such interference — the RH's "concern" stays even-handed across both fields regardless of bias. Consistent with this, transfer of information from left to right hemisphere is slower than right to left.

Competition is also visible after injury: temporarily disabling the *undamaged* hemisphere (e.g., via transcranial magnetic stimulation) improves function in the damaged one — a finding traced back to the neurophysiologist **Brown-Séquard**, who reversed a frog's lesion-induced paralysis by lesioning the opposite hemisphere. This suppressive competition is itself asymmetric: the LH suppresses the RH more than the reverse. Split-brain patients, freed from LH suppression of the RH, can perform certain focused-attention tasks **in half the time** of normal subjects because both hemispheres can now contribute focused attention simultaneously. Similarly, children with early left-hemisphere damage who must relocate language to the right hemisphere show IQ deficits in non-verbal function, because language interferes with the RH's native synthetic-Gestalt processing when housed in the same hemisphere — again showing LH-over-RH dominance in interaction.

McGilchrist returns to the inter-manual conflict cases to argue that it is always the LH — Gazzaniga's "interpreter" — that is identified, at the conscious level, as "I": the patient who says "I know what I want to wear" really means "my left hemisphere knows what it wants me to wear, and I am identified with my left hemisphere." Yet despite the LH's practical dominance, McGilchrist maintains the deeper asymmetry runs the other way: "The right hemisphere certainly needs the left, but the left hemisphere depends on the right." Without the RH's re-integration, "the left hemisphere comes to believe its territory actually is the world."

**On whether hemispheres have separate "wills."** Bogen's two "crucial facts": "it takes only one hemisphere to have a mind," and hemispheres "can sustain the activity of two separate spheres of consciousness following commissurotomy." Sperry: each disconnected hemisphere "appears to have a mind of its own," oblivious to the other. Yet unified consciousness persists. McGilchrist proposes inverting Sperry's model: rather than asking how two wills become one consciousness, ask how **one field of consciousness accommodates two wills** — because consciousness, following **Jaak Panksepp**, is unified at low levels (originating in the **peri-aqueductal grey matter** of the midbrain) and only becomes divisible once it rises to high-level cognitive interpretation. Panksepp: "The unity of an underlying form of consciousness in split-brain individuals, perhaps their fundamental sense of self, is affirmed by the fact that the disconnected hemispheres can no more easily execute two cognitive tasks simultaneously than can the brains of normal individuals." Panksepp figures consciousness migrating upward through the "cerebral canopy" (cingulate, temporal, frontal cortex) — an image McGilchrist prefers to the common metaphor of consciousness as a bird alighting on the brain: it is instead "a tree, its roots deep inside us."

**Self-consciousness as the left hemisphere inspecting the right.** Drawing on Thomas Nagel's "what it is like to be" formulation and Patricia Churchland's reductive account of blueness, McGilchrist argues the objective/subjective divide is itself "a creation of the left hemisphere's analytic disposition." He distinguishes plain consciousness from *self*-consciousness — being aware of oneself as an object of attention, versus simply being aware. Most RH functions must, by nature, stay outside the focus of attention to work (skills, intuitions, social judgment); self-consciousness arises specifically when the LH turns its attentional "spotlight" on the RH's activity. Evidence: casualties of excessive self-consciousness (social phobia, with its sense of an observing "eye" watching one's "I") are all RH-based skills; schizophrenic patients, whose psychosis involves reflexive hyperconsciousness and who often paint a detached observing eye, show relative RH hypofunction. He connects this to Hume (introspection finds no self, only a string of impressions), Fichte (the self only appears through resistance — "not as an object but as that which is obtruded upon by some kind of recalcitrant reality"), Heidegger's *vorhanden*, and Merleau-Ponty — all converging on the idea that the self-conscious self emerges precisely when LH attention is trained on the RH's world.

**The "binding problem."** McGilchrist frames the classic puzzle of how modular brain functions unite into a single self as itself a left-hemisphere artefact — a problem generated by modeling the mind as separable parts in the first place ("the Frankenstein's monster problem"). He cites **V.S. Ramachandran**'s experiments, which "flatly contradict the theory that the brain consists of a number of autonomous modules acting as a bucket brigade": connections are "extraordinarily labile and dynamic," with perceptions emerging from reverberating signals across levels and senses. He illustrates whole, pre-integrated experience with the case of a Parkinson's patient undergoing **deep brain stimulation** of the subthalamic nucleus by **Professor Yves Agid**'s team at the Pitié-Salpêtrière: minute repositioning of the electrode flipped her, within seconds, from an impassive "switched-off" state to severe depression ("I'm falling down in my head, I no longer wish to live...") and then, moments after stimulation stopped, to hypomania ("over the moon," playfully pulling the examiner's tie). The point: the fused cognitive-emotional-motor experience was already coherent at this very low, subcortical level — not assembled afterward at the cortical top.

### Level Three: long-term strategic asymmetries

Recapping: more than one will does not require more than one consciousness. McGilchrist now names **five asymmetries** that determine where power actually lies between the hemispheres — the first two favoring the RH as it *should* be, the latter three explaining why power is in practice captured by the LH anyway.

**1. Ontological asymmetry** (favours RH): the RH is "the primary mediator of experience," from which the LH's re-presented world derives and on which it depends. Quoting Blake — "Reason is the bound or outward circumference of Energy... [Energy] is the only Life, and comes from the Body" — and Goethe's Faust inverting the Gospel of John: not "In the beginning was the word [logos]" but *"Im Anfang war die Tat!"* ("In the beginning was the deed").

**2. Asymmetry of function** (favours RH): LH products *must* be returned to the RH to live; the reverse direction (RH products sent to LH for "unpacking") enriches experience but is not necessary. "One process is literally vital: the other is not."

These two indicate where the balance of power *ought* to lie — with the right hemisphere. "But it does not," because of three further asymmetries favoring the left:

**3. Asymmetry of means** (favours LH): the LH dominates because it is most accessible to the self-aware, self-inspecting intellect, and because it monopolizes the tools of argument — "the three Ls, language, logic and linearity." Its case is always "easily defensible, because analytic," and "most easily expressible... it has a voice," while the RH "hasn't got a voice anyway." The laws of non-contradiction and the excluded middle bind the LH but not the RH, which treats paradox and ambiguity as native to reality. The LH also builds cumulative systems over time — "brick on brick" — giving it lasting external "permanence and solidity" (foreshadowing Chapter 12), whereas the RH works by sudden holistic "aha!" recognition, "never finished, never certain." This asymmetry also determines what can be *taught*: LH knowledge (Wissenschaft, not Erkenntnis) can be transmitted because it's built from replicable pieces, while RH knowledge requires the listener already to have some prior understanding to be "awakened." McGilchrist calls the LH "the Berlusconi of the brain, a political heavyweight who has control of the media."

**4. Asymmetry of structure** (favours LH): systems built on language and reason are inherently closed — "language can only refer to itself, and reason can only elaborate... the premises it starts with." There is no way out of the "hall of mirrors" except through poetry (for language) or Gödel's logic (for reason); the leap of faith that grounds reason must come "from behind and beyond, from intuition or experience." McGilchrist frames this as evidence the LH's foreclosure is not passive but "willed," previewing Part II's claim that the LH mounts "intemperate attacks on nature, art, religion and the body" — the Master's emissary "become a tyrant." He develops a long riff on creation-as-negation (via Scheler, Heidegger, Spinoza's *omnis determinatio est negatio*, Hegel, and Popperian falsification): we do not *make* things live, we only "permit, or not permit, life" — like a river that comes into being only through water's encounter with a resisting landscape, or "a cat pushing a dead mouse about the floor in order to see it move."

**5. Asymmetry of interaction** (favours LH): the overall hemisphere relationship has shifted from **stable dynamic equilibrium** to **inequilibrium** — negative feedback (self-correcting, like a pendulum) has given way to positive feedback favoring the LH, "as if a violent swing of the pendulum shifted the whole clock, which then over-balanced." McGilchrist states plainly: "Despite the primacy of the right hemisphere, it is the left hemisphere that has all the cards and, from this standpoint, looks set to win the game. That is the subject of Part II." He maps this onto Heidegger's account of the Apollonian (rationalistic) overwhelming the Dionysian (intuitive) — reversing the ancient Greek balance — producing a "frenzy of 'forming projects, enclosures, frameworks, division and structuring'" that turns nature into "one gigantic filling station." Yet he finds grounds for hope in Hölderlin's line, quoted approvingly by Heidegger: *"Wo aber Gefahr ist, wächst / Das Rettende auch"* ("Where there is danger, that which will save us also grows") — the LH's analytic "danger" can, if its results are returned to the RH, produce a richer "presencing" (as in the best art criticism or medical practice), so long as the LH does not refuse to hand its work back.

## Coda: Sleepwalking into the abyss

McGilchrist closes with Goethe's lifelong preoccupation with the **Faust** legend and the related poem **Der Zauberlehrling** (*The Sorcerer's Apprentice* — familiar via Disney's *Fantasia*). In both stories, a apprentice/pact-maker oversteps and causes destruction, but crucially, *there is a saving awareness that things have gone wrong* — Faust is redeemed through remorse and refocusing on serving humanity; the returning "old master" (*der alte Meister*) gently corrects, rather than punishes, his apprentice. McGilchrist's point: in the story he is telling about the LH's dominance, that saving self-awareness is precisely what's missing — the LH resembles "a sorcerer's apprentice that is blithely unaware that he is about to drown, a Faust that has no insight into his errors."

He returns to the neurological evidence of denial from Chapter 5: the LH would rather believe authority ("what it says on this piece of paper") than its own senses, and famously denies paralysis it cannot explain. Ramachandran: "In the most extreme cases, a patient will not only deny that the arm... is paralysed, but assert that the arm lying in the bed next to him, his own paralysed arm, doesn't belong to him!" By contrast, patients with *left*-hemisphere damage (relying on the right hemisphere) "almost never experience denial" despite equal disability — "it is the vehemence of the denial... that cries out for an explanation." Nietzsche is quoted: "'I have done that', says my memory. 'I cannot have done that'—says my pride, and remains adamant. At last—memory yields." The LH will accept responsibility only when it can offload blame elsewhere — demonstrated by Ramachandran's salt-water injection experiment, where a patient in denial of her paralysis accepted it only once she had an external "cause" (the fake injection) to blame. Ramachandran: "The left hemisphere is a conformist, largely indifferent to discrepancies, whereas the right hemisphere is the opposite: highly sensitive to perturbation."

**Rebutting the "right hemisphere as zombie" intuition.** McGilchrist argues against the popular assumption (which he says even some neuroscientists share) that the *right* hemisphere is the more zombie-like, dissociated one. He marshals evidence that dissociation — amnesia for autobiographical information, identity disturbance, depersonalisation, derealisation, reports of "going to another space or place," even feeling reduced to "just a casing" with the "I" relocated outside the body — is a signature of *right*-hemisphere hypofunction, confirmed empirically: dissociation-prone subjects show faster LH-driven inhibition of the RH, versus balanced inhibition in non-prone subjects. Even **hypnosis** — popularly assumed to release the right hemisphere — shows the opposite pattern: imaging studies confirm predominant *left*-sided activation during hypnosis, decreased activity in RH-linked agency regions (precuneus, posterior cingulate, right inferior parietal lobule), and hypnosis's hallmark intensely focused, narrowed attention ("analogous to macular vision: intense and detailed, but restricted") is itself the left hemisphere's characteristic mode. More hypnotisable subjects also show higher dopaminergic activity, consistent with the LH's greater dopamine transmission.

McGilchrist closes: if the story of the Western world is one of increasing left-hemisphere domination, "we would not expect insight to be the key note. Instead we would expect a sort of insouciant optimism, the sleepwalker whistling a happy tune as he ambles towards the abyss" — the pivot into Part Two, on the divided brain's influence on Western culture.
