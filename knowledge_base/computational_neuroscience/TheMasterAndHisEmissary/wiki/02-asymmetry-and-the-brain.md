# Chapter 1: Asymmetry and the Brain

This opening chapter of Part One lays the anatomical and evolutionary groundwork for the book's thesis. McGilchrist asks why the brain is divided into two hemispheres at all, examines the corpus callosum and finds its function is largely *inhibitory* rather than integrative, surveys the structural asymmetries of the human brain (frontal lobe expansion, Yakovlevian torque, planum temporale), and then turns to comparative neuroscience across birds, amphibians and mammals to argue that hemispheric asymmetry long predates language and instead reflects two irreducibly different, competing *types of attention* an organism must pay to the world simultaneously. The chapter closes with a philosophical meditation on the nature of attention itself — that it is not a neutral cognitive function but a "howness," a relationship that brings the world into being and is inescapably value-laden — setting up the right-hemisphere/left-hemisphere distinction (broad, contextual, living attention vs. narrow, focused, mechanistic attention) that structures the rest of the book.

## Historical background

- Speculation on hemispheric difference goes back over two millennia: third-century-BC Greek physicians held the right hemisphere was specialised for perception, the left for understanding.
- Physician Arthur Wigan published *The Duality of the Mind* (1844), prompted by post-mortem cases where a person who seemed unremarkable in life was found to have had one cerebral hemisphere destroyed by disease. Over 20 years of collecting such cases, Wigan concluded each hemisphere alone could support consciousness, so we "must have two minds with two brains," with mental disease resulting when they conflict. He did not, however, propose *how* the hemispheres differ, treating them as roughly interchangeable — a "belt and braces" evolutionary redundancy.

## Why two hemispheres?

- There is no obvious necessity for an organ whose function is to make connections to be so thoroughly divided; a unified brain seems like it should offer advantages, yet evolution moved the opposite way.
- Embryologically, the two hemispheres arise from a single midline structure, the **prosencephalon**, at about five weeks' gestation — so the division is not simply a leftover embryonic accident, especially since midline connections do develop later in fetal life even as the hemispheres remain divided. The skull, by contrast, starts in several pieces and fuses into a whole — the brain does the opposite, "positively enhanc[ing] the anatomical separation."
- The **corpus callosum**, the main band of neural tissue connecting the hemispheres, was long thought to be merely a structural "bolster" preventing the hemispheres from sagging.
- It contains an estimated **300–800 million fibres** connecting topologically similar areas of the two hemispheres, yet only **2 per cent of cortical neurones** are connected by this tract.
- Crucially, a large number of these connections are **inhibitory**, not merely transmissive. Most callosal-projecting cells use the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate, but significant populations use the inhibitory neurotransmitter **GABA** (gamma-amino butyric acid), and even excitatory fibres often terminate on inhibitory interneurones. Stimulation of one hemisphere typically produces a brief excitatory response in the other, followed by prolonged inhibitory arousal, visible on imaging. Some neuroscientists have proposed the corpus callosum's whole point is to let one hemisphere inhibit the other.
- The corpus callosum does retain excitatory/information-transfer functions too, and both roles are necessary for normal function. But its inhibitory character helps explain why **"split-brain" patients** (corpus callosum severed for epilepsy treatment) function surprisingly normally in everyday life.
- As brains get larger across evolution, interhemispheric connections **decrease relative to brain size**, not increase — "the bigger the brain, the less interconnected it is." Greater brain asymmetry correlates with a smaller corpus callosum, suggesting brain size, hemispheric asymmetry, and reduced interhemispheric connectivity evolved together. The modern human brain's two hemispheres have been characterised as **two autonomous systems**.
- McGilchrist endorses Kinsbourne's view that duality follows the principle of "opponent processors" and so refines control, but argues the real story goes further: "the brain is not just a tool for grappling with the world. It's what brings the world about."

### A brief digression on mind and brain

- McGilchrist explicitly steps aside from the mind–brain problem, noting the book's argument doesn't depend on resolving it, but states his own view briefly.
- One could call mind "the brain's experience of itself," though this is already problematic since the brain helps constitute the world in which experience occurs.
- Explanation always works by analogy to something already understood, but consciousness is unique — it is "the ground of all experience" with an "inwardness" nothing else has, and the analytic process, faced with uniqueness, tends to slide from "unique" to "non-existent."
- The one certainty: "everything we know of the brain is a product of consciousness" — more certain than the reverse claim that consciousness is a product of the brain.
- Descartes was right that consciousness is undeniable, but wrong to split mind and body into two separate substances (two "whats"). McGilchrist attributes this error to a characteristically **left-hemisphere** concern with the "whatness" of things, where the real distinction is one of **"howness"** — two modes of being, not two things. Materialist monism, which reduces mind to matter, commits the same left-hemisphere error in reverse — collapsing two whatnesses into one rather than recognizing "more than one howness."
- Quotes Descartes's *Meditation VI*:
  > "… I am not merely present in my body as a sailor is present in a ship, but … am very closely joined and, as it were, intermingled with it, so that I form with it a single entity."
- McGilchrist's gloss: a thing/quantity/whatness (per the left hemisphere) can be reduced to its constituents, but "one way of being, a quality, a howness, cannot be reduced to another."

## The frontal expansion

- The human **frontal lobes** — the most recently evolved brain region — represent about **35 per cent** of total human brain volume, versus roughly **7 per cent** in a dog and **17 per cent** in lesser apes; the proportion is similar to great apes, but humans differ in the amount of **white matter** in the frontal lobes.
- White matter's white appearance comes from **myelin**, a phospholipid sheath around axons that speeds transmission; more white matter implies more profuse interconnection. Incidentally, there is also more white matter in the human **right** hemisphere than the left.
- The frontal lobes give humans the capacity to "stand back from the world" — enabling planning, flexible thought, and control rather than mere passive response.
- McGilchrist frames this as a tension between a **horizontal axis** (immersion in the immediate, felt, lived world) and a **vertical axis** (rising above to see "the territory," like a general's strategy map). Living purely at ground level is animal-like; floating entirely above is "not to live at all — just to be a detached observing eye."
- There is an **optimal "necessary distance"** between self and world, analogous to the distance between an eye and a page — too close or too far and legibility fails.
- This necessary distance is not the same as detachment: distance can produce cold calculation and exploitation (Machiavelli), but the same capacity for distance also enables empathy and trust, seeing others "as beings like ourselves" (Erasmus). The frontal lobes "not only teach us to betray, but to trust."
- This creates a structural problem: staying in touch with the immediacy of experience (to empathize and bond) pulls toward the horizontal axis, while control and manipulation require the vertical, map-making axis. McGilchrist notes this doesn't itself explain hemispheric division, since animal and bird brains are also divided, but it may hint at how an already-divided brain becomes useful to humans specifically.

## Structural asymmetry

- The most familiar structural fact is that the brain is asymmetrically larger on the left, though the effect is subtler than commonly assumed.
- Language-related asymmetries were identified through 19th-century clinical work: **Marc Dax** anticipated (by about 25 years) the observations popularly credited to **Paul Broca** linking speech loss to left frontal damage; **Carl Wernicke** later located language *comprehension* (as distinct from speech production) further back in the left hemisphere, in the **posterior superior temporal gyrus** (now Wernicke's area). This association with language led to the left hemisphere being called "dominant."
- Austrian anatomists **Richard Heschl** and **Oscar Eberstaller** independently found visible asymmetries in this region; Heschl's gyri are the transverse gyri in the left superior temporal lobe processing incoming auditory information.
- In the 1930s, **Richard Pfeifer** found the **planum temporale** (just posterior to Heschl's gyrus, within the Sylvian fissure) larger on the left; **Geschwind and Levitsky** confirmed this in the 1960s, reporting that in **65 per cent of cases** the planum temporale is on average **30 per cent larger** on the left than the right.
- Skull and brain-scan analysis also revealed a generalised enlargement of the posterior left hemisphere around the parietal lobe, called the **left petalia** (petalia originally referred to the impression left on the skull's inner surface by a hemisphere's protrusion, now used for the protrusion itself).
- But the right hemisphere has its own area of expansion: the brain is "twisted" about its central axis — wider on the left toward the back and on the right toward the front, extending further back on the left and further forward on the right — a consistent effect called **Yakovlevian torque**, "as though someone had got hold of the brain from below and given it a fairly sharp tweak clockwise."
- Structure is generally taken as an expression of function (a principle traced to anatomist **John Hunter** and reinforced by **D'Arcy Thompson**). Examples given: the right posterior hippocampus (which stores 3-D spatial maps) is enlarged in **London taxi drivers** with extensive navigational experience; the left hemisphere of **songbirds** expands during mating season and shrinks afterward — both suggesting brain regions can grow with use.
- The conventional explanation for the left-hemisphere language-area expansion is that language is complex, needs to be localised in one place, and happened to land in the left hemisphere. McGilchrist states flatly that he believes **every part of this proposition is wrong** (to be argued in Chapter 3), and notes it in any case fails to explain the right frontal lobe's expansion.

## The asymmetry of function

- Functional hemispheric differences extend far down the phylogenetic tree, well before anything like language or handedness — consistent with the bihemispheric brain being an old, evidently adaptive structure. Lateralisation of function is widespread in vertebrates, and even some neurotransmitter/neurohormonal receptor asymmetries found in humans are already present in **rats**.
- McGilchrist proposes the underlying adaptive pressure is the need to manage **two incompatible types of attention simultaneously**: narrow, focused attention (e.g., a bird isolating a grain of corn from surrounding grit) versus broad, vigilant attention (watching for predators) — "like a particularly bad case of trying to rub your tummy and pat your head at the same time — only worse, because it's an impossibility."
- This maps onto a broader contextual difference: the world of "me" (individual needs, competition, using/manipulating the world) versus the world of the self as part of something larger (social group, allies, mates, the flock/pack/family) requiring open, receptive, widely diffused attention with "allegiances outside of the self."
- Extensive cross-species evidence for a consistent lateralisation pattern (right eye/left hemisphere for narrow getting-and-feeding; left eye/right hemisphere for vigilance and social/global awareness):
  - Birds use the **right eye (left hemisphere)** for feeding and the **left eye (right hemisphere)** for environmental vigilance; chicks prioritise local information with the right eye and global information with the left eye, and properly lateralised chicks use both attention types more effectively than experimentally de-lateralised chicks.
  - Many bird species show more alarm behaviour, better predator detection, and preference for examining predators with the **left eye**; birds that spot a predator with the right eye will turn to re-examine it with the left. Hand-raised **ravens** follow a human's upward gaze using their left eye.
  - Population-level bias toward left-eye predator vigilance appears across many species; **marmosets** with more strongly lateralised brains forage and watch for predators better; cats with lateralised paw preference have shorter reaction times; **lateralised chimpanzees** are more efficient at termite-fishing than unlateralised ones; less-lateralised human brains show global deficits. One researcher's summary: **"asymmetry pays."**
  - In predatory birds and animals, the left hemisphere (via right eye/right foot) latches onto prey; in **toads**, a novel/unfamiliar prey activates the right hemisphere until it becomes familiar, whereupon the left hemisphere takes over — toads generally attend to prey with the left hemisphere but interact with fellow toads via the right.
  - Lateralisation also aids **social cohesion** at the population level; the right hemisphere is implicated in social functioning broadly, including in lower animals and birds. Chicks use the left eye (right hemisphere) to distinguish familiar from unfamiliar conspecifics and to approach parents/imprinted objects, as do **Australian magpies**; male **black-winged stilts** peck at prey more successfully with the right eye but direct courtship displays toward females seen with the left eye; the right hemisphere is the main locus of early social experience in **rats**; intense emotional responses in most animal species are tied to the right hemisphere and inhibited by the left.
  - The New Zealand **wry-billed plover** has a beak curved to the right, suited to its manipulative left hemisphere.
  - Instrumental vocalisations (analogous to speech) arise in the left hemisphere across frogs, passerine birds, mice, rats, gerbils, and marmosets; **crows** show a strong right-eye (left-hemisphere) bias for tool manufacture even when this makes the task harder.
  - The right hemisphere, in animals as in humans, is of crucial importance in mediating **new** experience and information.
  - The right hemisphere in birds (as in humans) handles detailed discrimination and topography; the left hemisphere specialises in categorisation and fine motor control. **Pigeons** can categorise pictures of everyday scenes, and each hemisphere uses its own strategy: the left hemisphere applies a "local" strategy (grouping by invariant features), the right a "global" strategy (comparing the whole to an ideal exemplar).
- Synthesis: the left hemisphere yields narrow, focused attention for getting and feeding; the right yields broad, vigilant attention for awareness of surroundings, other creatures, and social bonding. In humans this becomes: the right hemisphere sees things whole and in context, while the left sees things abstracted from context, broken into parts, and reconstructed into "a 'whole': something very different." Capacities for bonding — empathy, emotional understanding — are largely right-hemisphere functions.

## The nature of attention

- Attention is not just one cognitive function among others; ontologically it is prior to functions and even to things — "the kind of attention we bring to bear on the world changes the nature of the world we attend to." Attending to the same person as friend, employer, patient, criminal suspect, lover, aunt, or cadaver changes what they (and you) become, even though "nothing objectively has changed."
- The same holds for any object: "A mountain that is a landmark to a navigator, a source of wealth to the prospector, a many-textured form to a painter, or to another the dwelling place of the gods, is changed by the attention given to it. There is no 'real' mountain..."
- Science's claim to value-free, objective description is itself a value-laden stance — a "view from nowhere" (borrowing **Nagel's** phrase) that privileges detachment; useful for some purposes, but not thereby truer or closer to the nature of things.
- Attention also changes the attender: citing mirror neurones and association-priming research, McGilchrist notes that attending to (or even just thinking about) someone performing an action makes us "objectively, measurably, more like them." Attention is thus inescapably bound up with value — a "howness," a relationship, "not a brute fact."

## Understanding the brain

- This creates a reflexive problem specific to studying the brain: any attempt to understand it changes what is found, and there is no neutral, context-free vantage point — even claimed "detachment" or "no context" is itself a special, value-laden kind of attention/context.
- We always understand via some model or exemplar; where none is explicitly named, "we have tacitly adopted the model of the machine."
- This does not mean all versions of reality are equally valid (McGilchrist defers full treatment of this to later chapters), but it does mean our understanding of the brain and our understanding of the brain's own ways of understanding are **reciprocal**, not one-directional.
- For physicians, this is not abstract: patients with neurological lesions or psychiatric conditions have not suffered mere "data loss" but have had the world itself genuinely change for them — which is why simply arguing them into "an alternative reality" rarely helps.

## Conclusion

- The chapter's questions remain largely open, deliberately: why are the hemispheres separate at all? The separation appears "not accidental, but positively conserved," with the corpus callosum carefully regulating the degree of separation — suggesting the mind and the world of experience it creates has an analogous need to keep things apart.
- In animals and birds, division serves the need to attend to the world in two incompatible ways at once; the frontal lobes' evolution in humans adds the further need for "necessary distance" to gain foresight and empathy while still returning to lived engagement.
- Structural asymmetry (planum temporale, petalia, Yakovlevian torque) likely reflects functional asymmetry, but McGilchrist rejects the standard explanation that language alone drove left-hemisphere expansion — he defers his counter-argument to Chapter 3, noting it in any case leaves right-frontal expansion unexplained.
- Closing philosophical move: experience is "forever in motion, ramifying and unpredictable," evoking **Heraclitus**'s river that one can never step into twice as "a brilliant evocation of the core reality of the right hemisphere's world." To know anything, we must fix what is inherently in flux — hence the need for two fundamentally different ways of attending, producing two different "worlds": (1) the lived, embodied, ever-changing, interconnected world of experience, and (2) a "re-presented" version — static, separable, fragmented, classified, and predictable, which "renders things inert, mechanical, lifeless" but grants the power to know, learn, and make things.
- McGilchrist stresses these two aspects are **not symmetrically opposed**, and not equivalent to the traditional subjective/objective distinction (itself already a product of one particular way of being in the world). Rather, one mode precedes any subject/object split — a world of pre-reflective "betweenness" — while the other divides into separate poles. "These are not different ways of thinking about the world: they are different ways of being in the world. And their difference is not symmetrical, but fundamentally asymmetrical."
