# Chapter 10: The Enlightenment

The Enlightenment, "the Age of Reason," is McGilchrist's most self-contradictory case study: at its best a rare moment of right-hemisphere/left-hemisphere co-operation — balance, irony, tolerance of frailty — and at its worst the point where Western culture built into its foundations a set of precepts (certainty, transmissibility, symmetry, abstraction, categorisation, explicitness) that would harden over time into left-hemisphere domination alone. The chapter opens by distinguishing reason (nous/Vernunft, right-hemisphere) from rationality (logos/Verstand, left-hemisphere), then reads Descartes's philosophy as a case study in left-hemisphere takeover with uncanny parallels to schizophrenia, traces the era's devitalisation, its need for certainty, its "deceptive clarity" of vision, its cult of symmetry and stasis, its pursuit of equality through the French and American Revolutions, and closes with the rise of "the uncanny" as the dark, repressed underside of an Enlightenment that had exiled the right hemisphere's world.

## Reason versus rationality

- McGilchrist opens with philosopher Max Black's observation: "Rational and rationality, reason and Reason, remain hotly contested notions, whose users disagree even about the nature of their disagreement."
- He anchors the chapter in one long-standing linguistic distinction, present since antiquity: Greek *nous* (or *noos*), Latin *intellectus*, German *Vernunft*, English **reason** (allied to common sense, *sensus communis*, in Vico's rather than Kant's sense) — congenial to the right hemisphere; versus Greek *logos*/*dianoia*, Latin *ratio*, German *Verstand*, English **rationality** — congenial to the left hemisphere.
- Reason: flexible, resists fixed formulation, shaped by experience, involves the whole living being. Rationality: rigid, rarefied, mechanical, governed by explicit laws.
- Reason (nous) was traditionally the higher faculty because rationality (logos) cannot ground itself by its own principles of proof and argumentation — deriving premises only postpones the question into infinite regress, ending in "an act of intuitive faith governed by reason." Plato held nous is characterised by intuition; Aristotle held nous grasps first principles through induction. So rationality is founded on reason, not vice versa.
- **Kant reconsidered**: Kant is commonly read as reversing this priority, since for him *Verstand* (rationality) is constitutive/primary and *Vernunft* (reason) merely regulatory, operating after *Verstand* has done its work. McGilchrist argues this is better read as an *extension*, not a reversal: not simply A (reason) → B (rationality), but A → B → A again — rationality's products must be returned to reason for judgment (Kant's regulatory role). He maps this directly onto his model of hemispheric co-operation: the right hemisphere delivers something to the left, which unfolds it and gives it back to the right in enhanced form.
- Reason depends on context (right-hemisphere); rationality is context-independent, abstracted, categorising, and in principle transmissible/teachable between minds because it is rule-based — reason cannot be taught in this narrow sense, only grown from lived experience. Reason holds "sometimes incompatible elements in balance" (prized by Renaissance humanists); rationality imposes an "either/or" on life.
- The Enlightenment's best achievements represent right/left co-operation (left delivered back to right); its foundational precepts point toward the left hemisphere alone.
- **Metaphor as a diagnostic**: the Enlightenment view holds metaphor is either reducible to literal statement or mere ornament irrelevant to truth — a linguistic device, not a vehicle of thought. Citing Lakoff and Johnson, "metaphor is centrally a matter of thought, not just words," and "the loss of metaphor is a loss of cognitive content."

## Descartes and madness

- **Descartes** is presented as the most influential Enlightenment philosopher and "one of the first and greatest exemplars of the left hemisphere's salience in the philosophy of the Enlightenment."
- Descartes distrusted body, senses, and imagination as routes to error and even madness; in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* he cites "madmen" who imagine "that their heads are made of earthenware, or that they are pumpkins, or made of glass."
- **The irony**: these are classic schizophrenic delusions — yet schizophrenia is not characterised by *trusting* the senses but by an unreasonable *mistrust* of them, leading to a dehumanised, devitalised view of self and others (sufferers reporting themselves as, e.g., copying machines, or wondering if their wrists contain engine oil).
- Drawing on Louis Sass (*Madness and Modernism*, *The Paradoxes of Delusion*) and Giovanni Stanghellini (*Disembodied Spirits and Deanimated Bodies*), McGilchrist argues schizophrenia is not primitive/emotive regression but "an excessively detached, hyper-rational, reflexively self-aware, disembodied and alienated condition" — Sass compared Wittgenstein's philosophical method of detached scrutiny to Daniel Paul Schreber's psychotic memoirs (Schreber being the subject of Freud's only schizophrenia case study) and found extensive similarities.
- Philosopher David Levin on Descartes looking out his window and seeing passers-by as possibly mere machines wearing hats and coats: "What could be a greater symptom of madness than to look out of one's window and see (what might, for all one knows, be) machines, instead of real people? … Not by mere chance, not by a momentary caprice, but by the inexorable logic of the rationality to which he is committed."
- Descartes denied any intelligible connection between "that curious tugging in the stomach which I call hunger" and the desire to eat, and questioned why "that curious sensation of pain" should cause mental distress — and doubted even the certainty of his own body's existence, holding that bodies are perceived "by the intellect alone… not from their being touched or seen but from their being understood."
- Countering this, citing Lakoff and Johnson: "Reason is evolutionary… Reason is thus not an essence that separates us from other animals… Reason is not completely conscious, but mostly unconscious… not purely literal, but largely metaphorical and imaginative… not dispassionate, but emotionally engaged… not radically free" — abstract thought is built from bodily metaphor: "bodily inference forms are mapped onto abstract modes of inference by metaphor."
- On time: citing Charles Sherover, Descartes had "problems with the very idea of temporal continuity," treating each moment as "an irreducible real self-enclosed atomic point… devoid of any sustaining continuity with any other moment" — paralleling the left hemisphere's atomistic time-sense discussed earlier in the book.
- Descartes aspired to be "a spectator rather than an actor in all the comedies" the world displays — detached, all-seeing, achieving certainty and fixity at the cost of content. Levin: objectification is "a means to domination and control," but the ego cannot escape its own strategy — becoming simultaneously a mere object and "a purely inner subjectivity that is no longer recognised as enjoying any truth, any reality."
- McGilchrist lists the Cartesian/schizophrenic parallels: passive-observer stance (linked to passivity phenomena in schizophrenia — sufferers' paintings often show a detached, floating, all-observing eye); affective non-engagement; the world experienced as mere representation/"play-acting"; a combination of omnipotence and impotence from lack of "betweenness" with the shared world.
- Citing John Cutting, schizophrenia appears to reflect excessive reliance on an abnormally functioning left hemisphere. McGilchrist stresses his aim is not to discount Descartes but to illuminate the parallel: "divorce from the body, detachment from human feeling, the separation of thought from action… concern with clarity and fixity, the triumph of representation… reduction of time to a succession of atomistic moments… tendency to reduce the living to the devitalised and mechanical" — Cartesian philosophy "belongs to the world as construed by the left hemisphere."

## Devitalisation and the need for certainty

- Johann Georg Hamann, an early Enlightenment critic, foresaw that the Cartesian worldview would lead to devitalisation and bureaucratisation.
- **Boredom** as an eighteenth-century concept: Alberto Moravia describes it as "a kind of insufficiency, or inadequacy or lack of reality… an almost instantaneous loss of vitality." Patricia Spacks relates it to "the dreariness of non-engagement." Isaiah Berlin cites Vauvenargues complaining of "the appalling emptiness of life" and Madame de la Popelinière wishing to throw herself from a window because life had "no meaning and no purpose."
- McGilchrist links boredom to a passive view of experience — vitality as an externally supplied "stimulant force" (like power to a computer, mirroring the left hemisphere's passive relation to what the right hemisphere delivers) — versus the Romantic view of vitality as imagination actively fashioning experience between self and world (the right hemisphere's mode). Anton Zijderveld calls boredom "a typically modern characteristic of the experience of subjective time"; Martin Waugh notes that in boredom "time seems endless, there is no distinction between past, present and future… an endless present" — which McGilchrist likens to Plato's realm of Forms.
- **Isaiah Berlin's three propositions** (from *The Roots of Romanticism*), the foundations of the Western/Enlightenment tradition that Romanticism later challenged: (1) all genuine questions can be answered; (2) all these answers are knowable and can be taught; (3) all the answers must be compatible with one another. Before the Enlightenment, implicit cultural wisdom (poetry, drama, painting, religious ritual) had kept these tenets from dominating the culture at large; the Enlightenment's heightened self-awareness let them govern not just philosophy but "the business of life itself" — the age of *les philosophes*, in which "we all became philosophers malgré nous."
- The demand for certainty and "transmissibility" created a problem for the arts (intrinsically ambiguous, non-transmissible), downgrading imagination in favour of fancy and mistrusting metaphor (equated with lying).
- **Continuities with the Reformation** (discussed in the previous chapter): banishment of wonder, triumph of the explicit, mistrust of metaphor, alienation from the embodied/fleshly world, cerebralisation of life. The right hemisphere's bid for reason (holding opposites in balance) gave way to left-hemisphere rationality (one pole must annihilate the other) — "the impulse towards harmony was replaced with the impulse towards singleness and purity."
- Reynolds's *Discourses* (lectures to the Royal Academy, 1769–1791) criticised Bernini for portraying mixed emotions — once, and later again, considered a mark of genius.
- The Enlightenment's optimism de-emphasised death and darker emotions: from 1681 Nahum Tate's revision gave *King Lear* a happy ending for 150 years, and other Shakespeare tragedies received comic resolutions — evidence, McGilchrist suggests, of denial, paralleling the left hemisphere's literal bias toward "lighter," more "positive" emotion.

## Deceptive clarity

- Enlightenment vision became camera-like; perspective grew more detached than even in the Renaissance. An early eyeglass-maker's 1317 tomb in Santa Maria Maggiore, Rome, bears the epitaph "God forgive him his sins" — an early hint of unease about optics' alienating power (though, per Joseph Needham, optics were already well developed in ancient/medieval China, so this is likely an effect rather than a cause of Western alienation).
- Locke (1690) defined **reflection** as "that notice which the mind takes of its own operations, and the manner of them" — the word's shift to mean thought-process dates to the seventeenth century; Vico was already writing of "the barbarism of reflection" by 1725.
- Picturesque tourism treated scenery as prey to be "captured": Thomas Gray describes tourists who "capture prospects at every ten paces"; William Gilpin's essays on the picturesque describe "the pursuit of his object — the expectation of new scenes continually opening."
- The very idea of "the picturesque" (nature as if a painting) implies nature needs improving by human hand — nature became, like metaphor, "Locke's perfect cheat," a pretty deception. Illustrated with the exchange in Jane Austen's *Emma* between Mrs Elton (wanting an alfresco "gipsy party") and Mr Knightley, who insists "my idea of the simple and the natural will be to have the table spread in the dining-room."
- **Bentham's Panopticon**: the all-surveying eye's apotheosis — a prison enabling total surveillance while prisoners remain unaware of when they're watched; later made famous through Michel Foucault, prefiguring modern technological surveillance.
- **Jeremy Bentham** himself is profiled at length as a left-hemisphere type: socially awkward (per J.S. Mill, he "probably never talked to women at all, except for his cook and housemaid"), pedantic ("antejentacular circumgyrations" for his morning walk), attached to inanimate objects with pet names (his stick "Dapple," his teapot "Dick"). Mill's assessment: Bentham "had neither internal experience nor external… never felt life a sore and a weary burthen… knew so little of human feelings" yet still attempted "to give a rule to all human conduct." McGilchrist notes the description's similarity to Balzac's Fontenelle (referenced again in Chapter 11).
- Bentham despised organised religion ("The Jug," short for juggernaut) and English common law, coined the words *international*, *codify*, and *maximise*, and held — "in keeping with… the world as seen by the left hemisphere" — that "the community is a fictitious body."
- Bentham on poetry: "prose is where all the lines but the last go on to the margin – poetry is where some of them fall short of it"; and, more seriously, "the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry… Everybody can play at push-pin: poetry and music are relished only by a few… between poetry and truth there is a natural opposition."
- Art is implicit, ambiguous, and embodied — making "Enlightenment art" something of an oxymoron. **Music and architecture** survived best because their implicitness resists being made explicit: Haydn's music exemplifies the Enlightenment spirit (tension between opposites held in balance, symmetry, decorum) while containing mysterious elements beyond mere order; Mozart, in his later works, prefigures Romanticism. Domestic architecture of the period drew on Palladio and the Italian Renaissance.
- **Poetry** fared worse: Lord Chesterfield told his son to use pages of Latin poetry as toilet paper, sent "as a sacrifice to Cloacina" (goddess of sewers) — prompting Keats's remark that he "would not bathe in the same River with lord C." The Enlightenment held that a finite set of true ideas existed in the abstract, merely re-clothed by language — captured in Pope's line, "What oft was thought, but ne'er so well express'd," and "Expression is the dress of thought." This view is what Wordsworth and Coleridge's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* later attacked, contrasting mere fancy (recombination) with true imagination (creating genuinely new experience). McGilchrist quotes Max Scheler at length on poets soaring "above the prevailing network of ideas… to press forward into the whole of the external world and the soul."
- Dryden and Pope's poetry belongs to the Enlightenment's best side (generous, non-dogmatic); Samuel Johnson's own practice often escaped his stated precepts, as did Reynolds's and Johnson's genuine responses to the "uncouth genius" of Michelangelo, Shakespeare, and the Scottish Highlands — succeeding, McGilchrist suggests, only by jettisoning Enlightenment theory when faced with the sheer scale of experience.

## Symmetry and stasis

- The classical heroic couplet's symmetry (with pointed caesura) is illustrated by the line on Queen Anne: "Here thou, great ANNA! whom three realms obey / Dost sometimes counsel take – and sometimes Tea." This self-referring, punctuated form contrasts with Milton's open, "river-like" syntax (later recaptured by Wordsworth) and with Bach's ever-growing musical form versus Haydn's self-contained classical perfection. Dryden's best lines (e.g., his elegy "Farewel, too little and too lately known," and "All, all, of a piece throughout…") evade this closure with a final alexandrine that "sleepwalks beyond the frame."
- **Symmetry** as a concept: means "equal measure," associated with the Greek regular solids, and in mathematics denotes invariance under a transformation and universality (Newtonian mechanics obeys symmetry) — aligning it with stasis, universals, and simple ideal forms: the left hemisphere.
- Oddly, symmetry barely appears in the phenomenal/living world (living things, like the brain, are not truly symmetrical and constantly change); and, contrary to common claims about mate preference, humans do not actually find symmetry attractive — even when it reads as healthier, it is experienced as less attractive, and symmetry in faces borders on the uncanny (echoing Blake's "fearful symmetry" of the tiger). Per F. D. Martin, Enlightenment portraiture represents faces more symmetrically than any other Western style, which is "one of the reasons why this portraiture is… 'once seen, never remembered'" (Wilde).
- Symmetry relates to two other left-hemisphere-aligned Enlightenment values: **stasis** and **equality**.
- **Equality** follows from the left hemisphere's categorical method: individual entities, respecting context and change, are never truly equal — but once classified into categories, members become substitutable/equal (Cassirer notes Arabic has five to six thousand words for "camel," where we have one general category). Categories themselves remain hierarchically unequal, so inequality is displaced from individuals to categories.
- **Stasis**: to the left hemisphere, a known thing must be fixed — it may be moved according to will but does not itself change, "as the butterfly is skewered, unmoving, a specimen in the collector's cabinet." This fixity enables manipulation and power, which in turn generates new inequality and change — "the recalcitrance of the particular is subjected to the Procrustean bed of the category," so change and difference outlawed at the individual level "return by the back door."

## The pursuit of equality

- The French and American Revolutions are the Enlightenment's most important legacies. Per Isaiah Berlin, the French Revolution's principles — "universal reason, order, justice" — had almost nothing to do with Romanticism's concern for uniqueness and difference.
- The American Revolution's "pursuit of happiness" exemplifies the left hemisphere's belief that any good can be pursued by will and rationality — yet this reveals rationality's paradox: the most valuable things (like happiness) cannot be directly pursued, only arrived at as side effects.
- The left hemisphere, McGilchrist argues, "misunderstands the importance of implicitness": liberty, equality, and fraternity, pursued directly and explicitly (left-hemisphere fashion) rather than allowed to emerge from a right-hemisphere disposition toward the world, become purely negative — the left hemisphere "can only say 'no' or not say 'no'" to what the right hemisphere gives it (paralleling the right hemisphere's own relation to "the Other," discussed in Chapter 5). Hence equality becomes a drive to level anything that stands out; liberty becomes an abstract concept detached from lived experience — Edmund Burke, in his 1775 speech on conciliation with America: "abstract liberty, like other mere abstractions, is not to be found."
- Because the left hemisphere can only negate, it proceeds by dismantling naturally evolved communities that it sees as obstacles to an "unconstrainedly free" society. Fraternity likewise lives in real communities of kinship (linked to the evolution of the right frontal lobe), whereas the left hemisphere's version is *Gesellschaft* (Tönnies) — bureaucratic "care" substituting for actual communal bonds.
- The **American Revolution** differed: lacking "Jacobins," it pursued a laissez-faire, negative-liberty approach (few restraints), winning even Burke's support, aiming to reduce formal restriction while maximising communality — Jeffersonian democracy's local, agrarian, organic structure was, McGilchrist says, in harmony with right-hemisphere ideals, before being swept away by large-scale, rootless, mechanical capitalism.
- **De Tocqueville** foresaw that the absence of right-hemisphere values in the fabric of society would lead to bureaucratic servitude: a society keeping citizens in "perpetual childhood," developing "a network of small complicated rules" that "does not tyrannise but it compresses, enervates, extinguishes, and stupefies a people, till each nation is reduced to be nothing better than a flock of timid and industrious animals, of which government is the shepherd." John Passmore calls this "Benthamite or Fabian perfection made manifest."
- This gap between Enlightenment ideal and reality recurs wherever societies identify most strongly with Enlightenment concepts (McGilchrist cites the twentieth century's "people's democracies"), explained partly by **Elster's paradox** — that rationality contains the seeds of its own destruction — and partly by the left hemisphere's inherent inability to generate life, only to negate.
- The clearest case of this negative force: liberty, justice, and fraternity leading to the guillotine. Anything sacramental or grounded in reverence/awe (right-hemisphere terrain) becomes the left hemisphere's enemy. As at the Reformation, it is "not the abuse, but the thing abused… not corrupt priests but the sacerdotal and the sacred" that become targets.
- The French Revolution's attack on the Church continued the Reformation's secularising transfer of sacerdotal power to the state, but went further in openly attacking the sacramental nature of royalty itself.
- **Villeneuve's 1793 engraving** *Matière à réflexion pour les jongleurs couronnés* (Fig. 10.1) is read at length as an emblem of left-hemisphere triumph: citing Linda Nochlin's *The Body in Pieces*, the imagery of dismemberment was central to Revolutionary ideology through and past Robespierre's fall in 1794; Nochlin reads the regicide image as "a castration image of unprecedented power." McGilchrist highlights the right hand (the left hemisphere's tool) seizing the sacramental, the fragmentation/decapitation motif (fragmentation being a hallmark of left-hemisphere perception, per Chapter 2), and the uncanny half-aliveness of the severed head.
- The image is read as a parody of the Eucharist (transubstantiation) — the royal head's *ostensio* mirroring the sacrament's, and *hoc est enim corpus meum* echoed in the mocking "hocus pocus." Blood from the head is depicted fertilising "the ploughed fields" for "the people" — the sacramental subjugated to utilitarian function.
- Citing David Freedberg (*The Power of Images*) and Joseph Koerner: the impulse to mutilate an image betrays belief in its power — iconoclasm risks investing images with the very uncanny personhood it condemns. McGilchrist disputes Koerner's suggestion that iconoclasts attack "representation itself," arguing instead that what stood trial was **metaphorical understanding** — the sense in which a wooden saint, or a king, is neither merely material nor merely symbolic-as-representation, but holds a genuinely metaphoric, sacramental status (the king "does not have to be either a mere person… or superhuman").
- Descartes again: "It is 'a mental disorder,' wrote Descartes, 'which prizes the darkness higher than the light'" — yet, McGilchrist argues, Descartes's own world (light, comedy, detached spectatorship) harbours the madness of schizophrenia, just as the Romantics who followed carried a different "madness" — melancholia and depression.

## The uncanny

- Freud held the uncanny arises from the repression of something that "should not come into the light." McGilchrist ties this to his broader thesis: the Enlightenment's drive toward objective, scientific detachment — excluding the personal, intuitive, and non-explicit — entrenched the separation between hemispheres, making right-hemisphere promptings appear alien once excluded from left-hemisphere rational discourse (paralleling how the voices of the gods came to seem alien to the ancient Greeks, discussed earlier in the book).
- Terry Castle's *The Female Thermometer: Eighteenth-Century Culture and the Invention of the Uncanny* catalogues the era's phantasmagoria, grotesquerie, carnivalesque travesty, hallucinatory reverie, paranoia, and nightmarish fantasy. Citing Freud's 1919 essay "The 'Uncanny'" via Castle: recurring motifs include "doubles, dancing dolls and automata, waxwork figures, alter egos… 'mirror selves', spectral emanations, detached body parts… the ghastly fantasy of being buried alive, omens, precognition, déjà vu."
- McGilchrist links these motifs directly to schizophrenia — living things experienced as mechanisms, the body as an assemblage of independently moving fragments, the self losing its "intuitive ipseity" — arguing these are the left hemisphere's disengaged attempt to make sense of what comes to it, alienated, from the right hemisphere. He notes the uncanny/"delusional mood" (Kraepelin and Bleuler's terminology) may be schizophrenia's defining experience.
- Freud (quoting Schelling) held the uncanny is "what should have remained hidden, but has been brought to light." Crucially, per Freud, the uncanny requires a context of "common reality": children's animated dolls and fairy-tale marvels are not uncanny, nor is Hamlet's ghost, because these occur in an acknowledged non-realistic frame. The uncanny appears only when the storyteller "pretends to move in the world of common reality" — i.e., when right-hemisphere phenomena intrude into a left-hemisphere world of the rational, mechanistic, and controlled.
- Some uncanny tales reassure the left hemisphere by resolving the mystery rationally at the end — e.g., Ann Radcliffe's *The Mysteries of Udolpho* — paralleling contemporary phantasmagoria showmen who revealed their "magic lanterns" apparatus after the show.
- The chapter closes on **Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*** ("The Modern Prometheus") — the left hemisphere's project of assembling a living whole from dead parts — which "ends, as we know, rather less obligingly" than Enlightenment optimism would predict. McGilchrist contrasts the Enlightenment belief that "the living was thought to be the sum of its parts" (so parts could be reassembled into life) with Romanticism's contrary conviction that the living is irreducible to the mechanical, and that even the inanimate world could be seen as alive — "the reintegration of the left hemisphere's realm into that of the right," setting up the following chapter on Romanticism.
