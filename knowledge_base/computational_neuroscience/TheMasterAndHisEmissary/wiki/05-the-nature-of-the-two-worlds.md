# Chapter 4: The Nature of the Two Worlds

Having established in Chapters 1–3 that the hemispheres are anatomically distinct, functionally opposed, and hold "coherent and possibly irreconcilable sets of values," McGilchrist now asks what kind of *world* each hemisphere brings into being, and whether the two worlds are symmetrical or whether one takes precedence. He argues that attention is not a neutral scanning process but a reciprocal, world-creating relationship (imaged by Escher's hands drawing each other), that Western philosophy is by its very nature a left-hemisphere discipline, and that its most important twentieth-century figures — Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, Scheler, James, Dewey, Wittgenstein — each found himself compelled, against the grain of his own analytic tools, to describe the primacy of the right hemisphere's world: contextual, embodied, intersubjective, always in process, never finally graspable. The chapter closes by cataloguing "faux amis" — words like belief, will, familiarity and passivity — that mean something quite different depending on which hemisphere is using them, and ends by drawing the conclusion that there is, strictly, no single "brain," only the brain as constituted by the right hemisphere and the brain as constituted by the left.

## Attention as a moral, reciprocal act

- Attention is responsive to the world, but the relation runs both ways: "It is not just that what we find determines the nature of the attention we accord to it, but that the attention we pay to anything also determines what it is we find."
- The same dying man can become, for a pathologist, "a textbook of disease," or for a photojournalist "a 'shot'" (both a captured image and a round of ammunition). "Attention is a moral act: it creates, brings aspects of things into being, but in doing so makes others recede."
- We neither discover an objective reality nor invent a subjective one; instead there is "a process of responsive evocation," the world "calling forth" something in us that in turn calls forth something in the world — true of perceptual qualities (a mountain's greenness) as much as of values, "as music arises from neither the piano nor the pianist's hands... but from their coming together."
- "We are transmitters, not originators."
- This reciprocity is imaged in **M. C. Escher's *Drawing Hands*** (Fig. 4.1) — a hand drawing the hand that draws it — "a paradox for linear analysis." The same paradox applies to any attempt to use the mind to understand how the mind itself comes to know things, since one is always already inside the process one is trying to examine.

## Neuropsychology and philosophy: three questions

- It is not possible to discuss the neuropsychological basis of our awareness of the world without adopting a philosophical position, "whether or not one is conscious of doing so." Not being aware of doing so simply means defaulting to scientific materialism.
- McGilchrist warns of a "treacherous, because undetected" hijack: instead of philosophy and science mutually shaping one another, the naïve worldview of science — brain as mechanism, mind therefore also mechanism — has come by default to dictate "neurophilosophy."
- He poses three questions: (1) does what we know about the hemispheres illuminate philosophical debate? (2) does philosophy help make sense of hemisphere differences? (3) what do the answers tell us about the nature of the brain? (The book returns to answer all three at the chapter's close.)
- Philosophy's default mode is itself that of the **left hemisphere** — "verbal and analytic, requiring abstracted, decontextualised, disembodied thinking... adopting a sequential, linear approach to truth, building the edifice of knowledge from the parts, brick by brick." This is true of most philosophers from Plato to the nineteenth century (when Schopenhauer, Hegel and Nietzsche began to question the method), though not of most pre-Socratics, particularly **Heraclitus**.
- Because philosophy's tools are left-hemisphere tools, we should *not* expect the history of philosophy directly to reflect hemisphere incompatibilities. But if philosophers, using only those left-hemisphere tools, nonetheless feel compelled to try to capture the right hemisphere's reality, that would be strong evidence for the right hemisphere's world being the more fundamental one — "a bit like trying to fly using a submarine."
- Parallel argument from science: **Georg Cantor** (infinity of infinities, uncountability), **Ludwig Boltzmann** (probability and time in physics), **Kurt Gödel** (incompleteness theorems — there are always truths in a system unprovable within that system), and **Niels Bohr**/**Werner Heisenberg** (Copenhagen interpretation, Uncertainty Principle) all reached, via left-hemisphere logic, conclusions that validate a right-hemisphere vision of an irreducibly uncertain, paradoxical universe rather than the tidy Newtonian one that scientific method itself would predict.
- The left hemisphere's governing principle is **division**: "Manipulation and use require clarity and fixity, and clarity and fixity require separation and division." Just as an object is neither purely a bundle of properties nor something underlying them, the self is "an aspect of experience that perhaps has no sharp edges."

## Paradox and the left hemisphere's usurpation

McGilchrist reads the classical paradoxes as the moment the left hemisphere's "either/or" view of reality collides with the right hemisphere's view of seamless process and Gestalt wholes:

- **The sorites paradox** (Greek *soros*, heap; attributed to Eubulides of Miletus, c. 350 BC): if one grain never makes the difference between heap and non-heap, how does a heap ever come into being? Left-hemisphere premise: the whole is the sum of parts, reached by sequential addition, and there must either be a heap or not (Law of the Excluded Middle). Right-hemisphere resolution: the heap is a Gestalt that comes into being gradually as a *process*.
- **The Ship of Theseus** (Plutarch, dated to c. 300 BC via the reference to Demetrius Phalereus): as planks are replaced one by one, is it the same ship? Also called the "Growing Argument" (*auxanomenos logos*) — the general problem of identity in anything that grows or self-repairs. Novalis: "There is no doubt that our body is a moulded river."
- **Zeno's paradoxes** (Zeno of Elea, c. 450 BC): Achilles and the tortoise, the dichotomy (we can never begin to move), and the arrow (which, at any instant, is either where it is or where it is not, and so cannot move). All rest on resolving continuous motion into a series of static points, "akin to the series of still frames in a ciné film" — which is what happens pathologically in **palinopsia** and **delusional misidentification syndrome** after right-hemisphere damage ("my wife one day is not the same person as my wife the next").
- **The Epimenides (Cretan liar) paradox** (Epimenides of Knossos, c. 600 BC: "Cretans are always liars"): resolved effortlessly by right-hemisphere pragmatics, which understands the humorous, self-aware intent behind the remark without needing rules. People who instead get stuck applying rules literally "are usually somewhere along the Asperger spectrum" — a sign of right-hemisphere failure (loss of context, humour, flexibility).
- The deeper point: paradox literally means a finding contrary to received opinion — but the left hemisphere inverts this, insisting *reality* must be wrong (arrows don't move, heaps can't exist) rather than admitting its own logic is inadequate to reality. McGilchrist calls this "an interesting usurpation, a swapping of roles, with the new dispensation redefining who is Master, and who emissary."
- Medieval philosophy captured the same split as *natura naturans* (nature "naturing," an ever-evolving, unknowable process) versus *natura naturata* (nature "natured," static, complete, knowable). **Spinoza** (with **Pascal**) is named as one of the few philosophers between Plato and Hegel with a strong sense of the right hemisphere's world: "the more we understand individual things, the more we understand God."

## Solipsism, objectivism, and the "view from somewhere"

- Since Descartes's subject–object divide (*cogito ergo sum*), philosophy has struggled with solipsism: if all I know stems from my own mind, how do I ever reach something other? Wittgenstein compared the solipsist to someone trying to make a car go faster by pushing the dashboard from inside.
- The position is self-undermining: using "I" presupposes a "not-I," just as Hegel's master needs a slave in order to be a master.
- **Louis Sass**'s work on schizophrenia shows solipsistic subjectivity (fantasy of omnipotence) and alienated objectivity (fantasy of impotence) collapse into one another — both are forms of isolation rather than connection.
- **Thomas Nagel**'s "view from nowhere" (objectivism's pretension) is ultimately as empty as solipsism — it falsely equates to a "view from everywhere." What actually exists is the "view from somewhere": we can only know things from an individual point of view, never in totality, and what we know is relationships, not things-in-themselves. This does not, however, rule out "a reliably constituted shared world of experience."

## Dewey and James: context and the nature of truth

- American pragmatists **John Dewey** and **William James**, writing around the turn of the twentieth century, rejected the atomistic, rationalistic tradition in philosophy.
- Dewey: "neglect of context is the besetting fallacy of philosophical thought... the most pervasive fallacy of philosophic thinking goes back to neglect of context... the greatest single disaster which philosophic thinking can incur." And: "We are not explicitly aware of the role of context just because our every utterance is so saturated with it that it forms the significance of what we say and hear."
- Context implies process and change: "To see the organism in nature, the nervous system in the organism, the brain in the nervous system, the cortex in the brain... they will be seen to be in, not as marbles are in a box but as events are in history, in a moving, growing, never finished process." Similarly: "The qualities never were 'in' the organism; they always were qualities of interactions in which both extra-organic things and organisms partake."
- James, addressing the impossibility of a "detached" objectivity while still valuing truth:
  > "The much lauded objective evidence is never triumphantly there; it is a mere aspiration or *Grenzbegriff* [limit or ideal notion]... Our great difference from the scholastic lies in the way we face. The strength of his system lies in the principles, the origin, the *terminus a quo* of his thought; for us the strength is in the outcome, the upshot, the *terminus ad quem*. Not where it comes from but what it leads to is to decide."
- McGilchrist's gloss: the left hemisphere builds understanding from the parts, from a *terminus a quo*, "pushed from behind"; the right hemisphere derives understanding from the whole, toward a *terminus ad quem*, "pulled from in front" — and its truth, while only ever provisional, does not require abandoning "the quest or hope of truth itself."
- Dewey's 1929 Gifford Lectures, *The Quest for Certainty*, argued that philosophy since Descartes's era (the 1630s) had rested on "too passive a view of the human mind" and inappropriate demands for geometrical certainty — deploring the "spectator theory of knowledge," in which "the thing to be known is something which exists prior to and wholly apart from the act of knowing."

## Husserl and the idea of intersubjectivity

- **Edmund Husserl** (b. Moravia 1859, d. 1938), trained in mathematics, physics and astronomy, founded phenomenology and is "generally seen as one of the most influential thinkers of our age," with Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty and Scheler among those it influenced.
- He developed "reductions" (unrelated to reductionism) — thought experiments aiming to reach things as they are in themselves and transcend the subject–object divide.
- He located the European crisis of modernism in "*verirrenden Rationalismus*" and "*Blindheit für das Transzendentale*" — a mad rationalism and a blindness to the transcendental.
- His later work grounds objective reality in **intersubjectivity**, made possible by shared embodied existence, and gives empathy (not just perspective-taking but actually feeling what another feels) a constitutive role.
- He distinguished **Körper** (the body as a material object among others, alien to us) from **Leib** (the body as lived, from the inside); seeing others in action, we feel them as *leibhaft*, as if sharing their embodied consciousness.
- Understanding any one thing depends on understanding the whole (an "epistemological circularity"); his own method is linear, but the reality he describes "arises from a circular process that circles and searches its origins, more like a picture that comes into focus all at once, than a linear address to a target" — Escher's hands again.
- Developmental psychology corroborates that empathy precedes, rather than follows from, a separate sense of self: we do not build outward from an already-separate self by analogy to others; we are "intersubjective in our consciousness" from the start, and separate inwardness (ours and others') develops out of shared experience.
- The left hemisphere, oriented to utility, is "not impressed by empathy," which is why Anglo-American philosophers largely untouched by phenomenology have had to build elaborate logical contortions to explain altruism away as covertly selfish.
- The **Prisoner's Dilemma** (first posed by Flood and Dresher, 1950) is analysed at length: two suspects, interrogated separately, each do better by defecting regardless of the other's choice, yet mutual defection (two years each) is worse for both than mutual silence (six months each). Purely rational calculation traps both players in the worse outcome — "another paradox that illuminates one of the 'Gödelian' points within the left hemisphere's system."
- Most real subjects actually prefer mutual cooperation to unilateral defection — not explained by selfish calculation. Examples of non-calculating altruism: **Binti Jua**, a gorilla at Brookfield Zoo, Chicago, who saved a boy who fell into her enclosure; dogs acting altruistically toward humans. Human altruism goes beyond kin selection, reciprocal altruism, and reputation management — "it is mutuality, not reciprocity, fellow-feeling, not calculation, which is both the motive and the reward."
- Neurological evidence: cooperating with a human partner in the Prisoner's Dilemma activates pleasure circuitry (mesolimbic dopamine system, striatum, orbitofrontal cortex) and is predominantly **right-sided**; the same apparent "cooperation" with a computer is predominantly left-sided ("stuff the empathy, we're just both out to win"). The right caudate, involved in these right-sided networks, also mediates altruistic punishment of defectors.

## Merleau-Ponty: empathy and the body

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (b. 1908), publishing in French between the war years and the 1960s, was profoundly influenced by Husserl's intersubjectivity and has been hugely influential across philosophy, psychology and art criticism.
- "It is as if the other person's intentions inhabited my body and mine his."
- Central concept: the **"lived body"** (recapitulating Bergson) — the body not as something we live *inside* but as fundamental to our very being, the medium through which consciousness and world are interrelated.
- No object can be viewed in isolation; each is embedded in a nexus of relations that gives it meaning, and any single view of it is a partial disclosure or "take" — an *Abschattung* (Husserl's term, often translated "adumbration") — which does not undermine but confirms the thing's real existence, since only a theoretical ideal could claim completeness. Depth is the foundation of such experience.
- The right-hemisphere basis of the body–world relation is visible clinically in the **apraxias**: a classic paper by **Hécaen** and colleagues (nearly 50 years before McGilchrist's writing) found that impairments of the relation between subject and body, or body and surrounding space, occur with lesions of the "minor" (right) hemisphere; straightforward object-use apraxias are usually left-hemisphere, but **constructional apraxias** (loss of the sense of the whole) are commonest and most severe after right-sided lesions.
- For Merleau-Ponty, truth comes through engagement with the world, not further abstraction from it; the universal is reached *through* the particular, the infinite *through* the finite; the artist does not merely reflect what exists but "brought into being a truth" not there before.
- Because thought and language are rooted in a body we share with others, truth can be relative to the knower yet still shared. This is corroborated by **Lakoff and Johnson**:
  > "The mind is not merely embodied, but embodied in such a way that our conceptual systems draw largely upon the commonalities of our bodies and of the environments we live in... truth is mediated by embodied understanding and imagination. That does not mean truth is purely subjective... Rather, our common embodiment allows for common, stable truths."
- Related essay title: "The primacy of perception, and its philosophical consequences."

## Heidegger and the nature of Being

- **Martin Heidegger** (b. 1889, southern Germany), originally destined for the priesthood and trained on Aristotle and Duns Scotus, published his major work ***Sein und Zeit*** (*Being and Time*) in 1927.
- Using a word like "being" makes us feel we already understand it, concealing the radical astonishment true understanding would require — paralleling Cantor's insight that treating infinity as "just another number" stops us understanding it. As with mathematics, the answer is not to abandon language but to stay vigilant against its tendency to foreclose understanding.
- McGilchrist rejects the "anarcho-existentialist" reading of Heidegger (Julian Young's term) that treats all interpretation as oppressive power and licenses "anything goes"; for Heidegger, that no single truth exists about a thing does not mean all versions are equally valid — "no single truth does not mean no truth."
- Truth, for Heidegger, is **aletheia**, "unconcealing": it pre-exists our attempts to discover it, is defined negatively (against concealment), is a *process*, an act or journey rather than a static thing, has degrees, and is reached by removing things rather than adding them — contrasted with truth-as-correctness, which is static and can in principle be fully known. Even a scientifically "true" fact (Patricia Churchland's account of blue as a disposition to scatter light "preferentially at about 0.46µm") is only one *take*, like the duck–rabbit: "we can have only one 'take' on it at a time."
- Truth-as-unconcealing is necessarily also concealing: opening one horizon closes others; there is no single privileged viewpoint. The impression of seeing things "as they truly are" often substitutes something graspable and familiar for the thing itself — a **left-hemisphere re-presentation**: "The inexperienced mariner sees the ice floe; the experienced mariner sees the berg and is awe-struck." Heidegger: "*in der Unverborgenheit waltet die Verbergung*" ("in unconcealment dwells hiddenness and safekeeping"). Compare Wittgenstein: "The work of art does not aim to convey something else, just itself."
- The proper stance is **nachdenken**, "waiting on" rather than "waiting for" — a patient, respectful nurturing into disclosure (George Steiner compares it to the "bending toward" in Fra Angelico's *Annunciation*). Steiner's gloss on Heidegger's **Dasein** ("being there") and **Ent-sprechen** ("response," not "answer"):
  > "For Descartes, truth is determined and validated by certainty... The self becomes the hub of reality... As knower and user, the ego is predator. For Heidegger, on the contrary, the human person and self-consciousness are not the centre... Man is only a privileged listener and respondent to existence... We are trying 'to listen to the voice of Being.'"
- Everyday existence: the famous example of the **hammer**, which is *zuhanden* ("ready-to-hand") — an unnoticed extension of the acting self — until something goes wrong, when it becomes *vorhanden* ("present-at-hand"), an object of focal (left-hemisphere) inspection. Aspects of things continually move between these modes rather than being permanently filed in one hemisphere or the other.
- **Gestell** ("framing") describes the detachment of conceptualising things out of their living context — "a bit like ripping the heart out of a living body" — as if viewed through a window, a picture, or a screen, severing the totality of Being at the frame's edge.
- Heidegger's prose deliberately favours circuitous paths (*Holzwege*, *Feldweg*, *Wegmarken*) over linear argument, reflecting **Eindeutigkeit** ("one-meaningness")'s inadequacy; Steiner called him "an indefatigable walker in unlit places": *solvitur ambulando*. Both Heidegger and Wittgenstein, per Richard Rorty, "ended by trying to work out honourable terms on which philosophy might surrender to poetry."
- Language, for Heidegger, "speaks in us," not the reverse: "Words and language are not wrappings in which things are packed for the commerce of those who write and speak." We must already, in some sense, understand something before we can come to "understand" it — echoed in Jung: "complexes can have us," not merely the reverse.
- **Schopenhauer** is named as an exceptional philosopher who held that art, especially music, reveals reality more directly than philosophy: "philosophical astonishment is therefore at bottom perplexed and melancholy; philosophy, like the overture to Don Juan, begins with a minor chord."
- The later **Wittgenstein** reaches similar conclusions from a different tradition: "If my name survives, then only as the *terminus ad quem* of the great philosophy of the West. As the name of him who burnt the library of Alexandria." He emphasised judgment and practice over rule-following, distrusted science's tendency to "reduce" and its deceptive clarity, and held that "Everything is what it is and not another thing." Per his disciple Friedrich Waismann, a psychological motive "thickens, hardens, and takes shape, as it were, only after we express it in words." Wittgenstein: "Man has to awaken to wonder — and so perhaps do peoples. Science is a way of sending him to sleep again."
- Heidegger, McGilchrist argues, also intuited the book's larger historical thesis (developed in Part Two): a relentless erosion of the right hemisphere's power across Western history. This parallels Freud's view that "secondary process" (reason) increasingly encroached on the "primary process" of instinct and intuition. Heidegger: "the fundamental event of the modern age is the conquest of the world as picture"; capitalism and communism are merely variants of a common exploitative "technicity"; "the long patient waiting for the gift" now looks like mere weakness.

## Scheler: the importance of value in constituting reality

- **Max Scheler**, Heidegger's contemporary and friend (died young), was praised by Heidegger as "the strongest philosophical force in Germany today... in contemporary philosophy as such." He extended phenomenology further into the exploration of value and feeling as constitutive of reality, not epiphenomenal.
- Values reach us *through* feeling (as colours reach us through sight) but are not themselves feelings. In ***The Nature of Sympathy*** (corroborated by later research), Scheler argued early experience is intersubjective, prior to any self/other distinction: "an immediate flow of experiences, undifferentiated as between mine and thine, which actually contains both our own and others' experiences intermingled."
- Scheler followed Pascal (per translator Manfred Frings) in giving affect primacy, but held that **love** specifically is the primary affect: man is *ens amans*, "a being that loves," its attractive power as fundamental as gravity.
- **Value** is pre-cognitive — neither purely subjective ("whatever I take it to be") nor purely consensual ("whatever we agree it to be") — it "comes to us in its own right," prior to any calculation. The same act by two different people can carry entirely different value, because we judge a person by the whole character we know them to be, not by summing their acts or traits ("It's like making mayonnaise: add too much oil too fast and the suspension breaks down"). Scheler's term for the capacity to register value is ***Wertnehmung*** ("value-ception"); our value-ceptive grasp of the whole governs our understanding of the parts, "one way of breaking into Escher's circle of hands."
- Scheler's **hierarchy of values** (Fig. 4.2, a pyramid):
  1. ***sinnliche Werte*** — sensory values (pleasant/unpleasant); utility sits at this same lowest level, since "nothing can meaningfully be called useful except as a means to pleasure."
  2. ***Lebenswerte*** — values of life/vitality: the noble (courage, bravery, sacrifice, daring, magnanimity, loyalty, humility) versus the mean (*gemein*: cowardice, pusillanimity, self-seeking, small-mindedness, treachery, arrogance).
  3. ***geistige Werte*** — values of intellect/spirit: justice, beauty and truth.
  4. ***das Heilige*** — the holy, the highest realm.
- The right hemisphere sees lower values as deriving their power from the higher ones they serve; the left hemisphere is reductionist, explaining higher values away in terms of use and pleasure.
- **Jon Elster**'s *Sour Grapes* (subtitled *Studies in the Subversion of Rationality*) is cited for values — wisdom, humility, virtue, courage, love, sympathy, admiration, faith, understanding — that vanish if pursued instrumentally for their utility ("yet another Gödelian point of weakness in rationalism," cf. the paradox of hedonism).
- The Hebrew of Genesis's "good and evil" (Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil) is said to mean precisely "the useful and the useless" — survival value in the most literal, left-hemisphere sense.

## Two worlds

Because twentieth-century philosophers, like physicists, kept reaching conclusions at odds with their own left-hemisphere method, and pointing instead to the primacy of the right hemisphere's world, McGilchrist concludes we live in two different types of world — and that most words we use to describe our relationship to the world (not just ones like "grasp") carry two distinct meanings.

### Seeing the world

- "Knowing as seeing" is one of the oldest and most consistent metaphors across all Indo-European languages, likely dating to the Ursprache. "I see" means "I understand."
- The common modern self-image — eyes as a camera lens we actively point, and a photographic plate that passively and objectively records "impressions" — is "the left hemisphere's vision of vision": linear, unidirectional, acquisitive.

### Are we active choosers?

- The left hemisphere, once it locks onto something, is "remarkably entrapped by its vision," with little real choice involved; the range it selects from is supplied by the right hemisphere's broader attention, and salience is often decided **pre-consciously** ("pre-attentional processing") before conscious reading even occurs.
- We are drawn to vitality, life and movement (a lit television, a fire — "focus" is Latin for hearth) almost involuntarily; this is the "stickiness" of the left hemisphere — "we are prisoners of expectation."
- New experience first engages the **right** hemisphere and only becomes "re-presented" by the **left** as it grows familiar; because the left hemisphere specialises in the already-familiar (per **Goldberg and Costa**) and tends to keep recurring to what it already knows, it risks trapping us permanently in the re-presented, "no longer alive" world.
- **Simons and Chabris**'s famous "invisible gorilla" experiment: subjects counting basketball passes in a video overwhelmingly fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene, beating its chest — demonstrating that we consciously see only what we are focally (left-hemisphere) attending to, and that our expectations circularly determine what we experience (related to what **Noë and O'Regan** call "the grand illusion").
- Neurocognitive science holds we can only "re-cognise," and therefore know, what we already have a model of in the brain — which would trap us permanently in left-hemisphere re-presentation, and doesn't explain how any model got there in the first place (Escher's hands again). After right-hemisphere stroke, the left hemisphere alone "sees what it expected to see." Heraclitus: "he who does not expect will not find out the unexpected, for it is trackless and unexplored." It is the right hemisphere's task to carry the left beyond itself to something genuinely new or "Other."
- The left hemisphere delivers what we *know* rather than what we *experience* — visible in its drawing style: "X-ray" drawings that include bones known to be present, and a poor grasp of scale, spatial relationship and depth.
- Conscious awareness lags unconscious apprehension by nearly half a second (citing **Chris Nunn**), so we can consciously perceive an illusory size while unconsciously grasping the object correctly. Nunn: conscious attention depends entirely on memory of what was being attended to a moment before — objects of attention are "extracted from memory that happen to coincide with features of the world 'out there.'" Compare Jung: "all cognition is akin to recognition." The Greek word for "to know," ***eidenai***, is related to ***idein*** ("to see") and originally meant "to have seen."

### Are we passive receivers?

- We are never simply passive recorders — "gaze alters what it finds." The ancient/Renaissance belief in rays emanating *from* the eye captures this: Homer's eagle-beams, Empedocles's "primeval fire" hidden in the pupil, and Plato's *Timaeus*, where light from within merges with light from the seen object to form "one body." Donne's *Extasie*: "Our eye-beames twisted, and did thred / Our eyes, upon one double string." Blake, anticipating Husserl: "A fool sees not the same tree that a wise man sees."
- Gaze can harm as well as connect (Pliny's basilisk, which kills with a look): the surgeon's focused, caring attention and the torturer's focused, controlling attention can look identical from outside, differing only in intention — which is also why the calculated detachment of the Holocaust's planners (engineers, physicians, psychiatrists) is so chilling. Attention changes what is attended to even when the object is a corpse.
- Scientific detachment is not an absence of relationship but "a disengaged relationship" that denies rather than eliminates "betweenness." **Merleau-Ponty**: "My eye for me is a certain power of making contact with things, and not a screen on which they are projected... The other's gaze transforms me into an object, and mine him, only if both of us withdraw into the core of our thinking nature [left hemisphere]..." Elsewhere he describes the moment "vision ceases to be solipsistic." In *The Visible and the Invisible*, the flesh's *l'épaisseur* ("thickness") — its semi-transparency, neither fully opaque nor fully transparent — is what allows self and other to be aware of one another as embodied beings; total transparency would just render it invisible, and alienate.
- Priming experiments show attention reshapes the self: quiz-takers primed with "professor" stereotypes scored 60%, those primed with "hooligan" scored 40% (secretary in between); "punk" priming increased rebelliousness versus "accountant" priming; violent video games increased aggression in young men; negative ageing stereotypes ("senile," "demented") worsened elderly subjects' memory performance and could even undermine the will to live, while positive ageing stereotypes ("wise," "experienced") improved memory performance; nurses in constant contact with elderly patients performed worse on memory tasks than those with infrequent contact.
- Mutual and shared gaze is a highly evolved trait: dogs read human gaze direction fluently, cats mostly do not (pointing at something just makes a cat look at the finger). The **right hemisphere** is the neurological substrate for shared/mutual gaze, face interpretation of a partner (right insula), and shared mental states generally; autism disrupts eye contact, gaze-following, joint attention and reading "the mind behind the gaze."
- Language reveals the same left/right divide: scientific prose says "the phenomenon was observed," not "I saw it happen." Japanese ***kansatsu*** ("to observe") carries a nuance closer to "gaze" — a rapt, self-losing attention producing a felt "one-body-ness" (*kan*) with the object — quite unlike the detached Western sense of scientific "observation."
- Even the hand and the concept of "grasp" have more than one mode: an outstretched hand can comfort, cure or quicken rather than merely take — as in Michelangelo's *Creation of Adam* (Fig. 4.3), where divine life passes to Adam's **left** hand (and so the right hemisphere), or the Chora church image of Christ grasping the dead by the arm to raise them. Etymologically, to "attend" means to reach a hand toward — "ad-tend" — the root of "tact," and implies giving as much as taking.

## Faux amis

The chapter closes by cataloguing words that mean something different depending on which hemisphere uses them — "like the left-hand and right-hand worlds seen by Alice on either side of the looking glass."

### Belief

- Left-hemisphere sense: belief is simply weaker knowledge, an admission of uncertainty ("I believe the train leaves at 6.13" = "I think, but am not certain, that...").
- Right-hemisphere sense: belief describes a **relationship of care** — "a calling and an answering, the root concept of 'responsibility.'" "I believe in you" doesn't report an uncertain fact but commits both parties to mutual obligations. Wittgenstein: "my attitude towards the other is an attitude towards a soul. I am not of the opinion that he has a soul."
- Belief in God, on this view, is not a factual existence-claim but a disposition — one can't avoid having *some* disposition toward the world (even materialism is itself a chosen "as if," per **Hans Vaihinger**, for whom all knowledge, including scientific knowledge, is an acting "as if" certain models were true).

### Will

- Left-hemisphere will concerns control and use; right-hemisphere will is "a desire or longing toward something... beyond itself, towards the Other."
- "Type" has two meanings: a reductive category (left hemisphere) versus Wittgenstein's Gestalt-based "family resemblances" (*Philosophical Investigations*) — categories with no single defining shared feature, learned the way children learn shapes. Citing **Bateson**: "all knowledge is knowledge of difference."
- **Ideal types/archetypes** ("the noble Roman," "the saint" — from Chris Nunn's *De la Mettrie's Ghost*) are aspirations rather than reductions: derived from but not encompassed by experience, affectively meaningful, narratively structured, and destroyed the moment they are converted into rules ("do this and this, and you will be a saint"). McGilchrist links these to **Jung's archetypes**, which bridge unconscious instinct and conscious cognition and exert a "pull" from collective human experience.
- The body itself has this dual nature — an instrument of manipulation, but also, per **Laban**, the vehicle for both "the attainment of tangible values in all kinds of work, and the approach to intangible values in prayer and worship."

### Familiarity and newness

- The same table is familiar as irreplaceably "mine" (right hemisphere) or as a generic member of the category "table" (left hemisphere); the same holds for the Eiffel Tower (lived-under vs. clichéd icon) and for music (deadened by passive overhearing vs. enlivened by active practice).
- One sense of "newness" is really the recovery of right-hemisphere familiarity from left-hemisphere staleness — Heidegger's "astonishment," related to **Jan Patočka**'s concept of "shakenness." Coleridge on Wordsworth's aim: to counter "the lethargy of custom... the film of familiarity and selfish solicitude" that leaves us with "eyes, yet see not, ears that hear not."
- A quite different, left-hemisphere sense of "newness" is the willed, shocking recombination of already-known parts — the modernist "make it new" (Pound), "The Shock of the New."

### Activity and passivity

- The activity/passivity dichotomy stems from a need for control, under which passivity looks like mere loss of instrumentality.
- But there is a "wise passivity" — sleep, and ultimately wisdom — that cannot be produced by an act of will. Keats's **"negative capability"**: "when a man is capable of being in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason."
- The chapter's penultimate point: the left hemisphere's attention must be reintegrated with the right hemisphere's "if it is not to prove damaging."

## Conclusion

- Intention shapes attention: the left hemisphere is always instrumentally purposive, downgrading whatever lacks a use; the right hemisphere is "vigilant for whatever is, without preconceptions," holding a relationship of care or concern — Heidegger's ***Sorge***.
- McGilchrist's summary contrast: the left hemisphere's world is "known, fixed, static, isolated, decontextualised, explicit, disembodied, general in nature, but ultimately lifeless"; the right hemisphere's world is "individual, changing, evolving, interconnected, implicit, incarnate, living... within the context of the lived world," but never fully graspable, always imperfectly known, and held in a relationship of care.
- The left hemisphere's knowledge is a **closed system**: perfect, but at the price of emptiness and self-reference, capable only of mechanically rearranging what it already knows, never truly able to "break out" to the genuinely new, because it knows only its own re-presentations. What is *present* to the right hemisphere becomes merely *re-presented*, "an idea of a thing," by the left. "Where the right hemisphere is conscious of the Other, whatever it may be, the left hemisphere's consciousness is of itself."
- This answers the chapter's third opening question: "There is no such thing as the brain, only the brain according to the right hemisphere and the brain according to the left hemisphere" — the two hemispheres that bring everything else into being also, inevitably, bring themselves into being, like Escher's hands. To some, the brain is simply a thing, a machine, understood bottom-up for a recognisable purpose; to others its nature is unique, understandable only by accepting "a degree of not-knowing" — hence some will always be confident they know exactly what kind of thing the brain is, while others will know only "precious little."
