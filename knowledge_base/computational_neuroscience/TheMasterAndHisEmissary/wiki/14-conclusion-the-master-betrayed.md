# Conclusion: The Master Betrayed

The book's final chapter draws together its whole argument into a single verdict: the left hemisphere, meant to serve as emissary to the right hemisphere's Master, has usurped him, and the modern West increasingly resembles what a world *would* look like if that usurpation succeeded completely. McGilchrist first paints an extended, systematic portrait of such a left-hemisphere-dominated world — bureaucratic, mechanistic, abstracted, anxious, controlling — and argues we are already living much of it. He then tests the left hemisphere by its own stated goal, the maximisation of happiness, and shows with data that decades of rising material prosperity have not delivered it. He surveys three remaining "escape routes" from the left hemisphere's self-enclosed system — the body, the spirit, and art — and how each is currently under assault. He closes by asking whether there is room for hope, finding it in the circular (rather than linear) shape of real change, in the possibility of a further, redemptive form of reflection, and in what the more right-hemisphere-balanced cultures of the East might teach the West. The chapter, and the book, end with McGilchrist's own carefully hedged final position: that the two hemispheres may be, at the very least, a metaphor for two fundamentally opposed but complementary ways of being — and that he would be content even if it proved to be "just" a metaphor.

## The Master's necessary vulnerability

- Epigraph, from Pascal: "All the miseries of man but prove his greatness. They are the miseries of a great lord, the miseries of a king that is dispossessed."
- McGilchrist frames the hemispheres' relationship as inherently asymmetric and precarious: the right hemisphere "believes, but does not know," and must depend on the left hemisphere, which "knows, but doesn't believe." An "infinite, and therefore intrinsically uncertain, potential Being needs nonetheless to submit to be delimited — needs stasis, certainty, fixity — in order to Be."
- > "The Master needs to trust, to believe in, his emissary, knowing all the while that that trust may be abused. The emissary knows, but knows wrongly, that he is invulnerable. If the relationship holds, they are invincible; but if it is abused, it is not just the Master that suffers, but both of them, since the emissary owes his existence to the Master." — Iain McGilchrist

## What would the left hemisphere's world look like?

McGilchrist runs a thought experiment: imagining a world where the left hemisphere has "phenomenologically" suppressed the right hemisphere's world almost entirely.

- **Loss of the broad picture and rise of specialisation.** The whole would be seen as no more than the sum of its parts; narrower and narrower focus would drive increasing specialisation and "technicalising" of knowledge; **information** (gathered) would replace **knowledge** (gained through experience), and knowledge in turn would seem more "real" than **wisdom**.
- **Expertise replaced by credentialed "expert" knowledge.** Skill and judgment — the traditional "summit of human achievement," acquired slowly through living — would be discarded for quantifiable, regulated, algorithmic procedures; the concrete would give way to the theoretical/abstract, which would come to seem more convincing. (McGilchrist notes the etymology: *expertus*, "one who is experienced.")
- **Abstraction and reification together.** Bodies, people, the material world and works of art would become simultaneously more conceptual and more "thing"-like; more work would shift from contact with the real, lived world to paperwork, management, and the "meta-process" of documenting or justifying what one is (supposedly) doing.
- **Bureaucracy would thrive.** Drawing on **Peter Berger** and colleagues, McGilchrist lists the essential features of bureaucracy that the left hemisphere facilitates: the necessity of procedures that are known and knowable; **anonymity**; **organisability**; **predictability**; a concept of justice reduced to mere equality; and **explicit abstraction** — with a complete loss of the sense of uniqueness.
- **Technology and "mechanisticity."** Technology would flourish as an expression of the left hemisphere's desire to manipulate and control, reinforced by an expanding bureaucracy. Berger and colleagues' predicted features of a technologically dominated consciousness: **"mechanisticity"** (endless reproducibility, submergence of the individual into an organisation/production line), **"measurability"** (quantification over qualification), **"componentiality"** (reality reduced to self-contained, interchangeable units), and an **"abstract frame of reference"** (loss of context).
- Philosopher **Gabriel Marcel** is invoked on the danger of losing "the mystery of being" to a set of merely technical problems, warning we might be "too easily persuaded to accept the role thrust upon us, to become an object, no longer a subject," and so "connive at our own annihilation."
- **Quantity over quality.** The left hemisphere is "the hemisphere of What," so quantity (scale, speed, precision) would become the only criterion it understands, displacing the right hemisphere's sense of **How** (quality) — even though increasing scale, speed, or precision in real human affairs can be actively destructive, not just neutral.
- **The impersonal over the personal; exploitation over co-operation.** Social bonds between people, and between people and place, would be neglected or actively disrupted; **resentment** would push uniformity and equality to become the *ultimate* desirable rather than one value among others; identity would collapse into competing categories (socioeconomic groups, races, sexes); **paranoia and lack of trust** would pervade both interpersonal relations and the relationship between government and governed.
- **Total control and the surveillance state.** Talk of "liberty" would increase for Machiavellian reasons even as individual liberty is curtailed; panoptical control (CCTV, interception of communications, DNA databases) would be introduced ostensibly against exceptional threats but really to expand state power and shrink the individual; the state would erode individual responsibility even as it claims to promote it.
- **Suspicion of altruistic institutions.** Family, and vocations built on trust and altruism (priests, teachers, doctors), would be regarded with suspicion, since the left hemisphere misreads altruism as disguised self-interest, and would be pressured under increasing bureaucratic control.
- **Death taboo, sex explicit.** Where the Renaissance and nineteenth century (right-hemisphere-ascendant periods) treated death as omnipresent and part of the fabric of life, in a left-hemisphere world death — the ultimate challenge to its sense of control — would become the unspeakable taboo, while sex, whose power depends on the implicit, would become relentlessly explicit.
- **Rationality replacing reasonableness; loss of common sense.** Anger and aggression (the most left-hemisphere-characteristic emotions) would rise, unchecked by right-hemisphere empathy; there would be a loss of insight paired with an "unwarranted optimism" and rising intolerance/inflexibility.
- **Passivity and "forced utilisation behaviour."** An analogue of the neurological syndrome in which patients compulsively use whatever is placed before them; increasing suggestibility, decline in self-motivated will-power (though not in acquisitive greed), and people relating to culture passively, "like a photographic plate to light."
- **Disenchantment.** Weber's "disenchanted" world: religion dismissed as fantasy, exemplars of value constantly ironised or deconstructed, **pathos** becoming "impossible, perhaps shameful," and boredom/nausea before life driving a craving for novelty and stimulation.
- **Loss of flow and uniqueness** — the **Zeitraffer** phenomenon (time-lapse-like fragmentation of experience into discrete frames), affecting historical, cultural, and personal time alike, coupled with over-familiarity through endless reproduction.
- **Loss of the tacit; rise of explicit rule.** Difficulty understanding non-explicit, non-verbal meaning; ever-increasing legislation — **de Tocqueville's** "network of small complicated rules" — filling the gap left by a shared intuitive moral sense; loss of tolerance for ambiguity; over-explicitness applied even to art and religion.
- **Art and language reduced.** Art becoming conceptual and losing metaphorical/incarnate power; visual art losing depth; music reduced to rhythm without harmony or melody; dance becoming solipsistic rather than communal; nature seen as mere exploitable resource; language becoming diffuse, abstract, and dominated by technical/bureaucratic idiom even when applied to descriptions of the human mind itself.
- McGilchrist's summary judgment: "This is what the world would look like if the emissary betrayed the Master. It's hard to resist the conclusion that his goal is within sight."

## Could the left hemisphere succeed according to its own criteria?

McGilchrist turns to judge the left hemisphere's project — mechanistic control of the world in pursuit of happiness — by its **own** professed standard, not the right hemisphere's.

- Materialist well-being and happiness are shown to be essentially uncorrelated once basic needs are met.
- **United States:** life satisfaction has *declined* over the last 25 years despite an enormous increase in prosperity, with a possible **inverse** relationship between economic growth and happiness. Citing **Putnam**, in 1955, 44% of US workers said they enjoyed their working hours more than anything else they did; by 1999, only 16% did.
- **Britain:** Gallup data show the British happier throughout the 1950s than today, despite now being three times richer in real terms — 52% called themselves "very happy" in 1957 versus 36% today.
- **Japan:** in 1958 one of the poorest countries in the world (comparable to India and Brazil then), with average income about one-eighth of the US level in 1991; over 40-plus years it saw an unprecedented ~500% real increase in per capita income — yet happiness levels have not changed at all, with a slight downturn just before the recent global economic crisis.
- **Europe:** the Euro-Barometer satisfaction surveys across fifteen countries over the decade to 2000 show four clusters, each trending flat or slightly negative — the "hedonic treadmill" keeping modern consumers in a "permanent state of unfulfilled desire." (Samuel Johnson, quoted: "Life is a progress from want to want, not from enjoyment to enjoyment.")
- Psychologist **Geoffrey Miller**: age, sex, race, income, geographic location, nationality, and education level together explain **less than 2%** of the variance in happiness (with the exception that people below a minimum threshold of calories/security are somewhat less happy). Happiness plateaus at a national income of roughly **$10,000–$20,000 (£7,500–£15,000)** per year.
- **Robert Putnam** (*Bowling Alone*): the single most consistent finding across half a century of research worldwide is that happiness is best predicted by "the breadth and depth of one's social connections."
- Depression rates vary as much as **12-fold** between cultures and track social interconnectedness. Among Mexican immigrants to the US, lifetime prevalence of any mental disorder rises from **18%** (under 13 years in the US) to **32%** (over 13 years) to **49%** for those born in the US (matching the national rate).
- A study of nearly **40,000 people** across North America, Western Europe, the Middle East, Asia, and the Pacific Rim found depression occurring more often, at younger ages, and more severely in successive birth cohorts, having **doubled** in the US since the Second World War.
- **"Social connectedness"** predicts lower rates of colds, heart attacks, strokes, cancer, depression, and premature death generally, with protective effects rivalling those of avoiding smoking, obesity, high blood pressure, and inactivity. Putnam: "statistically speaking, the evidence for the health consequences of social connectedness is as strong today as was the evidence for health consequences of smoking at the time of the first surgeon general's report on smoking."
- Case study: **Roseto**, Pennsylvania, a close-knit Italian-immigrant community that in the 1940s had a heart-attack rate less than half the national average despite higher-than-average risk factors; once the younger generation moved away and rejected the traditional "folkways," researchers' prediction that the heart-attack rate would rise came true by the 1980s.
- McGilchrist's conclusion: happiness and fulfilment are "by-products of other things," of broad empathic attention rather than narrow getting-and-using — something the right hemisphere would grasp readily even as it stays opaque to the left. "It seems, then, that, even in its own terms, the left hemisphere is bound to fail." Yet that will not stop it continuing on its current path, especially since two of the traditional sources of resistance — a coherent cultural tradition of communal wisdom, and direct contact with the natural world — are themselves prime targets of the process it has set in motion.
- He also rejects the framing (via **Panksepp**) of this shift as cognition simply out-evolving emotion: the right hemisphere is not a more primitive competitor but has "the most sophisticated and extensive, and quite possibly most lately evolved, representation in the prefrontal cortex, the most highly evolved part of the brain." The apparent triumph of rationality over intuition across history is, he suggests, itself evidence that "we have already fallen for the left hemisphere's propaganda."

## The left hemisphere's attempts to block our exit from its hall of mirrors

The left hemisphere's self-enclosed, self-reflexive world has three points of weakness it cannot fully close off — the **body**, the **soul**, and **art** (which depends on body and soul together) — each grounded principally by the right hemisphere and each bypassing rational, explicit language. Because of this they threaten its dominion and have drawn a determined counter-response.

### The body

- Our apparent overvaluing of the body (exercise, diet, "lifestyles," constant display) is deceptive: the body has instead become "a thing, a thing we possess, a mechanism... a bit like a sports car with a smart sound system" — an object like other objects, as **Merleau-Ponty** feared, reflecting the left hemisphere's fundamentally **narcissistic** view of the world as a mirror of itself.
- Quoting **Martin Foss** (*Symbol and Metaphor in Human Experience*): the body is "an instrument to life," a "potential for the soul," whose most essential characteristic "is that it disappears as an independent thing the more it fulfils its service" — we notice it mainly when something is wrong.
- McGilchrist extends **Merleau-Ponty's** idea of art's "necessary transparency": we live *through* the body as we see *through* a work of art, and it must remain transparent to allow us to be fully alive. Modern pornography (which he notes began, tellingly, in the Enlightenment) keeps the flesh opaque and explicit, robbing sex of its power — "like most answers to boredom, pornography is itself characterised by the boredom it aims to dispel."
- The body-as-machine view, McGilchrist observes, closely resembles the phenomenology of **schizophrenia**: patients often describe themselves as robots, computers, or cameras with metal/electronic parts, lacking "transparency of the flesh." One patient: "body and soul don't belong together – there's no unity." **R. D. Laing** describes a patient who perceived his lively wife as a mechanical "it," her laughter at his joke dismissed as merely "conditioned" and without real feeling.
- Citing **Alain Corbin**, we have become more cerebral, retreating from smell, touch, and taste as if repelled by the body, with sight — "the coolest of the senses," most capable of detachment — coming to dominate.
- Consequence for medicine: treating illness as an assemblage of discrete parts (ignoring emotional, psychological, spiritual dimensions) limits the effectiveness of Western medicine and drives people toward alternative treatments.
- The natural world suffers the same fate as the body: it has been **commodified**, debate over it reduced to "the environment," jobs, "recreational" benefit, "biodiversity," or "viability of the biomass."

### The spirit

- The left hemisphere's assault on religion, well underway by the **Reformation** and furthered by the **Enlightenment**, saw a countervailing resurgence during **Romanticism** — a reaffirmation not so much of religion as of "a sense of the holy," best understood as a form of **panentheism** (God as *in* all things, contrasted with pantheism, which equates God *with* the sum of things).
- Religion nonetheless withered through the twentieth century under capitalism (as it was meant to under Marxism). In early Soviet Russia, where religion was still a living force, the reaction was extreme: Stalinists converted the Cathedral of Our Lady of Kazan in Moscow into a public lavatory — the left hemisphere, McGilchrist notes drily, "pissed on religion, as it had pissed on art when **Marcel Duchamp** exhibited his notorious urinal."
- Quoting **Nietzsche**: "Did one not finally have to sacrifice everything comforting, holy, healing, all hope, all faith concealed in harmony... Did one not have to sacrifice God himself and out of cruelty against oneself worship stone, stupidity, gravity, fate, nothingness?"
- McGilchrist argues the Western Church has undermined itself — abandoning confidence in its own values for material answers, and its liturgical reform movement eroding the metaphoric power of ritual by insisting religious truths can be stated literally — even as religious practices (e.g., Zen meditation) are rehabilitated as secular *utility* for stress or health.
- His broader claim: **myth/mythos is not optional** — we cannot avoid choosing some myth to live by, and absent a better one we default to the myth of the machine. He treats the 2,000-year-old Christian mythos (agnostic as to its literal truth) as exceptionally rich for understanding our relationship to the world, centred on **incarnation** (matter and spirit united) and **resurrection** (redemption of that union), with a God who is "engaged, vulnerable... and, like the right hemisphere rather than the left... suffering alongside" creation rather than resentful of it.
- In an age when conventional religion appeals to fewer people, art may carry ultimate meaning instead: Schumann said of Bach's chorale prelude *Ich ruf' zu dir* (which Tarkovsky used to open *Solaris*) that hearing it could restore faith in a man who had lost it entirely; McGilchrist cites the same power in Bach's Passions and in the depiction of Christ and his mother at the ancient church of St Saviour in Khora, Istanbul.

### Art

- McGilchrist writes personally here, contrasting **Tracey Emin's** unmade bed (and "so much other post-modern art") with Bach versus Stockhausen, feeling "we have lost not just the plot, but our sense of the absurd" — predicting the age will be remembered for both its cynicism *and* its gullibility.
- He rejects the idea that taste in art is purely a social construction: before modernism, audiences did not typically find new styles unpleasant or incomprehensible — Monteverdi's first listeners were "enrapt," Handel had to keep rehearsals secret against overcrowding, and Liszt and Chopin were "mobbed and swooned over," more like pop idols than today's art-music composers.
- **Adorno's** predicted commodification of art has proceeded apace, taming and trivialising it into mere utility for relaxation or self-improvement.
- On beauty: it is not simply whatever we agree to call it, nor does it vanish if art theory ignores it — like goodness, its core concept remains "remarkably stable over time" even as its edges shift, which is why we can still appreciate medieval or ancient art. Yet contemporary art criticism has effectively airbrushed beauty out, favouring the "rhetoric of power" — works praised as "strong" or "challenging" — with pathos (belief in a shared reality we cannot isolate ourselves from) replaced first by Angst (modernism) and then by mere joking irony (post-modernism).
- Quotes Nietzsche's later self-critical reflection on *The Birth of Tragedy*, questioning whether "Socratism," scientific method, and "Greek cheerfulness" might themselves be signs of "decline... exhaustion... sickness" — "a subtle defence against — truth?"
- Purely intellectualised, "conceptual" art suits the age because it is easy and "democratic" — requiring neither long apprenticeship nor intuition, both partly innate gifts and therefore "undemocratic." McGilchrist likens the resulting cultural atomism to Warhol's "famous for fifteen minutes," and warns "a society is, or should be, an organic unity, not an assemblage of bits that strive with one another. It is as if every organ in the body wanted to be the head."
- He draws an extended parallel with the **Reformation** — the last great assault on art — noting its target was "the holy" rather than "the beautiful," and that reformers didn't need to declare "everything is equally holy"; it was enough that the very *terms* moved on until nobody believed in the holy at all. Modernism/post-modernism achieves the same with beauty, without needing to assert "everything is equally beautiful."
- Beauty, McGilchrist argues, is categorically different from desire: desire is "unidirectional, purposive, ultimately acquisitive," like an arrow's flight, whereas our relation to the beautiful is "more like longing, or love, a betweenness... which has no ulterior purpose... and is non-acquisitive" — citing **Leibniz's** "disinterested love," **Kant's** "disinterested pleasure," and **Burke's** "love [that is] different from desire."
- Synthesis: the body, the spirit, and art are all, ultimately, "vehicles of love" — "the attractive power of the Other, which the right hemisphere experiences, but which the left hemisphere does not understand and sees as an impediment to its authority." Through their assault we risk becoming trapped in the **I–it** world, with the exits back to the **I–thou** world progressively sealed.

## Is there room for hope?

McGilchrist states plainly: "My theme may seem pessimistic. I do think that there are, nonetheless, reasons for hope." He first notes small signs that we may be starting to move on from limiting preconceptions about physical existence, spiritual life, and art, then develops two more structural grounds for hope.

### Linear progression versus circular

- The left hemisphere's world is fundamentally **linear/rectilinear**: sequential cognitive style, mechanical construction piece by piece, "always having an end in view" — "the reaching of the right hand towards its object, or the flying of the arrow from the bow."
- McGilchrist re-reads Pavlov's classical conditioning as a case in point: framed as pure linear causation (bell → salivation), but a modified thought experiment (ringing the bell *after* eating begins) shows the dog is really registering an associative **context**, not a mechanical trigger — "the dog is reduced to a mechanism by the temporal sequencing... and by the stripping away of the context to focus on a sequence." He extends this to cause and effect generally, e.g., a bat striking a ball is as much "necessitated" by the ball's flight as the reverse — a "stickiness" of context rather than one-way causation.
- Citing **Leonard Shlain**: the only apparently straight line in nature is the horizon — itself actually a section of a curve; even space is curved. Straight lines proliferate wherever the left hemisphere predominates: the grid-planned towns and roads of the late Roman Empire, Classicism (versus the curve-loving Baroque), the Industrial Revolution's rectilinear machine products, and the modern grid-city.
- By contrast, the right hemisphere's shape is the **circle** — wholeness understood "in the round," cognition as something coming into focus rather than assembled piece by piece, movement like dance (which "always ultimately returns to its origins") rather than the unidirectional grasp. Shakespeare's comedies celebrate community in the closing **ring-dance**, against which the melancholy Jaques (in *As You Like It*) represents the linear tragedy of an individual life running through the seven ages to be "sans everything."
- A long survey of circle/sphere imagery across Western culture as evidence of right-hemisphere ascendancy: Dante's love "that moves the sun and the other stars"; Shakespeare's "our little life is rounded with a sleep"; Ralegh and Donne's compass imagery; Blake's "Reason is the bound or outward circumference of Energy"; Shelley and Wordsworth ("the round earth and the living air"); Van Gogh's "life is probably round"; Jaspers's "jedes Dasein scheint in sich rund" ("every Dasein seems in itself to be round"); Guillaume de Machaut's palindromic rondeau *Ma fin est mon commencement*; the sphere of God with "centre... everywhere and circumference nowhere," traced from the **Corpus Hermeticum** through Alain de Lille, Nicholas of Cusa, Giordano Bruno, and Pascal; Aristotle's 55 nested spheres; Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de Sphaera* (c. 1230); Kepler's *Harmonice Mundi* (1610); and Copernicus's view of gravity as a "natural inclination... to combine the parts in the form of a sphere," what Arthur Koestler called "the nostalgia of things to become spheres."
- These intuitions align, McGilchrist notes, with the **cyclical** views of history found in most cultures outside the modern West (e.g., Hindu cosmology) — "the myth of the eternal return is a cultural universal."

### The glint in the maiden's eye

- Hope also lies in the "fruitfulness of opposition," what Nietzsche (echoing Heraclitus) calls war: "The spirit grows, [and] strength is restored, by wounding" (*Increscunt animi, virescit volnere virtus*) — the obvious inauthenticity of the left-hemisphere world we inhabit may itself provoke the will to change it, though McGilchrist concedes he "may be wrong in seeing the present situation as different" from past cycles.
- He quotes **Hegel** on how, once "the oracles... no longer speak to men" and "the statues have become stone corpses," the remnants of past culture reach us only as "beautiful fruit broken off a tree," offered with the "glint of self-awareness" in the eye of the maiden who hands it to us — the same Nature that produced the fruit, but "at a higher level."
- This parallels Wordsworth's poem on the country folk at the fair seen from Helvellyn: lacking their unselfconscious immediacy, but achieving something at a higher level of self-awareness they could not reach — an "innocence of the adult" bought "at the cost of much painful awareness," achieved (per Wordsworth, Blake, Keats) through a vulnerability that risks being mistaken for foolishness.
- Excessive self-consciousness is itself a prison (like schizophrenia's inbuilt reflexivity, the "hall of mirrors"), and cannot be escaped by direct act of will. But McGilchrist proposes that reflection may itself supply the antidote: **Heidegger**, **Wittgenstein**, and **Merleau-Ponty** each embody, in their critique of reflection, "a reflective attempt to surmount reflection" — invoking Hölderlin again: "Where there is danger, that which will save us also grows."
- Merleau-Ponty's term **surréflexion** (hyper-reflection) names the hoped-for process of learning to see again by becoming conscious of consciousness's own distorting effects — an idea McGilchrist traces back to the Romantics, especially **Kleist's** essay "On the Puppet Theatre," which closes: "'Grace appears purest in that human form which has either no consciousness or an infinite one, that is, in a puppet or in a god.' 'Therefore,' I said, somewhat bewildered, 'we would have to eat again from the Tree of Knowledge in order to return to the state of innocence?' 'Quite right,' he answered. 'And that's the last chapter in the history of the world.'"
- McGilchrist reads Kleist (and Hölderlin) as warning that full return may only be possible "in another world, where there are gods," yet also as confirming we can move only **onward**, not backward — transcending our situation rather than reversing it.
- He closes this thread with the image (via **Gombrich** on art, and his own recurring language of transparency/translucency) of water present *in* an ice cube yet remaining water once the ice is gone — body and soul, metaphor and sense, myth and reality are not one thing hiding behind another; "we see it in — through — the mirror," as Goethe put it, rejecting the idea of going "round the back of the mirror" to find some separate reality.

### What we might learn from Oriental culture

- McGilchrist argues Oriental cultures — never having undergone the same sharp Greek-originated hemispheric split — remain "still effectively grounded" in right-hemisphere experience, offering a possible model for rebalancing the West.
- Linguistic/philosophical evidence: Japanese lacks an established method for abstract nouns and has no definite/indefinite articles (considered crucial to the emergence of Greek abstraction); the Japanese have "nothing that corresponds to the Platonic Idea." Quoting **Nakamura**: the Japanese "regard the phenomenal world itself as Absolute and reject the recognition of anything existing over [and above]" it.
- In Zen Buddhism, per abbot **Soiku Shigematsu**: "a word is a finger that points at the moon. The goal of Zen pupils is the moon itself, not the pointing finger."
- **Shizen**, the Japanese word for nature, is literally an adverb meaning "of itself"/"spontaneously," implying mutual trust between humanity and nature rather than opposition, and covers landscape and the "natural self" together — with an intuition of **animism** (per anthropologist Iwata) in which mountains, rivers, plants, and animals each possess their own spirit (**kami**).
- Impermanence itself is valued (**shizen** as **Buddhahood**/the essence of the divine); Japanese temples are considered "the same temple" though physically rebuilt every 20 years — McGilchrist notes this would resolve the paradox of the **Ship of Theseus** effortlessly, since the world is seen as process, not a collection of static things, "like Heraclitus' river."
- He traces a parallel Western intuition — that value lies in **becoming**, not static being — surfacing at the Renaissance and in Romanticism, quoting Spenser's *Faerie Queene* ("Cantos of Mutabilitie"): "all things stedfastnes doe hate / And changed be: yet being rightly wayd / They are not changed from their first estate; / But by their change their being doe dilate..." and Nietzsche's preference for "the value of the shortest and most fleeting" over "what remains eternally the same."
- Cross-cultural cognitive research is surveyed at length: East Asians categorise less rigidly, attend more to context and family resemblance versus Westerners' rule-based, single-dimension responses; they use more **"dialectical"** reasoning, tolerating or seeking contradiction (preferring proverbs like "too humble is half proud" over non-contradictory ones like "half a loaf is better than no bread," which Americans preferred); a study of an underwater cartoon found Japanese viewers recalled an integrated scene (pond, school of fish, seaweed) while Americans recalled a few foreground fish; Japanese and American cityscapes differ in ambiguity/density, and priming with Japanese scenes increased contextual attention in *both* Japanese and American participants.
- Self/society: Eastern cultures are more **"interdependent,"** with the Japanese word for self, **jibun**, implying something "both separate and not separate"; contrary to a common Western misconception, individuality (originality, self-direction) is highly prized, but alongside a stronger sense of obligation to belong. High self-esteem, positively correlated in the West with unrealism, taking offence, and violence, is not the Japanese ideal; Japanese are more self-critical and (unlike Western students) respond to failure with determination rather than discouragement.
- Left/right symbolism: unlike most cultures (where left is "sinister"), ancient Chinese thought held the left to be **yang** and superior; Zuñi Indians of North America hold the left the "elder and wiser" brother god.
- Neuroimaging: cerebral asymmetries in Chinese populations are broadly similar to, though slightly less marked than, those of North Americans; Chinese speakers show less overall hemispheric asymmetry for language and stronger right-temporoparietal activation than English/Spanish speakers; Hong Kong Cantonese speakers, however, show asymmetry similar to Europeans.
- Synthesis, citing sinologist **Joseph Needham's** observation of a Western "fondness for particles" to which the Chinese were "perennially averse": the evidence, taken together, consistently favours **greater left-hemisphere reliance in the West**, with East Asian cultures using both hemispheres' strategies more evenly rather than the right hemisphere alone. McGilchrist frames it: "the emissary appears to work in harmony with the Master in the East, but is in the process of usurping him in the West." He notes the Western skew brings real but narrow advantages (better performance on arbitrary rule-learning/categorisation), just as the dominant hand in right-handers improves at the cost of the other hand's decline — and that Asian-Americans, straddling both traditions, fall between the two cognitive profiles, suggesting acculturation could in principle run the other way if pursued "before [Eastern] cultures are Westernised beyond redemption."
- He closes the section suggesting music — which "brought us together before language existed" — might help regenerate commonality where words have become devalued or too readily met with cynicism ("it was with music that Orpheus once moved stones"), and calls for "a regrounding of both art and science in the lived world," including a more careful, less reductive scientific language for describing ourselves, especially given the current rise of interest in neuroscience.

## Conclusion: the final position

- McGilchrist opens the closing pages with **Richard Strauss's** opera *Ariadne auf Naxos*: a wealthy patron commissions a serious tragic opera (on Ariadne, abandoned by Theseus) alongside a comic commedia dell'arte troupe, then at the last minute demands both be performed **simultaneously**, "served up together" — a farrago at once moving, comic, and incongruous. He suggests librettist Hugo von Hofmannsthal, reading Milton's *L'Allegro* and *Il Penseroso* at the time, may have unconsciously channelled *Paradise Lost*, which McGilchrist reads as itself "a profound self-exploration of the divided human brain": two unequal powers, one grounding the other and needing it for fulfilment, one of which (through "blindness and vanity") rejects the union that would bring about their mutual **Aufhebung**, choosing instead "a state of war without end" — with Adam and Eve's expulsion from paradise as its fallout.
- McGilchrist restates his opening claim in its fullest form: that "the inner structure of our intellect reflects the structure of the universe" — not merely true by definition (as for those who think the universe is simply a creation of our brains), but that our brains "are likely themselves to reflect, in their structure and functioning, the nature of the universe in which they have come about."
- On method: the book's argument is not built left-hemisphere-fashion by summing details, but should be grasped right-hemisphere-fashion, as "the pattern... as the Dalmatian emerges from the blur of splashes and dots." Quoting **Karl Popper**: "bold ideas, unjustified anticipations and speculative thought, are our only means for interpreting nature: our only organon, our only instrument for grasping her." McGilchrist adds: "Or, perhaps, reaching out a hand to her."
- **A plea for uncertainty**, offered as close to the book's core stance as any single passage:
  > "Certainty is the greatest of all illusions: whatever kind of fundamentalism it may underwrite, that of religion or of science, it is what the ancients meant by hubris. The only certainty, it seems to me, is that those who believe they are certainly right are certainly wrong." — Iain McGilchrist
  He places both religious no-faith dogmatists and complacent scientific certainty on the same side of this error, and notes that "virtually every great physicist of the last century — Einstein, Bohr, Planck, Heisenberg, Bohm" — recognised that a leap of faith is involved in science too. Quoting **Max Planck**: "Anybody who has been seriously engaged in scientific work of any kind realizes that over the entrance to the gates of the temple of science are written the words: Ye must have faith... Science cannot solve the ultimate mystery of nature. And that is because, in the last analysis, we ourselves are part of nature and therefore part of the mystery that we are trying to solve."
- Quoting **Lessing**, on choosing the eternal *pursuit* of truth over possession of it — including the flourish that it is the **left hand** (servant of the right hemisphere) that "contains the ever-living striving for truth."
- On his own method and its limits: "In this book certainty has certainly not been my aim... I share Wittgenstein's mistrust of deceptively clear models," quoting **Waismann**: "any psychological explanation is ambiguous, cryptic and open-ended, for we ourselves are many-layered, contradictory and incomplete beings." He anticipates the charge that this sounds like a "cop-out," but insists that the intrinsic resistance of real things to precision "is not our failure, but an indication of the nature of what we are dealing with" — striving for understanding must be "imbued with a tactful recognition of the limits to human understanding. The rest is hubris."
- **His final, explicitly hedged position** on the hemisphere thesis itself:
  > "If it could eventually be shown definitively that the two major ways, not just of thinking, but of being in the world, are not related to the two cerebral hemispheres, I would be surprised, but not unhappy." — Iain McGilchrist

  What matters more to him than the neurological mapping is that there are "consistent ways of being that persist across the history of the Western world, that are fundamentally opposed, though complementary, in what they reveal to us" — and that the two hemispheres can be seen as, "at the very least, a metaphor" for this opposition. One consequence he draws explicitly: we may need to give up the assumption that we understand the world better than our ancestors, in favour of the more modest (and unsettling) possibility that we simply see it *differently* — "and may indeed be seeing less than they did."
- He situates this within a long lineage of thinkers who intuited the divided nature of experience without his neuropsychological frame: Goethe's Faust ("Zwei Seelen wohnen, ach! in meiner Brust" — "two souls, alas! dwell in my breast"); Schopenhauer's "zwei völlig heterogene Weisen gegebene Erkenntniß" (two completely heterogeneous forms of given knowledge); Bergson's "deux réalités d'ordre différent" (two different orders of reality); and Scheler's "Bürger zweier Welten" (citizen of two worlds), a formulation he attributes also to Kant.
- **The book's closing line:**
  > "But if it turns out to be 'just' a metaphor, I will be content. I have a high regard for metaphor. It is how we come to understand the world." — Iain McGilchrist
