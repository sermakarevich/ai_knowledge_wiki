# Chapter 11: Romanticism and the Industrial Revolution

McGilchrist argues that Romanticism was not a revolution against the Enlightenment but a right-hemisphere "evolution" out of it — a shift in *disposition* toward the world (a "how," not a "what") that recovered embodiment, particularity, depth, melancholic longing, half-perceived truth, and the union of opposites that Enlightenment rationalism had suppressed. He traces this recovery through philosophy, landscape painting, and above all the poetry of Wordsworth, Blake and Hopkins, before pivoting, in the chapter's final section, to argue that the Industrial Revolution was the left hemisphere's most audacious countermove: not just the mechanisation of production but the wholesale replacement of the right hemisphere's world of given, particular, embodied things with a manufactured world already in the left hemisphere's own image — identical, rectilinear, mechanistic — foreclosing, in ways the rest of the book will trace, the right hemisphere's access to anything genuinely "Other."

## Romanticism as a right-hemisphere evolution

- Isaiah Berlin's *The Roots of Romanticism* spends its whole first chapter cataloguing mutually incompatible definitions of the term; his conclusion is that, unlike the Enlightenment, which could be reduced to "the cognitive content of a relatively small number of beliefs," Romanticism concerns "a whole disposition towards the world" — not a *what* but a **how**.
- Against the image of a "Romantic Revolution" (analogous to the French Revolution), McGilchrist argues the actual transition was "an almost invisible, seamless transition" — better called a **Romantic Evolution**, since the seeds of Romanticism were already present within Enlightenment thought.
- The best of Enlightenment values were not negated but ***aufgehoben*** (sublated/taken up) by Romanticism and persist to this day, alongside some of the Enlightenment's "more damagingly simplistic notions."
- McGilchrist's hemisphere mapping: the **right hemisphere** grapples with experience that is "multiple in nature, in principle unknowable in its totality, changing, infinite, full of individual differences"; the **left hemisphere** sees only a representation of that experience — "single, knowable, consistent, certain, fixed... ultimately finite, generalised" — "the Enlightenment world." The left hemisphere is a closed, **"bootstrapping"** system, and its weakness is exposed wherever attention turns to what points beyond it.
- Since Enlightenment thought assumes all truths cohere and are reconcilable, its weak point is wherever *incompatibilities* surface. **Montesquieu** noticed that "man is everywhere different" is as true as "man is everywhere the same" — a paradox generated from within the Enlightenment's own premises. This marks the shift from a world where "A and not-A cannot both be true" is necessarily true, to one where "A and not-A can both hold" holds (McGilchrist links this to Hegel's thesis–antithesis–synthesis).
- The French Revolution exemplifies the ambiguity: fought in the name of Enlightenment principles (reason, order, justice, liberty), yet itself, in spirit, a Romantic phenomenon.
- For the Enlightenment/left hemisphere, opposites fight until one wins as "the Truth"; for the Romantics/right hemisphere, the *coming together* of opposites is the basis of both beauty and truth. **Hölderlin**, on Heraclitus's insight that beauty and philosophy both rest on *das Eine in sich selber unterschiedne* ("the one differentiated in itself"):
  > "[This] could occur only to a Greek, for it is the essence of beauty, and, before this was discovered, there was no philosophy."
- Trigger points exposing the left hemisphere's weakness: reason itself proclaiming its own insufficiency — a Gödel-like self-limitation. **Pascal**: "The ultimate achievement of reason is to recognize that there are an infinity of things which surpass it. It is indeed feeble if it can't get as far as understanding that." **Montaigne** similarly praised philosophy's capacity to "acknowledge her weakness, her ignorance and her inability to reach conclusions."
- Disjunction between explicit Enlightenment theory and implicit belief: **Reynolds** abandoned his own classical precepts before Michelangelo's genius; **Johnson** dismissed "the petty cavils of petty minds" before Shakespeare's greatness — his refusal of the classical unities, his mixing of comedy and tragedy, his individuals rather than "representative types" ("his story requires Romans or kings, but he thinks only on men"). **Pope** held that Shakespeare's characters were "so much Nature herself" that it was "a sort of injury to call them... Copies of her" — present, not re-presented. **Carlyle** wrote that Shakespeare's works "grow up withal unconsciously from the unknown deeps in him... as the oak-tree grows from the Earth's bosom," concluding: "Speech is great; but Silence is greater."
- The post-Enlightenment rediscovery of **Shakespeare** (in England, Germany and France) was as pivotal to Romanticism as the rediscovery of the Classical world had been to the Renaissance. In Shakespeare, tragedy arises not from a fatal flaw but from "a clash between two ways of being in the world... neither of which has to be mistaken" — tragedy as the coming together of opposites. **Maurice Morgann**'s 1777 essay is credited with anticipating the concept of the **Gestalt** nearly two centuries early.

## Body and Soul

- **Nicolas Chamfort**, Enlightenment wit, defined love as "*l'échange de deux fantaisies et le contact de deux épidermes*" ("the exchange of two fantasies and the contact of two epidermises") — yet was undone by a real love affair, and, disillusioned by the Revolution he had championed after being persecuted by the Jacobins, tried to kill himself by shooting himself in the face and stabbing his own neck.
- For Romantics, theory was not abstracted from and applied to experience but present within perception itself. **Goethe**: fact and theory, particular and universal, "are not only intimately connected, but... interpenetrate one another."
- Goethe: "we are, and ought to be, obscure to ourselves, turned outwards, and working upon the world which surrounds us" — self-knowledge comes only indirectly, through engagement with the world; objective self-scrutiny (Pope: "the proper subject of mankind is Man") subverts its own purpose by altering what it attends to.
- Philosophy's historic discomfort with embodiment: **Kant** described marriage as an agreement on the "reciprocal use of each others' sexual organs" and died probably a virgin. **Descartes**'s mock-clinical account of laughter — blood swelling the lungs, forcing air through the windpipe to make "an inarticulate, explosive sound" — is, McGilchrist notes, "a complete work of fantasy" delivered with total authority. **Voltaire**, asked if he had ever laughed, replied: "*je n'ai jamais fait ha! ha!*"
- Correctives from within the tradition: **Spinoza** — "the more capable the body is of being affected in many ways... the more capable of thinking is the mind"; **Wittgenstein** — "the human body is the best picture of the human soul"; **Diderot** — "*il y a un peu de testicule au fond de nos sentiments les plus sublimes et de notre tendresse la plus épurée*" ("there is a bit of testicle at the bottom of our most sublime feelings").
- **Blake**: "O Human Imagination, O Divine Body." **Wordsworth** on poetry versus "bald & naked" philosophy: no system of moral philosophy can "incorporate itself with the blood & vital juices of our minds." In the Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* he rejected the "mechanical device" of Augustan personification, wanting poetry to keep the reader "in the company of flesh and blood": "the same human blood circulates through the veins of" poetry and prose alike.
- Contrast with **Coleridge**, whose dinner guests fell asleep as he theorised endlessly about "sum-m-mject" and "om-m-mject" without ever transcending the polarity — whereas Wordsworth *embodied* the union of subject and object in the very movement of his verse, at times mistaking the "breathings" of Nature for the panting of his dog.
- **Eichendorff**: "Romanticism was the nostalgia of Protestants for the Catholic tradition" — read by McGilchrist as a move to redress hemispheric imbalance and curtail the dominion of the left hemisphere (associated with the conscious will and self-awareness). This creates a paradox: how to *self-consciously* seek (relative) unselfconsciousness — the topic of **Kleist**'s essay "On the Puppet Theatre."
- Childhood and the right hemisphere: personal/emotive and autobiographical memory are preferentially right-hemisphere; the right hemisphere is preponderant in early language development (hand gestures produced in right-hemisphere speech areas are abandoned as language shifts left); it is more advanced until the **second year of life** and more active up to **age four**; intelligence across cognitive faculties is linked principally to right-hemisphere function. Childhood offers the pre-conceptual immediacy of experience — "the glory and the freshness of a dream" (Wordsworth).
- Because there is no simple "fact of the matter" independent of one's disposition toward it, Romanticism's *contradictory* definitions (Berlin's puzzle) make sense: emphasis falls on the spirit in which something is done, not the thing itself. This is also why the French Revolution, fought for reason, order, justice and liberty, produced the opposite: the left hemisphere reifies concepts (statuesque Reason, Justice, Liberty) while neglecting how they are actualised — "ends come to justify means."
- Goethe: "My thinking is not separate from objects... Every new object, clearly seen, opens up a new organ of perception in us." Understanding is not discursive explanation but a moment of connection, an *aperçu*; "all seeing is 'seeing as.'" On the Enlightenment demand for theory-free empirical observation:
  > "This demand seems odd because it is useless simply to look at something. Every act of looking turns into observation, every act of observation into reflection, every act of reflection into the making of associations; thus it is evident that we theorise every time we look carefully at the world."
- Goethe distinguished *Vernunft* (reason, concerned with what is becoming) from *Verstand* (rationality, concerned with what has already become): "[Reason] rejoices in whatever evolves; [rationality] wants to hold everything still, so that it can utilise it." Reality, for Goethe and the Romantics, was never finished: "The phenomenon must never be thought of as finished or complete... but rather as evolving, growing."

## Depth

- The era's great landscape painter was **Claude Lorrain**, whose vision — in contrast to his more Cartesian contemporary **Poussin** — prefigured Romanticism: "a sort of high road of the right hemisphere which the Enlightenment left untouched." **Constable** called him "the most perfect landscape painter the world ever saw"; **Turner** idolised him; **Keats** was inspired by *The Enchanted Castle*.
- Claude's true subject is spatial and temporal *depth* itself, not the small figures nominally depicted, conveyed through deep perspective, angled foreground buildings, and light that suggests successive distances.
- Light in Romantic art and poetry is characteristically **transitional** — the half-light of dawn or dusk, not the "all-revealing" light of enlightenment: Collins's *Ode to Evening*, Gray's *Elegy*, Bowles's sonnets, Young's *Night Thoughts*, Keats's *Ode to Autumn*, Shelley's West Wind. Half-light and transitional states have affinities with the **right hemisphere** (complexity, transience, dream states, the implicit, the unconscious) as against the **left hemisphere**'s clarity, fixity, detachment and full consciousness.
- Depth is simultaneously the means of a felt relationship with something remote *and* incontestable evidence of separation from it — "the primal condition of the Romantics." Space often serves as a metaphor for time, as in Gray's "Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College" and Wordsworth's retrospective vantage in "Tintern Abbey" and in *The Prelude* Book VIII (the Helvellyn fair scene) — an elevated view that signifies heightened self-consciousness and permanent separation from unselfconscious rustic pleasure, even while appreciating it.
- The Romantics' **"pleasurable melancholy"** is not masochistic self-indulgence in pain, a misreading that arises from "either/or" thinking plus sequential causal analysis (assuming one emotion must give rise to the other); rather, pleasure and pain are caused *at the same moment by the very same phenomenon*.
- The **sublime**: ten years before Burke's famous *Enquiry*, **John Baillie** wrote that "every Person upon seeing a grand Object is affected with something which as it were extends his very Being, and expands it to a kind of Immensity" — the sublime *expands*, rather than dwarfs, the beholder. The same depth that unites (expansion of soul) is also evidence of separation (awareness of smallness) — the dual structure of awe. Reverence, so understood, is "no abasement... but with as much truth an exaltation."
- **Berlin** on "depth" as the Romantics' central discovery: not a formula but **inexhaustibility, unembraceability** — "no matter how long I speak, new chasms open... I always have to leave three dots at the end."
- A continuous visual lineage runs from Lafréri's mid-16th-century *Speculum Romanae Magnificentiae* through Claude, then to Piranesi's *Vedute di Roma*, Barbault, the *Carceri d'Invenzione*, Ducros (late 18th century), and on to Romantic landscapists Richard Wilson and Thomas Cole.
- It was **Romanticism, not the Enlightenment**, that revealed the beauty and power of light — Constable's cloud studies, Turner's "shimmering, deliquescent landscapes" (later studied by the Impressionists) — yet these are not proto-abstraction: abstraction is by definition disengaged from the world, whereas "light, like depth and texture, exists only in experience, not in the realm of thought" (illustrated by Bierstadt's *Conflagration*).
- Both the Renaissance and Romanticism, periods of right-hemisphere "release," share a fascination with the long view and the high view — because affective engagement with unique individuals/things forces a confrontation with separation and loss, expressed through distance in space and time. Prefigured in **Donne**'s image of the lovers' souls as compasses, "gold to ayery thinnesse beate," fusing separation with connection even in death.

## Melancholy and Longing

- Both Renaissance and Romanticism are captivated by the past (including the classical past — Shelley's *Ozymandias*), against the Enlightenment's orientation to the future; the elegy for lost youth recurs in both eras (Ralegh, Tichborne's "Elegy").
- Melancholy in both periods is emphasised as **uncaused** — not reducible to specific circumstance: Heine's *Die Lorelei* ("*Ich weiß nicht, was soll es bedeuten...*"), Lermontov, and Tennyson's "Tears, idle tears... Tears from the depth of some divine despair" — which Tennyson himself clarified was an expression not of woe but of **longing**, tied to distance ("it is the distance that charms me in the landscape, the picture and the past").
- Central conceptual distinction — **wanting** vs. **longing**:
  | Wanting (left hemisphere) | Longing (right hemisphere) |
  |---|---|
  | impulsion "from behind" | attraction "from in front" |
  | isolated, inert target | "division within union" |
  | hydraulic/mechanical force (Freud's drives) | magnetic field/electric attraction (Jung's archetypes) |
  | unidirectional | bidirectional, "betweenness" |
  | linear | holistic, field-shaped |
  | clear view of its target | intuits its "Other" |
  | simple, unmixed | full of mixed emotions |
- Typical objects of longing: home (Eichendorff's poem, set by Schumann), the loved one (Heine, Goethe's "*Nur wer die Sehnsucht kennt*"), *das Ewig-Weibliche* ("the eternal feminine," Goethe), the warm South (Goethe's "*Kennst du das Land, wo die Zitronen blühn?*"), childhood or the past (Wordsworth to Hardy) — ultimately longing for something nameless: Shelley's "devotion to something afar / From the sphere of our sorrow."
- Nostalgia, though the *word* is 18th-century, is ancient: Aristophanes' myth of the divided creature in Plato's *Symposium*; Saul Bellow's Ravelstein ("every soul was looking for its peculiar other, longing for its complement"); and it is the core emotion of Japanese poetry. **Donald Keene**: "Nostalgia for the past is a key to the understanding of Japanese poetry," with over a thousand years of seasonal poems, moods "more often bittersweet than either tragic or joyous," and unrequited love as a recurring theme (as in the 10th-century *Kokinshū* anthology).
- McGilchrist takes this cross-cultural convergence to mean that "many of the features of Romanticism are in fact potentially universals, part of the structure of the human mind and brain," not a culture-bound syndrome — in other cultures, the "Romantic" viewpoint is the natural default, and left-hemisphere rationalistic materialism is the culture-bound outlier.
- A predilection for the **partially perceived** — mist, haze, twilight, unfinished sketches, distant music — mirrors the finding (Chapter 2) that a right-hemisphere superiority emerges whenever an image is fleeting or degraded. Romanticism can be seen as "the wooing... of the world as delivered by the right hemisphere."
- Wordsworth: we "half create" and half perceive the world — completing the fragmentary impression through imagination without it being either wholly given or wholly invented, "something evermore about to be." The sublime, similarly, is "more truly present when only partially visible than when explicit."

## The Problem of Clarity and Explicitness

- Daylight/full consciousness has affinities with the left hemisphere's explicit processes; **Diderot** praised Richardson's novels for how they "light the depths of the cavern with his torch" — exploring the implicit, affective, unconscious mind. Wisdom via indirect/hidden paths: Homer made night the time of the entire creative process; Hegel's owl of Minerva "flew only at dusk"; Heidegger was "an indefatigable walker in unlit places"; De Quincey walked at night through gaslit London and Edinburgh and moonlit Dartmoor — in contrast to clockwork daytime walkers like Kant and Bentham, or Fontenelle, who avoided walking altogether. Wordsworth and Coleridge's night walks in the Quantock hills led the Duke of Portland's government agent to place them under surveillance in **1797** (mishearing Coleridge's reference to "Spy Nozy" — Spinoza).
- Neurobiologist **Semir Zeki**: "Vision... just happens to be the most efficient mechanism for acquiring knowledge" — but that very efficiency made it the Enlightenment's instrument and therefore suspect to Romantics. **Herder**'s *Sculpture* argues that sculptural truth is tactile, not visual: sight reduces the living form to a "pitiful polygon," a two-dimensional diagram, even across "a thousand viewpoints"; only when "the viewer's eye becomes his hand" is the fate avoided.
- **Goethe**'s *Römische Elegien* renders this synaesthesia erotically:
  > "*Sehe mit fühlendem Aug', fühle mit sehender Hand*" ("see with a feeling eye, feel with a seeing hand").
- **Winckelmann**'s rapturous, quasi-erotic response to the *Apollo Belvedere* — invoking the myth of **Pygmalion** (the statue that came alive) — is reciprocal: he animates the statue, and the statue restores him to a "renewed sense of life." **Hegel** praised Winckelmann for opening "a new medium and whole new way of looking at things for the human spirit." McGilchrist notes, via **Hall**, that as Enlightenment thinking recedes there is a renewed emphasis on the *left side* of the body — as in Winckelmann's famous description of the Laocoön's left side as where he "suffers most intensely" and "a wonder of art."
- Herder's and Winckelmann's aesthetic values — unbroken continuity, never-resting evolution, depth/volume/curvature, *Einfühlung* (empathy, "feeling in") mediated by the hand rather than the detached eye — belong to the right hemisphere's phenomenological world, and apply beyond sculpture to Homer, Milton, and J. S. Bach.
- Wordsworth named this danger "the tyranny of the eye," "the most despotic of our senses" — the loss of what Heidegger calls authenticity, when the eye substitutes a "planar re-presentation" for the depth of reality, reducing the sublime to the merely picturesque. Art requires a balance McGilchrist calls **"semi-transparency"** — between the facticity of the medium and what is seen through it. **Delacroix**: a painting's "music" — its color, light and shadow — can be felt "before you even know what the picture represents," rapt "at too great a distance to know what it represents."
- The same principle extends to science: Goethe warned against reducing observation to concept and treating words as objects — "how difficult it is to refrain from replacing the thing with its sign."
- Language, a left-hemisphere function, tends (per **Nietzsche**) to "make the uncommon common"; poetry, via metaphor and connotation, reverses this. **Shelley**: poetry "lifts the veil from the hidden beauty of the world... creates anew the universe." Wordsworth: "the hiding-places of my power / Seem open; I approach, and then they close" — the right hemisphere must use subterfuge. **Berlin**, on the artist's struggle: "I wish to convey something immaterial and I have to use material means for it... I know in advance that I shall not succeed... all I can do is to get nearer and nearer in some asymptotic approach."
- Distinguishes **fantasy** (producing novelty to replace an exhausted, over-familiar thing — a left-hemisphere response to the inauthentic) from **imagination** (clearing away the deadening effect of over-familiarity to see the thing itself anew — a right-hemisphere response): Steiner's aphorism, "**originality is antithetical to novelty**."

## Wordsworth and the Redemptive Power of Nature

- Wordsworth's distinctive use of prepositions, conjunctions, and double negatives conveys **"betweenness"** and lets something emerge from "an almost luminous absence or emptiness" — poetic counterparts, McGilchrist suggests, to what Heidegger laboured to express in discursive prose.
- *The Prelude* is an extended act of retrospection that is simultaneously about *healing* loss: memory matures from something inert into "**spots of time**... Which with distinct pre-eminence retain / A vivifying Virtue," by which "our minds / Are nourished and invisibly repair'd." This restorative bond with nature is linked to the psychoanalytic "maternal comforting substrate" — itself right-hemisphere mediated.
- Illustrative "spot of time": age five, lost on the fells near a murderer's gibbet, Wordsworth then sees an ordinary scene — a pool, a beacon, a girl carrying a pitcher against the wind — invested with "visionary dreariness." Reflecting later: "the hiding-places of my power / Seem open; I approach, and then they close."
- The visionary power is unwilled, unpredictable, and fades with age; it requires Wordsworth to be *positively looking away* — illustrated by the crag-climbing and birds-nesting passage, the "Winander boy" listening for owls, and De Quincey's account of Wordsworth lying on the road listening for the mail-coach when a star strikes him "with a pathos and a sense of the infinite." Book XII of *The Prelude* states inspiration needs both effort (the mind "aspires, grasps, struggles, wishes, craves") and a receptive stillness that "fits him to receive it, when unsought."
- McGilchrist's hemispheric reading: narrow focused attention is the left hemisphere's province, intensified by stress/fear; while the left hemisphere is absorbed in its "quarry," the right hemisphere is freed to see the scene afresh, unfiltered by the left hemisphere's habitual, over-familiar "picturesque" overlay. Effortful attention is necessary but must yield to an **"active passivity."**
- Further clues for right-hemisphere mediation: guilt and awe (tied to the religious sense) pervading the "spots of time"; a sense of "ultimate meaning" resembling right-temporal-lobe seizures; large visual masses/forms; and the strong association with childhood.
- Poetry, via metaphor and implicit meaning, is Wordsworth's answer for recapturing the numinous in adulthood — though inherently paradoxical, since unselfconsciousness cannot be consciously reproduced. The "Tintern Abbey" ode's repeated "again," and the wandering-and-returning movement of its verse (mirroring the river Wye), evoke "change within unchangingness" — echoing Donne's compasses.

## Self-Consciousness and Representation

- Wordsworth's lack of self-consciousness enabled both his greatest and his worst lines — a quality shared with **Blake** and **Keats** (and later Hopkins and Hardy), described by John Bayley (of Keats) and taken up by Christopher Ricks (*Keats and Embarrassment*) as an **"unmisgiving"** quality.
- Blake's titles — *Songs of Innocence and Experience*, *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* — allude to the right hemisphere's world where opposites are not "in opposition"; yet his poetry dramatises a real battle between **Ratio** (Newton's God — the left hemisphere) and creative **imagination** (Milton's God), a persisting opposition because a tolerant (right-hemisphere) order cannot secure the cooperation of an intolerant power seeking to undermine it.
- Blake, "There is No Natural Religion" (with McGilchrist's hemispheric gloss): without the "Poetic or Prophetic Character," the "Philosophic & Experimental" would just "repeat the same dull round" [the left hemisphere alone]; "He who sees the Infinite... sees God. He who sees the Ratio only sees himself only" [the left hemisphere is self-reflexive]; "Therefore God becomes as we are, that we may be as he is" [imagination as the bridge to the divine].
- Blake believed Milton's spirit had entered him through the instep of his left foot — "gaining literally direct access to the right hemisphere," an event he illustrated (Plate 11).
- Summary catalogue of Romanticism's right-hemisphere affinities: individual over general; unique over typical (**"typical"** being the true meaning of "Classical"); the **"thisness"** of things (*ultima realitas entis*) over their "whatness"; whole over aggregate-of-parts; metaphor over simile; indirect over literal expression; the body and senses; personal over impersonal; the "life of the mind" tied to the divine (Blake: "all living things are holy"); involvement over disinterested impartiality; betweenness over alienated distance; melancholy over optimism; and the provisional, uncertain, evolving, implicit, dark, unknowable over the final, fixed, evident, clear, known.
- As the 19th century wears on the picture becomes mixed and transitional — an "inspired" Tennyson versus the "Tennyson of fairyland" — with painters succumbing to fancy and academicism faster than poets.
- **Gerard Manley Hopkins** — priest, depressive — shows near-total right-hemisphere predominance. He coined **inscape** (a thing's unique quality, following Duns Scotus's *haeccitas*, "thisness") and **instress** (the sustaining energy, "something akin to authentic Dasein"):
  > "Each mortal thing does one thing and the same: / Deals out that being indoors each one dwells; / Selves—goes itself; myself it speaks and spells, / Crying Whát I do is me: for that I came."
  He also described an incommunicable "taste of myself... more distinctive than the taste of ale or alum," was a hyper-attentive observer of natural minutiae (moonlight, raindrops, cloud-shadows), loved wildness ("What would the world be, once bereft / Of wet and wildness?"), trusted intuitive leaps over rational method ("one cannot reach Parnassus except by flying thither"), and received several of his greatest sonnets as unbidden inspiration.
- **Wordsworth**'s **"wise passiveness"** — inspiration cannot be controlled or relied upon (Shelley: "the mind in creation is a fading coal") — sat badly with the confident, results-oriented spirit of the **Industrial Revolution** and the Protestant ethic. The response was **"the Imaginative"**: a self-conscious, willed substitute for true receptive imagination, visible in Victorian medievalising and in the Pre-Raphaelites' obsessive attention to detail at the expense of the whole (the left hemisphere re-colonising right-hemisphere territory), reaching an "apotheosis" in the schizophrenic **Richard Dadd**'s hyper-detailed paintings. **Henry James** (via Peter Conrad) judged *Middlemarch* "a treasure house of detail, but... an indifferent whole" — a broader Victorian symptom.

## The Second Reformation

- The German idealist philosophers of the Romantic era sought to combine reason with imagination, system with individuality, consistency with contradiction — and were enthusiastically active in science. **Goethe**'s **1784** discovery of the vestigial **intermaxillary bone** in the human foetal skull (present in apes) convinced him, well before Darwin, that all living forms were related and evolved from a common stem — work he valued above his own poetry.
- A historian of the period, echoing Nietzsche on Apollo and Dionysus: "even rationality cannot get by without imagination, but neither can imagination without rationality. The marriage of the two is, however, of such a peculiar kind, that they carry on a life and death struggle, and yet it is only together that they are able to accomplish their greatest feats."
- This marriage did not last: mid-19th-century Germany saw a **"second Reformation"** in **scientific materialism**, led by young Hegelians including **Ludwig Feuerbach** — explicitly rooted, by their own acknowledgment, in the original Reformation's earlier shift from metaphor/incarnation toward literalism. Rather than Hegel's unity of spirit *and* matter, materialists accepted only matter — replacing the Reformation's "Word" with "Matter" — another collapse into **"either/or"** thinking, abandoning the *coniunctio oppositorum*.
- Its deeper roots lie in French Enlightenment positivism: **Auguste Comte** held science the sole source of genuine knowledge, and proposed a three-stage law of societal development — theological, then metaphysical, then "positive"/scientific. The analytic strategies of mechanics were extended to society itself, reducing it to an aggregate of individual units — "not a society in fact, but the prototype of the 'masses'" — a violation of Aristotle's warning that what is rational for the geometer need not be rational for the physician or the politician; "the left hemisphere does not respect context."
- **Karl Vogt** claimed thought was a bodily "secretion" alterable "like other bodily secretions" by diet: "since belief is only a property of the body's atoms, a change in beliefs depends only on the way in which the atoms of the body are substituted" — a claim, McGilchrist notes, that self-undermines (it applies equally to the belief in materialism).
- Denial of the divine paired paradoxically with self-elevation: **Ludwig Büchner**, emerging from personal crisis: "No longer do I acknowledge any human authority over me." Yet materialists still needed a superhuman authority, which became **Science** itself. **Lyon Playfair** (1853): "science is a religion and its philosophers are the priests of nature"; **T. H. Huxley** called his own lectures "lay sermons." Both scientific materialism and the dialectical materialism of **Marx** and **Engels** share this root, against the backdrop of the **1848** European revolutions.
- **Marx** praised **Prometheus** — opponent of "all divine and earthly Gods" — as "the most eminent saint and martyr in the philosophic calendar." McGilchrist draws an uncomfortable parallel: **Hitler** later described the Aryan as "the Prometheus of mankind... kindling anew that fire of knowledge." By contrast, the ancient world (per **Kerényi**) held that "vulnerability was an attribute of the gods" — an admission the "Promethean left hemisphere" cannot make.
- Two dangerous consequences follow from the left hemisphere's disregard for context: (1) scientism applied indiscriminately across every field of human experience, ignoring Aristotle's insight that different domains require different kinds of rationality; (2) science exempting *itself* from the same historicising critique it used to undermine Christianity — what **Dewey** called "the dogma of immaculate conception of philosophical systems."
- The resulting **"mythos of science,"** fully formed by the mid-19th century: the myth of the **unity of science** (one logical path to knowledge, versus Gaukroger's actual "loose grouping of disciplines with different subject matters and different methods"); the myth of the **sovereignty of the scientific method** (versus the real role of chance, individual obsession and intuition — **Emil DuBois-Reymond**'s declaration *ignorabimus*, "we shall never know," and the indignant reaction it provoked); the myth of science as simultaneously above morality and its sole foundation; and the myth of science's brave stand against dogma.

## The Industrial Revolution

- McGilchrist frames the Industrial Revolution as "the left hemisphere's most audacious assault yet on the world of the right hemisphere" — of "the profoundest consequence" for the book, underwriting the character of the modern world (the subject of the book's final chapters).
- Left-hemisphere advances characteristically arrive as absolute, intolerant, and sweeping — the Reformation, the Cromwellian Revolution, the French Revolution, scientific materialism — and the Industrial Revolution, "slicing its way through the landscape and sweeping away cultural history," fits this pattern, but "goes beyond even that."
- The crucial move: if the right hemisphere normally delivers **"the Other"** — the realm of things that exist prior to and apart from our concepts of them — the Industrial Revolution let the left hemisphere **externalise and concretise its own workings**, so that the world of "actually existing things" increasingly consisted of the left hemisphere's own projections. This would outflank the right hemisphere's access to genuine otherness, trapping it in **"a hall of mirrors."**
- Mechanical mass production creates objects that are not merely approximately alike (as natural or hand-made things are) but **truly identical and interchangeable**, free of the "imperfections" of "living hands" — replacing natural variation with invariant, left-hemisphere-preferred forms: the circle, the straight line, the rectangle, the cube, the cylinder. **Delacroix** wondered whether straight lines exist only in our brains; **Leonard Shlain** observed that straight lines exist nowhere in nature except at the horizon. Machines that make machines become "self-propagating parodies of life."
- McGilchrist argues this aim has been substantially realised in a remarkably short time: rectilinear urban grids increasingly stripped of anything speaking of the natural world; a growing, increasingly isolated urban population; an assault on nature through exploitation, pollution, and subtler "management"; and an increase in the "virtuality" of both work and leisure (television, the internet).
- **Heisenberg**, writing in the 1950s, observed that technology no longer appears "as the product of a conscious human effort to enlarge material power, but rather like a biological development of mankind in which the innate structures of the human organism are transplanted in an ever-increasing measure into the environment of man" — a passage McGilchrist says he "could hardly believe" on first encountering it, since it precisely matches his own claim that the left hemisphere's innate structures are being incarnated in the world it has come to dominate.
- Even so, this leaves possible "exits from the maze" unbarred — through embodiment, art, and religion, the right hemisphere might still make a comeback. This sets up the book's turn, in the chapters that follow, to the 20th-century world of ideas and the **"asymmetry of interaction"** by which the swings between the hemispheres become ever more violent and tip toward "a possibly final triumph of the left-hemisphere world."
