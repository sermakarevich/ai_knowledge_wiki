# Chapter 3: Language, Truth and Music

This chapter dismantles the popular assumption that language and reason belong to the left hemisphere (LH) while music and emotion belong to the right (RH). McGilchrist first reframes the Goldberg–Costa finding that novelty engages the RH and familiarity the LH as evidence of two fundamentally different *kinds of knowing* — the encountering, personal *kennen* of the RH versus the fixed, factual *wissen* of the LH — rather than a mere division of informational labour. He then argues, from fossil, anatomical, developmental and clinical evidence, that neither language nor the hand-grasp asymmetry that accompanies it can explain why the hemispheres diverged in the first place, since apes already show the same left-sided asymmetry without language. Tracing language back to a musical, gestural, embodied origin — in which music, not language, did the work of communication, bonding and even much of "thought" for hundreds of thousands of years — he shows that referential, denotative language is a comparatively late "hijack" of resources originally in the service of manipulation and grasp. The chapter closes by identifying metaphor as the thread that keeps language tethered to lived, bodily experience, and by confronting the "left-hemisphere chauvinism" of neuroscience itself, which has long treated the right hemisphere, wrongly, as the dim, mute junior partner.

## The New and the Familiar, and Two Kinds of Knowing

Goldberg and Costa's finding (introduced earlier, pp. 40 and 75) is that new experience of any kind — music, words, real objects, imaginary constructs — engages the RH; as it becomes familiar or routine, the RH disengages and the "information" becomes the concern of the LH alone. Rather than reading this only as a novelty/familiarity processing split, McGilchrist proposes it reflects two whole ways of knowing.

The first, *kennen* (Latin *cognoscere*, French *connaître*, German *kennen*), is knowledge as **encounter** — with someone or something "other." It is personal ("carnal knowledge"), permits a sense of the other's uniqueness, can't be captured by listing parts ("quick-tempered," "lively"), depends on betweenness, and is never final. The second, *wissen* (Latin *sapere*, French *savoir*, German *wissen*), is knowledge of **facts** — "born on 16 September 1964," "lives in New York," "5ft 4in tall," "red hair," "freckles" — general, impersonal, fixed, certain, repeatable, and disengaged; it reads like a police database or a description of "chest of drawers, two single over three double, bun feet, circa 1870."

Music is McGilchrist's key example of a non-living thing that nonetheless demands *kennen*: research shows we attribute human qualities (age, sex, personality, feelings) to music and approach understanding it much as we approach knowing a person. Music does not *symbolise* emotional meaning (which would require interpretation) — it *metaphorises* it, "carries it over" direct to the unconscious. Aristotle's comparison of tragedy to an organic being is cited as an ancient version of the same intuition.

McGilchrist links this to Jung's dictum that "all cognition is akin to recognition" — we come to *cognise* (*wissen*) only by *recognising* (*erkennen*) something already *kennen*-known — and to Nietzsche: "it is through 'knowing' [*erkennen*, re-cognition] that we come to have the feeling that we already know [*wissen*] something; thus it means combating a feeling of newness and transforming the apparently new into something old." Gregory Bateson's principle that all knowledge is knowledge of *distinction* — of something other than the self — underlies both: experience requires difference, and understanding of anything depends on the metaphor/model we choose to compare it with (a football match compared to a trip to the betting shop, versus to a church service, reveals different aspects). "Our first leap determines where we land." The machine model is "the only one the left hemisphere likes," since machines are built from inert, context-independent parts. McGilchrist proposes instead treating each hemisphere as having a "disposition" or "stance" toward the world, noting that split-brain hemispheres each display a distinct personality (citing a researcher closely involved in such studies), and quoting Freud on the unconscious as "a particular realm of the mind with its own wishful impulses, its own mode of expression and its peculiar mental mechanisms which are not in force elsewhere."

## What Language Tells Us About the Hemispheres

The left parietal asymmetry associated with language turns out not to be explained by language at all. Fossil records of early humans show the same brain asymmetry long before language is believed to have developed, and great apes and baboons — which clearly have no language — already show the equivalent left-sided enlargement: the planum temporale is larger on the left in orang-utans, gorillas and chimpanzees, and Yakovlevian torque is present in fossil humans and great apes alike.

Language itself is not seated in one hemisphere: syntax and vocabulary are mostly in the LH, but the RH subserves the *higher* linguistic functions — understanding a whole phrase or sentence in context, tone, emotional significance, humour, irony, metaphor. "If it is the right hemisphere that, in linguistic terms, paints the picture, it is still the left hemisphere that holds the 'paint box'" — hence the old, misleading idea of LH "dominance."

Handedness doesn't explain the asymmetry either: ambidexterity would confer more skill than one-sidedness, and some higher apes already prefer the right hand despite not being tool-makers. Most strikingly, genetic research shows the left-hemisphere "expansion" is really a deliberate *inhibition* of growth in the right hemisphere — specific genes suppress the corresponding right-hemisphere region. The researcher who identified them concludes: "It is safe to assume that the asymmetry that ended up leading to language is unlikely to have appeared because of language … It is likely to have appeared for some other reason and basically got co-opted by language."

## The Origins of Language

*Homo heidelbergensis* — common ancestor of *homo sapiens* and *homo neanderthalensis* — had a large brain and vocal apparatus comparable to modern humans as long as 300,000–400,000 years ago, possibly half a million. Yet sophisticated symbol manipulation (language proper) is not thought to have arisen until as recently as 40,000 years ago, and at any rate not before roughly 80,000 years ago, when cultural artefacts, visual representation and ritual burial suddenly proliferate.

The puzzle is resolved by a clever anatomical inference: the size of the anterior condylar canal (housing the hypoglossal nerve, which supplies the tongue) and the thoracic vertebral canal (supplying respiratory muscles) indicate the degree of vocal and respiratory control an individual possessed. Apes and monkeys have much smaller canals than modern humans — but the earliest human skeletons, from long before language, already show canal sizes almost indistinguishable from ours. The likely explanation: this sophisticated vocal control evolved for a non-verbal "language" of intonation and phrasing without words — in other words, **music**.

Music and language share architecture: both are built from intonational phrases governed by a kind of "syntax" (music's syntax concerns the shape of a whole piece, over minutes or, as in Wagner, hours), both use the phrase as the basic structural unit, and both rely on melody and rhythm for expressiveness. Three positions exist on their relationship: music as a useless spin-off of language; language as having developed out of musical communication; or the two developing together from a common ancestor dubbed "musilanguage" — which McGilchrist finds hard to distinguish from the "music came first" view, since a "musilanguage" would necessarily rely on prosody, pitch, intonation, volume, rhythm and phrasing — the musical aspects of language.

## Language or Music: Which Came First?

Several lines of evidence favour music's priority. Music's "syntax" is simpler and less evolved, suggesting an earlier origin. Child development confirms the pattern: intonation, phrasing and rhythm develop before syntax and vocabulary. Newborns prefer "infant-directed speech" (baby talk), which emphasises prosody, and mothers respond by broadening pitch range, raising pitch and slowing tempo. Newborns distinguish their mother's voice timbre and intonation, and their "mother tongue's" intonation, from others — capacities that rely on RH holistic pattern discrimination, not LH analytic processing. Invoking (with caveats about its reliability) the principle that "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny," McGilchrist cites Salomon Henschen: "The musical faculty is phylogenetically older than language; some animals have a musical faculty—birds in a high degree. It is also ontogenetically older, for the child begins to sing earlier than to speak."

Music, on this view, is fundamentally **the communication of emotion** — "our love of music reflects the ancestral ability of our mammalian brain to transmit and receive basic emotional sounds"; "music was built upon the prosodic mechanisms of the right hemisphere that allow us affective emotional communications through vocal intonations." Anthropologist Robin Dunbar: "the deeply emotional stirrings generated by music suggest to me that music has very ancient origins, long predating the evolution of language."

Geneticists resist this because music seems to confer no obvious competitive advantage; Steven Pinker treats music as a meaningless "exaptation" of language, comparable to pornography or a taste for fatty food. McGilchrist counters that the evidence runs the other way — if anything, language evolved from music, not vice versa. He notes that Rousseau, von Humboldt and Jespersen (across three centuries) already held that language derived from music, and cites the "singing Neanderthals" theory (Mithen). He argues that Western culture has simply lost music's historically central, communal, participatory role, quoting Oliver Sacks on the "actual binding of nervous systems" that occurs in collective musical performance.

If music precedes language, it explains why poetry evolved before prose: prose was originally called *pezos logos* ("pedestrian, walking logos"), as against the "dancing logos" of poetry, and early poetry was sung. The proposed sequence: right-hemisphere music (sung words) → right-hemisphere language (metaphorical poetry) → left-hemisphere language (referential prose).

## Communication Without Language

McGilchrist estimates that over 90 per cent of human communication is non-verbal, through body language and especially intonation — a fact obscured by our language-saturated culture. Animals manage rich communication without language: dogs use an intonation-based "musilanguage" with humans; whales, dolphins and killer whales coordinate complex social life, including coordinated attacks, through pitch, intonation and temporal pattern — "music." The Pirahã, an Amazonian hunter-gatherer tribe, speak a language so tonal that they can "dispense with their vowels and consonants altogether and sing, hum or whistle conversations."

One theory holds that singing arose as "grooming at a distance": once human social groups grew too large for one-on-one physical grooming to sustain bonding, musical vocalisation took its place, with referential language a later evolutionary add-on. Because language, unlike music, is the perfect medium for *concealing* rather than revealing meaning, McGilchrist doubts it was ever primarily needed for communication: music has an "I–thou" existence — it speaks *to* us — while language enables the vastly expanded "I–it" (or "we–it") relationship of referring to things not present.

## Thought Without Language

McGilchrist argues the belief that thought requires language is an introspective illusion — we only notice the (relatively rare) instances of explicit verbal thinking. Famous examples of non-verbal discovery: Kekulé seeing the benzene ring in the image of a snake biting its tail; Poincaré solving Fuchsian functions after black coffee ("ideas rose in crowds – I felt them collide until pairs interlocked") and their link to non-Euclidean geometry occurring to him as he boarded a bus; Mendeleyev dreaming the periodic table's structure; Einstein's statement that "the words or the language, as they are written or spoken, do not seem to play any role in the mechanism of my thought." Rudolf Arnheim (*Visual Thinking*, 1969): "perceptual and pictorial shapes are not only translations of thought products but the very flesh and blood of thinking itself."

Concept formation and categorisation clearly predate language, evolutionarily: pigeons can categorise leaves, fish, faces, and even discriminate a Monet from a Picasso (generalising Monet→Renoir and Picasso→Braque) or Bach from Hindemith or Stravinsky; carp can distinguish blues from classical music (Muddy Waters from the Trout Quintet). Irene Pepperberg's parrot Alex could communicate, plan and reason. "Theory of mind" — attributing mental states to others — survives loss of language in aphasics and appears to some degree in chimpanzees, so it cannot depend on language either.

Jacques Lordat, a professor of physiology who documented his own 1843 aphasic episode, wrote: "when I wanted to speak I could not find the expressions that I needed … the thought was all ready, but the sounds that had to express it as intermediary were no longer at my disposition … I was unable to accept … the theory that verbal signs are necessary, even indispensable for thought." Stroke patients who lose syntax retain complex reasoning and calculation ability; patients with semantic dementia can still calculate, sometimes exceptionally well.

Cross-cultural number research reinforces the point: the Mundurukú, an Amazonian tribe with no word for values greater than three, succeed at arithmetic tasks involving values up to 80, performing comparably whether or not they also speak Portuguese, and as well as French-speaking controls. The Oksapmin of Papua New Guinea, who count using body parts, readily generalised a "generative counting rule" to handle magnitudes far beyond available body parts once money was introduced. Colour perception is unaffected by vocabulary gaps — Quechi Indians (five colour terms) and Germans (no native word for "pink") discriminate the relevant hues fine — though words *can* subsequently reshape perceptual boundaries. The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis is judged "no more than a half or quarter truth": five-month-old infants possess a concept of tightness-of-fit that they *lose* if their native language doesn't encode it — meaning concepts can precede words but may atrophy without them. Overall: "thinking is prior to language... it shapes, rather than grounds, our thinking."

## Language and the Hand

The co-location of grasp and speech in the LH is, McGilchrist argues, not accidental. Restricting hand movement in normal subjects impairs speech fluency. Ramachandran describes a woman born without upper limbs who nonetheless experiences phantom arms that gesticulate involuntarily when she speaks. Neurophysiologically, speech production and right-hand movement share mechanisms; Marcel Kinsbourne holds that language develops from "right-sided action and, particularly, rightward orienting," an "elaboration, extension and abstraction of sensorimotor function" rooted in a proto-language of "utterances that were coincident with and driven by the same rhythm as the movement in question." Babies babble while pointing, and "the child always points while naming and does not name without pointing."

Everyday metaphor encodes this link: we "grasp" what someone is saying (Latin *comprehendere*, German *begreifen*). Géza Révész, quoted at length, notes that German *handeln* "embraces all meaningful and goal-directed human activities… If we wish to convey that we have acquired something mental, we say that we have grasped it" — citing *Erfassen, Begriff, begreiflich, Eindruck, Ausdruck*; McGilchrist adds English *grasp, comprehend, impression, expression, intend, contend, pretend* (from Latin *tendere*, to reach). Touch/grasp, Révész notes, gives only piecemeal information (never the whole) and distinguishes *type* rather than *individual* — both hallmarks of LH processing.

Imaging confirms the pattern: tool-use concepts and grasping actions activate the LH preferentially even in left-handers (dissociating the *concept* of grasp from control of the hand itself), while exploratory (non-grasping) hand motions activate the right superior parietal cortex even when using the right hand. Clinically, patients with right-hemisphere damage compulsively (but volitionally, not reflexively) grasp objects; by contrast, a patient relying on an intact right hemisphere after left-hemisphere damage, asked to copy a gesture, instead tries "to put his own hands on the examiner's... as if trying 'to find companionship in something that fills up the empty space.'"

## Language and Manipulation

Herder (1772) held that "language appears as a natural organ of reason." Norman Geschwind proposed language originated not to communicate but to *map* the world; McGilchrist extends this: language is a means of *manipulating* the world. It is poorly suited to intimate "I–thou" communication (telephone conversations, stripped of non-verbal signal, are unsatisfactory for lovers, friends, or therapy) but ideal for the "I–it" business of transmitting information about what is absent in space or time. Naming confers power — Adam names the beasts he is given dominion over — and written language further extends fixity and manipulability (the oldest surviving texts are bureaucratic records). Language, McGilchrist writes, "starts out with what look like imperial aspirations": it lets us hide truth, direct others, and act at a distance in ways non-verbal communication cannot. It gives the frontal lobes a virtual, "off-line," abstracted, disembodied, general and fixed representation of reality — at the cost of anything implicit, flexible, or unfixed, which "ceases to exist as far as the speaking hemisphere is concerned."

## Metaphor

"Language functions like money" — an intermediary that nonetheless "takes on some of the life of the things it represents"; in McGilchrist's own metaphor, "language is the money of thought." Only the RH can understand metaphor, which he calls the sole route by which understanding "can reach outside the system of signs to life itself." The word derives from Greek *meta-* (across) + *pherein* (carry). Metaphor operates at two ends: at the "top," words activate a broad, implicit net of connotations grasped as a whole (a joke, once explained, loses its power); at the "bottom," every word ultimately points back to embodied, lived experience — even "virtual" and "immaterial" trace to *vir-tus* (a man's strength) and *materia* (the feel of wood). Following Wittgenstein, this is where "one's spade reaches bedrock and is turned."

Metaphor's two terms are not merely *similar* but, at the level of experience (not language), the *same* — quoting Jean Paul (Johann Paul Friedrich Richter), *Vorschule der Ästhetik* (1804): originally "one did not compare that which showed no resemblance, but one proclaimed identities: metaphors were, as with children, necessary synonyms for body and mind." A clash of arguments and a clash of cymbals share a felt, embodied commonality prior to any abstracted comparison. Max Black: "If to call a man a wolf is to put him in a special light, we must not forget that the metaphor makes the wolf seem more human than he otherwise would." Bruno Snell, on Homer likening a warrior to a rock amid crashing waves: "man must listen to an echo of himself before he may hear or know himself."

Metaphor precedes denotation historically and developmentally — even scientific and philosophical language derives from metaphors grounded in tangible experience: "Eliminating metaphor would eliminate philosophy... Conceptual metaphor is what makes most abstract thought possible." Where the RH sees metaphor as the only way to keep language tethered to the world, the LH — echoing Locke's dismissal of metaphors as "perfect cheats" — treats it as either a lie or mere ornament, preferring the single fixed meaning that serves certainty.

## Language Rooted in the Body

Even mundane metaphors ("feeling down") derive from embodied experience, and McGilchrist argues language's origins in music and the RH keep it "deeply connected with the body." The gesture-origin and music-origin theories of language are not in conflict, since music is itself deeply gestural (dance; even stillness activates the motor cortex) — what Mithen calls "multimodal." Herder again: "We are full of such interconnections of the most different senses… in nature all the threads are one single tissue… what man sees with his eye and feels by touch can also become soundable." Against Saussure's insistence on the arbitrariness of the sign, McGilchrist cites the cross-linguistically reproducible "kiki/bouba" effect (spiky shapes paired with "kiki," rounded shapes with "bouba") as evidence that word-sounds are evocatively, synaesthetically non-arbitrary. He notes (flagging a fuller treatment in Chapter 5) that the deep structure of syntax may be founded on the fixed sequences of limb movement in running creatures, and quotes Lakoff and Johnson: "the very structure of reason itself comes from the details of our embodiment... Reason is thus not an essence that separates us from other animals; rather, it places us on a continuum with them."

This motivates a critique of Chomskyan universal grammar as reinforcing the idea of the brain as a "cognitive machine" rather than an embodied organism; McGilchrist notes it remains speculative and disputed, that world languages show very wide syntactic variety, and that children acquire language holistically through imitation rather than rule-application. He distinguishes rote copying from empathic imitation — "inhabiting" another person — illustrated by the Chuang Tzu's story of Cook Ting butchering an ox for Lord Wen-hui, and by Dreyfus and Dreyfus (*Mind Over Machine*): "an expert's skill has become so much a part of him that he need be no more aware of it than he is of his own body." He invokes Wittgenstein: "to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" — language as an extension of life, not an abstraction from it.

Rudolf Laban's account of sub-Saharan "rhythmic drum telegraphy" illustrates this inhabiting directly: listeners understand long-distance drummed messages not by decoding mimicked word-sounds but by visualising the drummer's bodily movement — "a kind of dance." Laban: "orchestral music is produced by the most precise bodily movements of the musicians," which is why we like to *see*, not just hear, performance — better to inhabit the performer's body. McGilchrist's recapitulation: language began as an embodied expression of emotion, transmitted by inhabiting another's body, acquired through imitation and mirror neurones, derived from music, itself an extension of grooming — binding a group we still call, tellingly, "a body" with "members."

He extends the group-selection argument from music to language itself: since a lone fluent speaker is useless if the group can't understand, referential language, like music, more plausibly evolved through group rather than individual selection. He also pushes back on purely utilitarian readings of human universals, siding with Robin Dunbar's own aside that dance ranks among "the most futile of all human universals" — McGilchrist calls dance, laughter and smiling "gloriously useless," and criticises Dunbar's appeal to endorphins as a reductive redescription rather than an explanation ("Sound familiar?... it is the endorphin story all over again"). Finally, the anatomical seat of the *urge* to speak is not Broca's area (whose lesion patients remain desperate to communicate) but the anterior cingulate — damage there causes akinetic mutism, a loss of the desire to speak despite intact speech machinery; dolphins and whales, noted for rich anterior cingulate development, are correspondingly highly (musically) communicative.

## The Right Frontal Expansion

Just as the left hemisphere shows an asymmetrical enlargement, so does the right — most pronounced, and most distinctively human, in the right frontal lobe (a petalia also present, to a lesser degree, in social monkeys such as macaques and in apes). Developmentally, the right hemisphere matures first in the fetus; the left overtakes it in the second year with the laying down of speech and language areas; but the right hemisphere then continues developing, with sophisticated emotional and prosodic language elements maturing in the fifth and sixth years — a right→left→right sequence McGilchrist suggests may recapitulate the phylogenetic story.

This right frontal expansion, not the left, underwrites empathy, humour and irony, and what makes language a means of reaching out to the "Other" rather than a tool of manipulation. McGilchrist argues that what truly separates humans from animals is not reason or language (both present in degree in other species — crows reason, bees have a language of sorts) but imagination, creativity, religious awe, music, dance, poetry, art, love of nature, moral sense, humour, and the capacity to change one's mind — capacities depending chiefly on the right hemisphere and right frontal lobe. Where the LH's relationship to the world is one of grasping in order to use, "the right hemisphere's appears to be one of reaching out — just that. Without purpose." The LH, unlike the RH, always has "an end in view."

## Conclusion

McGilchrist proposes two opposing but equally vital, fundamentally incompatible stances toward the world requiring separate neurological "sequestration" even before humans existed: a **centrifugal** tendency toward isolating things from context and from each other, ruled by utility and manipulation, which colonised the left hemisphere and, as frontal lobes expanded up the evolutionary tree, eventually became the seat of referential language; and a **centripetal** tendency toward connectedness and "betweenness," enhanced by growing capacity for empathy, seated in the right frontal expansion. He compares this to a broader tension, evident "at all levels," between forces toward coherence/unification and forces toward incoherence/separation — with harmony and counterpoint as models of how difference, far from being purely negative, can cohere into something greater than its parts.

He then turns to why the (more distinctively human) right hemisphere has been so neglected, documenting a long history of "left-hemisphere chauvinism" in neuroscience: R. C. Oldfield's 1966 remark about "a certain conspiracy of silence... about the lack of anything much for the right hemisphere to do"; composer Kenneth Gaburo's outsider observation of "something extraordinarily pejorative" in the literature's language; and, most starkly, Salomon Henschen's 1926 paper describing the right hemisphere's "manifest inferiority," claiming a person who loses left-temporal language function "sinks to the level of primitive man," and asking whether the right hemisphere might be "a regressing organ." McGilchrist argues Michael Gazzaniga continues this tradition, quoting him calling the disconnected right hemisphere "vastly inferior... to the cognitive skills of a chimpanzee" and saying it "knows precious little about a lot of things" — while noting Gazzaniga's own data show the right hemisphere is in fact *better* at certain attentional tasks, a result he reframes rather than accepts at face value. McGilchrist is equally dismissive of the opposite excess — anti-rational, "left hemisphere bad" pop parlance — insisting language and reason "are the children of both hemispheres, not one alone," and that "the left hemisphere is the Master's most prized counsellor, his valued emissary."

The chapter ends with evidence that LH "dominance" is itself built on suppressing the RH rather than any positive advance: Marian Annett's research finds left-handers over-represented among artists, athletes and skilled performers, and that strong right-handedness comes with the left hand's skill "declining dramatically." Anatomically, planum temporale asymmetry arises because the *right* planum is reduced, not because the left is enlarged, and of 27 genes implicated in this asymmetry, most act to suppress right-hemisphere growth. Christopher Walsh (Harvard), who led that research: "We tend to assume teleologically, because of our focus on language being that most beautiful thing, that it must be endowed by some special mechanism in the left hemisphere … in fact, it may just be normally repressed in the right hemisphere and allowed to take place in the left." McGilchrist frames the trade-off as an inverted-U: some lateralisation is necessary (strong right-handers are faster with the right hand but markedly slower with the left; unusually symmetrical brains have *two* large plana, not two small ones), but pushed too far it costs more than it gains. Atypical lateralisation (in left-handers, and in dyslexia, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, and their relatives) trades away some LH efficiency for occasional access to RH-mediated talents — his phrase, "flying mathematicians" and "inhibited trapeze artists," captures both the gains and losses. The chapter's closing claim: "left hemisphere superiority is based, not on a leap forward by the left hemisphere, but on a 'deliberate' handicapping of the right."
