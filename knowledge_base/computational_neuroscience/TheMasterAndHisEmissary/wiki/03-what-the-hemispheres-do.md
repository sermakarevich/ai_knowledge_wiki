# Chapter 2: What Do the Two Hemispheres 'Do'?

This is the longest chapter of Part One, and its task is to survey, domain by domain, the neuropsychological evidence that the hemispheres do not divide the world by content (language here, images there) but by *mode of attention* — the "how," not the "what." McGilchrist works through attention, novelty, flexibility, integration, Gestalt perception, context versus abstraction, individuals versus categories, sameness and difference, the personal, the living, empathy and theory of mind, emotion (asymmetry, receptivity, expressivity, affinity), reasoning, embodiment, meaning, music, time, depth, certainty, self-awareness, and the self — and in each domain finds the same pattern: the right hemisphere (RH) grounds experience in a broad, contextual, ever-changing, embodied, uncertain, relational encounter with what actually exists, while the left hemisphere (LH) narrows, abstracts, categorises, and re-presents a fixed, decontextualised, mechanistic model of it, convinced of its own correctness. The evidence is drawn from lesion studies, split-brain research, the Wada procedure, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), EEG, and functional imaging (fMRI, PET, SPECT), with a persistent methodological caveat: none of these techniques, alone, is trustworthy. The chapter closes by insisting that both hemispheres are indispensable — the left hemisphere's contribution is "civilisation itself" — but that the right hemisphere's grounding, integrating, world-disclosing role has been systematically underrated by a neuroscience that is itself, in McGilchrist's view, a left-hemisphere enterprise.

## The Pathways to Knowledge

McGilchrist opens with method rather than findings, warning against the "toy cupboard" model in which brain functions are assumed to be parcelled out wherever anatomical space happens to be available. The brain, he insists, is "a single, integrated, highly dynamic system" of networks, not modules — so any talk of localisation, even at the level of the hemispheres, must be read in that light. The one legitimate large-scale division, he argues, is the hemispheric one, because "nature has clearly made a division" there.

He catalogues the main investigative tools and their limits: brain lesion studies (strokes, tumours); temporary inactivation via the **Wada procedure** (injecting sodium amytal into one carotid artery to anaesthetise a hemisphere, historically used before neurosurgery to localise speech); **transcranial magnetic stimulation**; unilateral **electroconvulsive therapy** (which inactivates one hemisphere for 15–20 minutes); the **tachistoscope** (visual stimuli flashed for milliseconds, too brief for gaze redirection, to one visual field only); **dichotic listening** (different stimuli to each ear — the original evidence for a right-ear/left-hemisphere advantage with verbal material); and, most valuably, **split-brain (callosotomy) patients**, first studied by Sperry and Bogen in California in the 1950s–60s, in whom severing the corpus callosum for intractable epilepsy gives "a relatively pure picture of how one hemisphere on its own responds." He also surveys EEG (temporally precise, spatially imprecise) and functional neuroimaging — fMRI, PET, SPECT — which infer activity from blood-flow/metabolic changes.

He is emphatic about neuroimaging's limits: "one cannot assume that the areas that light up are those fundamentally responsible for the 'function' being imaged, or that areas that do not light up are not involved." Counter-intuitively, only effortful tasks reliably "light up" — people with higher IQ, or larger brains (which correlates with IQ), show *lower* cerebral metabolic rates during mental activity, and activation on a scan may in fact represent inhibition rather than engagement. Task framing, novelty, complexity, subtraction-paradigm design, sex, handedness (including the distinction between strongly and mixed lateralised handedness), race, and age all confound results. He quotes a "prominent neuroimaging specialist": "Some people believe that psychology is just being replaced by brain imaging, but I don't think that's the case at all … It's the confrontation of all these different methods that creates knowledge." His own method, accordingly, is to triangulate across multiple lines of evidence rather than rely on any single one.

Structurally, the right hemisphere is consistently longer, wider, larger and heavier than the left (true of social mammals generally), with more white matter (favouring long-range, global transfer) where the left hemisphere has more grey matter and prioritises local, within-region communication; greater dendritic overlap and interconnectivity; and different neurochemistry — the left hemisphere relies more on dopamine, the right more on noradrenaline, and the right is more sensitive to testosterone. Speech-area asymmetry in the left hemisphere is detectable from 31 weeks' gestation.

## Breadth and Flexibility versus Focus and Grasp

The chapter's central organising idea is attention. Conventional neuropsychology distinguishes five types: vigilance, sustained attention, alertness, focused attention, and divided attention, grouped into an **intensity axis** (vigilance, sustained attention, alertness) and a **selectivity axis** (focused and divided attention). The evidence is consistent: the right hemisphere underlies the entire intensity axis — right-hemisphere lesions (especially right frontal) grossly impair vigilance and sustained attention and produce "perceptuomotor slowing"; left-hemisphere lesions leave vigilance intact. Focused attention, by contrast, is more severely impaired by left-hemisphere injury and is linked to the left caudate, left anterior cingulate, left orbitofrontal cortex, and basal ganglia. Divided attention is more mixed but still primarily right-hemisphere (right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex).

McGilchrist's summary: **the right hemisphere is responsible for every type of attention except focused attention.** Because each hemisphere preferentially processes the contralateral field, and the right hemisphere dominates attention generally, most people asked to bisect a line will place the mark slightly left of centre — the right hemisphere's global view equalises the apparent length of the two halves. Right inferior parietal lobule lesions cause the worst global-attention impairment. Patients relying solely on left-hemisphere attention (after right-hemisphere damage) cannot adjust the breadth of their attentional "spotlight": they show "an excessive and more or less permanent narrowing of their attentional window."

## The New versus the Known

Because the right hemisphere alone attends to the peripheral visual field — where novelty tends to enter experience — **anything new must first register in the right hemisphere** before the left can focus on it. New stimuli trigger noradrenaline release predominantly on the right; novel experience changes activity in the right (not left) hippocampus. This extends to learning new information or skills, even verbal ones — once a skill (even playing a musical instrument) becomes familiar, its processing shifts to the left hemisphere. The left hemisphere "positively prefers what it knows," making it efficient in predictable situations but weaker whenever assumptions must be revised or old information distinguished from new. The right hemisphere's affinity for the new versus the known is phylogenetically old: horses perceive novel or emotionally arousing stimuli preferentially with the left eye (right hemisphere).

## Possibility versus Predictability

The right frontal lobe underwrites flexibility of thought and "frame shift"; damage there produces **perseveration** — a pathological inability to shift approach, so that a subject who solved one problem inappropriately reapplies the same solution to the next. In problem-solving, the right hemisphere keeps an array of possible solutions live while alternatives are explored; the left hemisphere seizes the single best-fitting solution and holds it. V. S. Ramachandran's work on **anosognosia** shows the left hemisphere denying discrepancies that don't fit its existing schema, while the right hemisphere acts "more like a devil's advocate," actively watching for anomalies.

This is not a verbal/visuospatial split: even in word-attention tasks, the left hemisphere narrows to closely related words and suppresses irrelevant meanings, while the right "processes information in a non-focal manner with widespread activation of related meanings," making distantly related or unusual words and novel uses for objects more available (right anterior temporal region; right posterior superior temporal sulcus for verbal creativity). Tellingly, the right hemisphere can also use the left's "single strong association" style, but not vice versa — a "meta"-level asymmetry in flexibility. On the **Remote Associations Test** (a standard creativity measure), relaxation of effortful, narrowing attention favours the broader right-hemisphere mode — explaining the "tip of the tongue" phenomenon: trying harder recruits narrow left-hemisphere attention and blocks recall, while relaxing lets the word surface. Creativity can actually *increase* after a left-hemisphere stroke — Alajouanine describes one painter whose work gained "numerous intellectual and affective components." But creativity ultimately needs both: the corpus callosum both separates and connects, and its division impairs creativity.

## Integration versus Division

The left hemisphere is more tightly interconnected within itself; the right has greater myelination (facilitating swift cortical/subcortical transfer) and greater overall connectivity, evidenced in EEG measures and more diffuse, overlapping somatosensory and auditory projections. This supports "heteromodal integration to an extent surpassing that possible in a focally organised hemisphere" — bringing together information from multiple senses and memory into one coherent experienced world, whereas the left hemisphere is "inadequate for the more rapid complex syntheses achieved by the [right] hemisphere." Noradrenergic neurons in the right hemisphere, uniquely, do not fatigue with continuous stimulation, holding exploratory attention open across greater expanses of space and time; the right hemisphere's longer working memory lets it hold more information, with more specificity, for longer. Together, this broader, more integrated attentional field is what makes possible "the recognition of broad or complex patterns, the perception of the 'thing as a whole', seeing the wood for the trees."

## The Hierarchy of Attention

Because novelty enters via the right hemisphere, there is a **temporal hierarchy**: any object of experience is grounded in the right hemisphere before being further processed by the left. This coexists with a hierarchy at any given moment — global (right-hemisphere) attention takes precedence over, and guides, local (left-hemisphere) attention, never the reverse. McGilchrist calls this the "**right → left → right** progression," a recurring theme of the book. The classic illustration is a large letter "H" built from small "E"s (or a "4" from "8"s): normally we see the whole first. In schizophrenia this right-hemisphere-dependent capacity is lost, and the figure collapses into "just a mass of Es and 8s" — one of the core attentional deficits of schizophrenia and schizotypy. The hierarchy can invert temporarily in normal individuals when the target is known to be local. Underlying it: "the left hemisphere's narrow focussed attentional beam, which it believes it 'turns' towards whatever it may be, has in reality already been seized by it" — the right hemisphere controls where attention is oriented, and we do not build up perception by serial scanning, whatever the introspecting left hemisphere claims after the fact.

There is also a fundamental *asymmetry of concern*, not just of attention: the right hemisphere attends to the *entire* visual field regardless of side, while the left hemisphere attends only to the right half of space and body. In split-brain patients, the right hemisphere covers the whole visual field; the left covers only the right. This asymmetry explains **hemi-neglect** after a right-hemisphere stroke: the entire left half of the world — a person standing to the left, the left half of a clock face, the left page of a book, the left half of one's own body — simply fails to materialise, sometimes to the point of denying the left side of the body exists at all (Figure 2.3, templates copied by neglect patients). This is not blindness; temporarily disabling the left hemisphere via TMS improves the neglect, showing it results from the *unopposed* action of the intact left hemisphere. There is no mirror-image neglect after a left-hemisphere stroke, because the intact right hemisphere still supplies a whole body and world. There is also a curious "stickiness": after right-hemisphere damage, the intact right hemifield exerts what looks like a magnetic attraction, with familiar stimuli producing facilitation rather than the normal inhibition. McGilchrist describes a patient of his own who, after a right-hemisphere stroke, would become fixated on a door hinge to his right and had to be actively disengaged by carers. Fittingly, it is the right hemisphere that controls conjugate eye movements — perhaps, McGilchrist suggests, also what keeps the hemispheres' worlds together as one.

## The Whole versus the Part

"The link between the right hemisphere and holistic or Gestalt perception is one of the most reliable and durable of the generalisations" in this literature. The right hemisphere's grasp of the whole is not built from summed parts — it is an "aha!" phenomenon, illustrated by the famous Dalmatian-dog-in-dappled-shade image (Figure 2.4), which emerges all at once rather than gradually. Split-brain subjects can match a felt object to its shape using the left hand (right hemisphere) but not the right hand (left hemisphere) (Figure 2.5). Drawing studies show complementary deficits: patients with right parieto-occipital lesions lose the Gestalt entirely — one, asked to draw an elephant, "draws only a tail, a trunk and an ear," and assembling a model elephant "is done slowly and ends in a complete fiasco," even though he recognises the individual elements. Figures collapse into absurd simplifications (a man as a blob with three stick limbs, a bicycle with small wheels above big pedals) (Figure 2.6). Left-hemisphere-damaged patients' drawings, by contrast, are impoverished in detail but preserve overall shape (Figure 2.7). The same split shows up in perception, not just execution: a patient of Hécaen and de Ajuriaguerra's could not recognise a drawn house as a house until he spotted the chimney — "the whole was inscrutable, but the part gave it away." Crucially, this integrative deficit is not confined to the visual domain: right-hemisphere patients' difficulty grasping visuospatial wholes correlates with difficulty grasping verbal-semantic wholes, undercutting any simple verbal/visuospatial dichotomy.

## Context versus Abstraction

Just as the right hemisphere sees the whole before the parts, it sees everything *in context* — "anything but abstract." Understanding anything indirect or non-literal depends on the right frontal lobe; the right hemisphere reads context (it infers winter from the trees), while the left hemisphere reads labels (it infers winter from the word "January"). Applied to language: the right hemisphere is specialised in **pragmatics** and **metaphor**, and underlies humour — it is why "it's a bit hot in here today" is understood by the right hemisphere as a request to open a window, while a purely left-hemisphere reading treats it as literal meteorological information. The left hemisphere, by contrast, is the "hemisphere of abstraction" — "the process of wresting things from their context" — which grounds its power of categorisation.

McGilchrist frames the deepest distinction through Hughlings Jackson, the "father of modern neuropsychiatry," who described a First World War patient who had lost the power of speech but could still pick up a brick on command:

> I do not believe that the man who cannot say (nor write) the word brick can be said to have a 'memory' of it (be conscious of the word itself). He has no consciousness of it, but of the thing it is a symbol of – a very different thing.

McGilchrist crystallises this as: **"The left hemisphere can only re-present; but the right hemisphere, for its part, can only give again what 'presences.'"** The left hemisphere's abstract visual-form system stores information invariant across specific instances (producing types/classes); the right hemisphere remembers what distinguishes one instance of a type from another, dealing "preferentially with actually existing things, as they are encountered in the real world." He illustrates the contextual/abstract split through two kinds of symbol: the rose, whose power lies in an endless, necessarily *implicit* network of connotations (right hemisphere) versus the red traffic light, whose power lies in an *explicit*, unambiguous 1:1 mapping (left hemisphere). On metaphor specifically, the right temporal region is essential for integrating two seemingly unrelated concepts into a live metaphor — but, intriguingly, *clichéd* or dead metaphors are processed by the *left* hemisphere, because reviving their literal sense (refreshing the cliché) is itself an act of insight, "a bit like seeing a joke."

## Individuals versus Categories

The right hemisphere distinguishes specific instances within a category — unique referents, unique individuals, "that face, that voice, that gait, that sheer 'quiddity'" — while the left hemisphere re-presents categories and generic, non-specific objects. Category specificity is relative and hierarchical: characterising something as a car is general; as a Citroën, more specific; as a 2CV, more specific still — and as categories become more individuated ("subordinate"), they shift to right-hemisphere processing, while general ("superordinate") categories stay with the left. Consistent with this, the left hemisphere is actually *better* at simple, easily-categorised shapes, while the right is better with complex, atypical, individual ones — functional imaging shows the left hemisphere taking a "God's eye," invariant view of objects, the right using stored "real world" views.

McGilchrist connects the left hemisphere's categorising drive, when unchecked, to **"punding"** — the mechanical, repetitive assembling/disassembling and collecting/categorising of objects (torches, TVs, stones) seen with excess dopaminergic transmission (amphetamine abuse, high-dose anti-Parkinsonian treatment) that favours the left hemisphere, and clinically resembles aspects of schizophrenia. He recalls a schizophrenic patient who built elaborate symmetrical "sculptures" from commercial packaging, and who, asked about his weekend, replied dryly: "I moved some things to the right" — a remark McGilchrist links to the left hemisphere's bias toward the right side of space. He quotes Henry Maudsley on this drive's danger even in the "sane": a "propensity not only to make divisions in knowledge where there are none in nature… but to go further, and to convert the generalisations made from observation into positive entities, permitting for the future these artificial creations to tyrannise over the understanding."

## The Differences in Sameness

The contrast surfaces strikingly in how sameness and difference are handled. An individual — say, one's spouse, who may have a different mood or haircut by evening — is, to the right hemisphere, a single continuous whole; the left hemisphere, taken alone, would see only "separate frames of the film." Certain right-hemisphere deficits break this unity: **Capgras syndrome**, the delusion that a familiar person has been replaced by an impostor — here "the significance of the part … outweighs the pull of the whole." The apparent mirror-image is **Fregoli syndrome** (named after an early-1900s Italian quick-change artist): the belief that one person is duplicated across different people/times. John Cutting describes this as: "Something personal and usually alive has been duplicated as if it were a mere item on an assembly line." McGilchrist gives clinical examples of his own: a patient who accused her husband of infidelity because she "saw him" repeatedly with different women; a patient convinced someone had replaced all her clothes with "slightly inferior" copies; and a patient who believed there were **eight impostor cities** duplicating his own, with eight duplicate wives and children, and had spent eight years wandering between them looking for the real one. McGilchrist stresses Capgras and Fregoli are not opposites but two expressions of the same underlying loss: the right hemisphere's sense of a unique whole.

## The Personal versus the Impersonal

Because it never sees anything in the abstract, the right hemisphere is drawn to the personal, where the left has more affinity for the abstract/impersonal. The right temporal lobe handles **episodic memory** (personal, emotionally charged), the left, memory for public-domain facts. McGilchrist notes the right hemisphere's concern with the personal past may connect to its tendency toward sadness (developed later in the chapter).

## The Living versus the Non-Living

The neurologist François Lhermitte, some thirty-five years before McGilchrist's writing, described a case establishing that the right hemisphere is specifically concerned with **living individuals**, as against man-made objects — consistent with its interest in whatever exists apart from ourselves, and with empathy. The left hemisphere alone codes for non-living things; both hemispheres code for living things (as independent individuals, for the right; as objects of use/prey, for the left). Food and musical instruments, being intimately bound up with bodily life, sort with the living. The body itself is a right-hemisphere entity; body *parts* belong to the left. When the right hemisphere fails to bring the left side of the body into being, the left hemisphere may substitute a purely mechanical structure — Ehrenwald describes a patient who, after a right-hemisphere stroke, reported: **"where the left half of his chest, abdomen and stomach should be, he's got only a wooden plank,"** divided into compartments through which food fell rather than passing through intestines — a genuine *percept*, not merely a delusional idea.

The left hemisphere's complementary affinity is for the mechanical and useful: tool- and machine-related words activate the left hemisphere even in left-handers; right-hemisphere damage leaves simple tool use intact but impairs sequenced "naturalistic" actions (making coffee, wrapping a present), while left-hemisphere damage impairs simple tool use itself. McGilchrist engages Michael Corballis's suggestion (in tribute to Sperry) that "the left side represents the fruits of human invention… the right side nature" — he would resist calling the left side "science" (since real empiricism draws on the right hemisphere too) but would accept "mechanism" in its place. The right temporal region also has areas specific to "humanness" as such, separate from face-recognition per se. Correspondingly, the right hemisphere prefers realistic scenes with lived meaning, while the left hemisphere has "a positive bias towards whatever is bizarre, meaningless or non-existent" — distorted, artificial, fantastic imagery.

## Empathy and 'Theory of Mind'

The right hemisphere's openness to interconnectedness makes it "the mediator of empathic identification": **"If I imagine myself in pain I use both hemispheres, but your pain is in my right hemisphere."** The same neurons in the right anterior cingulate cortex fire whether we are hurt ourselves or witness someone else's pain. McGilchrist quotes the summary: "Self-awareness, empathy, identification with others, and more generally inter-subjective processes, are largely dependent upon … right hemisphere resources." Perspective-taking recruits the right inferior parietal lobe and right lateral prefrontal cortex (which inhibits the automatic pull toward one's own viewpoint); right-hemisphere activation makes people more open to unfamiliar arguments. Crucially, the right hemisphere will only empathise with and imitate what it recognises as a *living being*, never a mechanism — real hands, but not virtual/computer hands, activate the right temporoparietal area, and imitative "empathic entrainment" of another's action collapses entirely if the observer believes the actor is a computer.

**Theory of mind** — imagining another's mental state — is illustrated with the classic **Sally-Anne false-belief test** (two dolls, a marble moved between boxes while one doll is absent; children lacking theory of mind say the returning doll will look in the box where the marble *actually* is, not where she last saw it placed). This capacity, tied to self-recognition and self-awareness, typically matures by around age four (with elements from 12–18 months) and is never fully acquired in autism. Right frontal deficits specifically (not left) produce loss of empathy and a personality change described as conducting "social intercourse … with a blanket disregard for the feelings, wishes, needs and expectations of others." On **mirror neurons** — cells active both when performing and observing an action — McGilchrist corrects the assumption that these are a left-hemisphere (Broca's-area) speciality: both hemispheres have them, with instrumental/object-directed imitation favouring the left and non-instrumental imitation (including facial-expression imitation, via the right pars opercularis) favouring the right — the latter area notably silent in autistic children performing such tasks.

## Emotional Asymmetry

Emotion is intimately tied to the body, and the right hemisphere's connections are correspondingly deeper: its orbitofrontal cortex is essential to emotional understanding and regulation and is where emotional significance is consciously registered; the right hemisphere has closer links to the limbic system and regulates the hypothalamic-pituitary axis (the neuroendocrine emotion-body interface) and autonomic arousal (heart rate, neuroendocrine function); the right frontotemporal cortex exerts inhibitory control over emotional arousal. Across virtually every form of emotional perception and most forms of expression, regardless of emotion type, **the right hemisphere is dominant.**

## Emotional Receptivity

The right hemisphere identifies emotional expression faster and more accurately than the left (right superior temporal sulcus for facial emotion), and is the seat of interpreting prosody and gesture too. Curiously, the left hemisphere reads emotion from the *lower* face/mouth even when told to look at the eyes; only the right hemisphere reads the subtler information in the eyes. McGilchrist recounts a patient with a right temporoparietal deficit who asked him, "What's all this with the eyes?" — she had noticed people apparently exchanging coded messages with their eyes but could no longer decode them, a source of paranoia. The right hemisphere also dominates emotional *language* comprehension (emotional language can survive even after a left-hemisphere stroke that abolishes ordinary speech) and remembers emotional language preferentially.

Faces bring together the right hemisphere's two great concerns — individual uniqueness and feeling. **Prosopagnosia** (inability to recognise faces) follows right-hemisphere lesions; Joachim Bodamer's 1947 patient described faces as "strangely flat, white with emphatic dark eyes, as if made from a flat surface, like white oval plates, all alike." Sergent and Villemure characterised the deficit as "an inability to combine the component features into a configurational facial representation that would uniquely define each face." The relevant network centres on the right middle fusiform gyrus but is broadly distributed across the right hemisphere; right-hemisphere-damaged patients are also worse at judging an unfamiliar face's age, sex, and attractiveness. This right-hemisphere face specialism goes deep evolutionarily — even sheep can remember individual human and sheep faces for years, relying principally on the right hemisphere.

## Emotional Expressivity

The right frontal lobe is critical for emotional expression through face and body posture — with one notable exception: **anger**, which is robustly tied to *left* frontal activation (dopamine reward plays a role in aggression's motivational pull). Autistic children, lacking right-frontal-mediated empathy and metaphor understanding, also lack prosodic skill. The right hemisphere mediates spontaneous smiling and laughter, and the distinctly human capacity to express sadness through tears. The **left hemiface** (controlled by the right hemisphere) is more expressive and, interestingly, physically larger in right-handers; emotions read from the left hemiface are perceived as stronger and more mixed, while the simpler right hemiface may be easier to read across cultures. The universal practice of **cradling infants facing left** — documented for at least 2,000–4,000 years, present even among left-handed mothers, and shared with chimpanzees and gorillas — exposes infants to the adult's more expressive left hemiface and keeps them within the domain of the adult's attentive right hemisphere.

## Differences in Emotional Affinity

The right hemisphere assigns emotional value to what is seen; without it, self and world become emotionally impoverished, and the right frontal lobe is central to personality itself. The left hemisphere is involved in emotion too, but in a more superficial, "social," and consciously *willed* register — willed facial expressions (again, mouth-centred) are left-hemisphere-controlled, and the left (but not right) amygdala is tied to explicit representation of an observed emotion, while the right amygdala handles unconscious emotional processing. The left hemisphere remains comparatively emotionally neutral — it shows an affinity for abstract, "non-emotional" paintings — and does not personally "adopt" emotional stimuli offered to it the way the right does. In **alexithymia** (inability to identify/verbalise emotion), the problem is a failure of the emotionally aware right hemisphere to communicate its arousal to the left.

McGilchrist rejects old, simple dichotomies (right = negative emotion, left = positive; or right = "withdrawal," left = "approach") as unsatisfactory, but finds broad support that the right hemisphere is more attuned to *sadness* and less to anger than the left, that "positive" emotions draw on both hemispheres, and that the left hemisphere leans toward an *unwarrantedly* optimistic view of self and future. He speculates that bonding/empathy-related emotions (whether labelled positive or negative) preferentially engage the right hemisphere, while competition/rivalry/self-belief emotions engage the left. A striking aside links colour to this asymmetry: conscious colour naming/categorisation is left-sided, but colour discrimination and mental-imagery colour perception (right fusiform area) are right-hemisphere-dominant, with hints that the right hemisphere prefers **green** and the left prefers **red** — and green's long folk-association with melancholy (Shakespeare's "green and yellow melancholy") and the medieval doctrine of black bile/spleen (a left-sided organ, etymologically tied to melancholy) provide a suggestive historical echo.

The chapter surveys the laterality of **depression** at length: left anterior lesions → depression; right anterior lesions → "undue cheerfulness"; the closer a left-hemisphere lesion to the frontal pole, the greater the depressive symptoms; for right-hemisphere lesions, the more posterior (caudal), the greater the depression risk. EEG confirms left frontal hypoactivation in depression and left-hemisphere overactivity in mania, its polar opposite; treatment response to antidepressants correlates with the degree of initial left anterior blood-flow reduction. Depression following right-hemisphere damage presents as global apathy/indifference; following left-hemisphere damage, as anxious, agitated depression with biological features — matching a further split between **anxious arousal** (right-lateralised) and **anxious apprehension**, rooted in fear of uncertainty and loss of control (left-lateralised). McGilchrist closes the topic with Jaak Panksepp's caution that neuroimaging of emotion likely captures the *cognitive content* of an affective state rather than the pre-cognitive affect itself.

## Reason versus Rationality

The stereotype that the left hemisphere monopolises reason is, McGilchrist argues, as mistaken as the one about language. Linear, sequential argument is indeed better executed by the left hemisphere, but deduction and much mathematical reasoning depend mainly on the right; "more explicit reasoning is underwritten by the left hemisphere, less explicit reasoning… by the right." The pleasurable "aha!" of **insight** — mathematical or verbal — correlates with right amygdala activity and right anterior temporal (specifically anterior superior temporal gyrus) activation, with added right prefrontal involvement when heavy restructuring is needed; becoming consciously aware of one's own reasoning process can *impair* performance, because it invites the left hemisphere's focus on explicit surface structure.

Mathematics is genuinely split: addition/subtraction engage the right parietal lobe, multiplication engages left-hemisphere verbal memory for times tables, and calculating prodigies rely on right-hemisphere episodic-memory strategies. Deductive reasoning is independent of both left-hemisphere language areas and right-hemisphere visuospatial areas — it engages the right superior parietal cortex and, bilaterally, the **precuneus** (part of the limbic system, tied to episodic memory, first-person perspective, and one of the brain's metabolically "hottest" resting regions, which goes quiet in sleep, anaesthesia, and vegetative states) even when problems are presented acoustically with no visual component; it is also linked to the right ventromedial prefrontal area, an emotion-processing region. On numbers: the right hemisphere carries an intuitive, approximate sense of magnitude, while the left has precision but "no intuitive sense of what it is actually doing, other than following rules and manipulating symbols" — McGilchrist notes the Pythagorean point that numbers can signify either absolute quantity (left-hemisphere affinity) or relation/proportion (right-hemisphere affinity, as in "the music of the spheres").

## The Twin Bodies

Only the right parietal lobe carries a **whole-body image**; the left hemisphere carries an image only of the contralateral (right) side of the body, so when the right hemisphere is incapacitated, the left side of the body effectively ceases to exist for the sufferer. This body image is not a representation or a mere sum of perceptions but "a living image, intimately linked to activity in the world — an essentially affective experience," whose disturbance underlies conditions like body dysmorphia and anorexia nervosa. For the right hemisphere the body is something *lived*, part of identity, "the phase of intersection between our selves and the world"; for the left it is a detached "thing in the world," in Sartre's terms *en soi* rather than *pour soi* — a "corpse." McGilchrist notes German distinguishes these senses lexically (**Leib**, the living body, versus **Körper**, related to "corpse") and that the Greek *sōma*, later used for the body-as-object, in Homer refers only to a corpse, never a living body.

The left hemisphere's tendency to see the body as an assemblage of parts produces striking pathology: **asomatognosia**, in which patients deny ownership of a paralysed limb — calling it "not mine," attributing it to a neighbour, a "dead hand," or (in one case) believing it belongs to one's own mother. McGilchrist transcribes a full clinical dialogue with patient "AR," who insists her paralysed left arm is her mother's ("It's warmer… my mother was a laundress") until vestibular stimulation (which inhibits the left hemisphere) temporarily restores recognition, only for the delusion to return hours later. Asomatognosia occurs in nearly 90% of right-hemisphere strokes and, per Feinberg's case series, in *none* of 100 known cases after left-hemisphere damage. It can escalate into a sense of alien control — a Lhermitte patient, euphoric despite left-sided paralysis, later complained of an "alien hand" that "presses on my tummy and chokes me… it doesn't belong to me"; another patient believed his left side was "evil," controlled by the devil in league with his dead father.

The right hemisphere also carries greater **proprioceptive awareness** (better sense of the contralateral hand's position without looking) and is more closely tied to the bodily/physiological correlates of emotion — reinforcing the leftward-cradling practice, since "the emotional impact of touch… is also more direct and immediate if an infant is held to the left side of the body." Sexual function, once depression is controlled for, depends more on the right hemisphere. Right-hemisphere damage can disintegrate the normal integration of body and self, reducing the body to "a compendium of drives" no longer tied to personality — producing morbid excess appetite for sex or food. Within the autonomic nervous system there is tentative evidence that sympathetic control leans right-hemisphere and parasympathetic control leans left, though McGilchrist flags this area as genuinely unclear.

## Meaning and the Implicit

The left hemisphere's great gift is language as symbol-manipulation — the subject of the next chapter — but its lateralisation is not about auditory processing per se: **sign language**, though visuospatial, is still left-hemisphere-mediated, and deaf signers with left-hemisphere lesions show deficits analogous to hearing aphasics with matching lesions. This shows the specialisation concerns *signs and representations*, not modality. The left hemisphere is, in a sense, less concerned with meaning than with form and system — when unconstrained by the right hemisphere, it can produce a "meaningless hypertrophy of language." The right hemisphere, despite having a smaller vocabulary and less complex syntax, uses language to grasp what others *mean*: it understands the moral of a story, the point of a joke, and — critically — makes **inferences**. Right-hemisphere-damaged patients "do not understand implicit meanings whatever their kind, but detect explicit meanings only," struggling with metaphor, humour, irony, and sarcasm.

Non-verbal communication is a right-hemisphere strength: unconscious reading of facial expression (fusiform gyrus) mirrors observed emotional shifts within 300–400 milliseconds, beneath conscious awareness; because the right hemisphere alone attends to the eyes, it is better at detecting deceit. McGilchrist quotes John Napier: **"If language was given to men to conceal their thoughts, then gesture's purpose was to disclose them."** He also quotes a description of how non-verbal behaviour, language, expression, and intonation "are instrumental in establishing complex contradictory, predominantly emotional relations between people" — precisely *because* of speech's accuracy, it is unsuited to what is "too complex, changeful and ambiguous." Fittingly, a right-hemisphere stroke, though it typically spares speech, is in practice *more* disabling than a left-hemisphere stroke (which typically abolishes speech) — chances of independent living are higher after left-hemisphere stroke — because what is lost on the right is not a function but a whole way of standing in relation to others.

## Music and Time

Music, "grounded in the body, communicative of emotion, implicit," is a natural expression of the right hemisphere's world. McGilchrist frames music as pure **"betweenness"**: notes mean nothing alone; melody, harmony, and rhythm live in the relations, the gaps, between notes and silence, yet the music is neither purely in the notes nor purely in the gaps but in the whole they make together — an idea he extends to poetry as "a speaking silence." He revisits the idea that the right hemisphere, though "silent" in the sense of having limited explicit vocabulary, communicates implicitly through exactly this kind of relational meaning. Musical features — syncopation, appoggiaturas, enharmonic changes — set up expectations that are confirmed or disrupted, producing measurable physiological effects (breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, gooseflesh, tears), mediated through the right hemisphere's links to the hypothalamus and body.

Music, like poetry, is described as intrinsically tinged with sadness — a "holiday of the minor key" — matching a documented affinity between the right hemisphere and the **minor key** (versus left hemisphere/major key). McGilchrist quotes the pre-Socratic Gorgias ("awe and tearful pity and mournful desire enter those who listen to poetry"), Suzanne Langer (music can "evoke emotions and moods we have not felt, passions we did not know before"), Mendelssohn's paradox that music's meaning is "not too indefinite to be put into words, but on the contrary too definite," and Nietzsche: **"Compared with music all communication by words is shameless; words dilute and brutalise; words depersonalise; words make the uncommon common."**

Clinically, singing survives left-hemisphere strokes that abolish speech (words in song draw on wide right-hemisphere activation), while right-hemisphere damage produces **amusia** — loss of music appreciation/performance with speech intact. Case studies include composer Vissarion Shebalin, who continued composing works Shostakovich judged indistinguishable from his pre-stroke output despite severe aphasia from a left temporal/parietal stroke; a blind professional organist who retained music-reading/writing in Braille despite losing word-reading/writing after a left MCA stroke; and a composer/conductor who lost the ability to read words but retained musical notation. Melody, timbre, and pitch are almost always right-hemisphere-mediated in non-professionals; rhythm is mostly right-hemisphere except basic metrical patterns (Broca's area, left hemisphere), with more complex/syncopated rhythms staying right-lateralised; harmony is essentially a right-hemisphere function. Intriguingly, *trained* musicians shift toward more bilateral/left-hemisphere processing — Jerre Levy's "flying mathematicians" observation that accomplished musicians (like accomplished scientists) show more evenly distributed skills across both hemispheres — yet even trained musicians show strong right-hemisphere activation listening to Bach's contrapuntal music, plausibly because tracking multiple simultaneous melodic lines demands the right hemisphere's superior working memory, or because such music, never fully captured on one hearing, remains perpetually "new."

Time itself is treated as music's medium: the sense of time passing depends on sustained attention (right prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobe), duration comparison depends on the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and virtually all lived appreciation of past/present/future is right-hemisphere-dependent — severely impaired after right-hemisphere damage. **Narrative sequencing** (a story with "real world" meaning) is right-hemisphere ("the left hemisphere cannot follow a narrative"), while sequencing of artificially decontextualised, unrelated momentary events is left-hemisphere — but McGilchrist stresses this second kind of "sequencing" is not really a sense of time at all, it's what takes over once the true, flowing sense of time (right-hemisphere) has broken down, "like a series of tangents that approaches ever more closely to a circle without ever actually achieving it." **Palinopsia** (visual trailing, disturbed flow of visual experience) follows posterior right-hemisphere lesions; its collapse into a succession of static frames is called the **Zeitraffer phenomenon**. He resolves an apparent paradox — the left hemisphere accepts permanence only in stasis, yet things (like a river) can be simultaneously flowing and permanent — by noting that only the right hemisphere perceives permanence-within-flux; its absence yields the Capgras phenomenon (loss of a person's continuous identity across change). The section closes with George Steiner's description of music as **"time made free of temporality."**

## Depth

The visual analogue of time is spatial depth (McGilchrist invokes Einstein's unification of space and time). The right hemisphere organises space by *degree of distance* ("nearer/further from me"), the left more categorically ("above," "below") — paralleling duration (right) versus sequencing (left). The right hemisphere is even biased toward what lies farther away, an extension of its broader attentional reach; the left hemisphere, correspondingly, struggles with depth and can misjudge object size dramatically. Right-hemisphere deficits specifically impair handling of irregular, smoothly curved 3D surfaces (as in living things), while a predictable rectilinear object like a cube remains manageable — a deficit possibly contributing to prosopagnosia (recall Bodamer's patient describing faces as flat "white oval plates," lacking depth). The right hemisphere delivers a realistic, detailed, three-dimensional world, with an aesthetic sense of beauty largely originating there; the left hemisphere represents the visual world schematically, abstractly, geometrically, and often flattened into a single plane — buildings drawn with all façades simultaneously visible, "as in a child's drawing," when the right hemisphere is inactivated (Figures 2.8–2.10). Gazzaniga and LeDoux's split-brain patient could draw a 3D cube with either hand before commissurotomy, but afterward could manage only a flat diagram with the (left-hemisphere) right hand, while the left hand (right hemisphere) still produced a proper 3D construction (Figure 2.11).

## Certainty

The left hemisphere favours the man-made precisely because it is knowable and fixed — "we know them inside out, because we put them together" — whereas the right hemisphere, tracking things as they actually and constantly change, has no comparable databank of fixed categories and so cannot claim the same certainty. Its natural mode is accordingly "virtually silent, relatively shifting and uncertain," while the left hemisphere "may be unreasonably, even stubbornly, convinced of its own correctness." John Cutting is quoted describing an "astonishing degree of ignorance on the part of the left (supposed major) hemisphere about what its partner… [is] up to," even as it "abrogates decision-making to itself in the absence of any rational evidence as to what is going on."

The chapter's most memorable evidence for this comes from split-brain **confabulation** experiments. Shown a nude photo in her right hemisphere only, a split-brain patient laughs and blushes; asked why by an experimenter addressing her speaking left hemisphere (which saw nothing), she invents an explanation ("someone in the room is upsetting her"). In Gazzaniga and LeDoux's famous experiment with patient PS, a snow scene shown only to the right hemisphere leads his left hand to correctly select a picture of a shovel, while a chicken claw shown only to the left hemisphere leads his right hand to select a chicken. Asked to explain the shovel choice, his speaking left hemisphere — ignorant of the snow scene — confabulates without hesitation: "I saw a chicken claw and picked the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed." The authors stress the left hemisphere offers this "not… in a guessing vein but rather [as] a statement of fact." McGilchrist connects this to confabulation generally, quoting Panksepp: "The linguistically proficient left hemisphere … appears predisposed to repress negative emotions, and even chooses to confabulate" — related to Gazzaniga's concept of the left-hemisphere "**interpreter**," while it is the right hemisphere that actually judges the truth or plausibility of these invented narratives.

This habit is not harmless. In a probability-matching experiment, split-brain subjects (JW and VP) predicted which of two colours (appearing in a 4:1 ratio) would appear next: the *optimal* strategy — always guess the majority colour, scoring 80% — is what the right hemisphere does when isolated, but the speaking left hemisphere instead tries to match the exact 4:1 ratio, scoring barely above chance, because "the left hemisphere develops a rule – a rule that is, however, wrong." An earlier experiment with normal subjects, secretly rigged so that whatever the subject predicted became the next outcome (guaranteeing 100% correctness), found subjects had been guessing at only 80% accuracy right up to the switch — yet afterward "overwhelmingly" insisted they'd finally cracked a fixed pattern, inventing elaborate, fictitious explanatory systems. The right hemisphere, by contrast, tolerates ambiguity and incomplete information (right prefrontal cortex), staying active during perceptual rivalry — for example with the duck–rabbit (Figure 2.12) or the Necker cube (Figure 2.13) — where right frontal cortex activity increases. Degraded or briefly presented images consistently favour right-hemisphere processing (even for verbal material), while longer, more certain presentation favours the left — even for faces, per Justine Sergent's work, who observed that letters "represent a finite set of stimuli that are sharply focussed, familiar and overlearned," unlike the "potentially infinite," largely unfamiliar set of visual images. McGilchrist notes the fovea itself — with roughly 100x the resolution of the periphery but covering barely 1° (and a total sharply-resolved field of about 3°) — is the physiological seat of the left hemisphere's narrow attentional "spotlight."

## Self-Awareness and Emotional Timbre

The right hemisphere is markedly more realistic about its own limitations; the left is "ever optimistic, but unrealistic about its short-comings." Patients with right-hemisphere strokes are largely unmoved by constructive feedback on their performance; one researcher describes children with "right-brain deficit disorder" who "ignore task obstacles, accept impossible challenges, make grossly inadequate efforts, and are stunned by the poor outcomes," behaving fearlessly because they "overlook the dangers inherent in the situation." Stuss describes a highly intelligent professional who, after right prefrontal tumour removal, could correctly advise a hypothetical colleague with his exact deficits to take medical retirement — but was wholly unable to apply that insight to himself.

Somewhat paradoxically, this same self-awareness deficit ties to mood: those who are somewhat depressed tend to be *more* realistic, including in self-evaluation, and depression itself skews toward relative right-hemisphere dominance — even schizophrenics show more illness insight in proportion to their depressive symptoms, and "it is not because insight makes you depressed, but because being depressed gives you insight." **Anosognosia** — denial of illness, e.g. of a completely paralysed limb — depends on right-hemisphere damage (almost never occurring after left-hemisphere/right-sided stroke) and can be reversed by activating the right hemisphere, or conversely induced by anaesthetising it. Hoff and Pötzl describe a patient who, shown her own paralysed left hand, looks away insisting "I don't see it," hides it under the bedclothes, and never looks or turns toward the left. The affected limb can provoke outright revulsion — **misoplegia** — with one patient dropping her own hand "with an expression of disgust." Right-hemisphere lesions can also produce a "fatuous" mood disturbance — euphoria, joviality, feeble punning — as in a Hécaen and de Ajuriaguerra patient exhibiting "surprising joviality" while simultaneously complaining of a severe headache.

Denial is, in this sense, "a left-hemisphere speciality": states of relative right-hemisphere inactivation bias people toward optimistic self-evaluation, more positive picture-ratings, and greater attachment to existing viewpoints; after a right-hemisphere stroke the intact left hemisphere is "crippled by naively optimistic forecasting of outcomes." Winning correlates with left amygdala activation, losing with right. McGilchrist links the right hemisphere's tendency to sorrow with its deeper connectedness to others — "No man is an island": sadness correlates strongly with empathy, and both correlate with guilt, shame, and responsibility — while **psychopaths**, lacking guilt/shame, show deficits specifically in the right ventromedial/orbitofrontal cortex. He traces an etymological thread from Greek *pathe* (feeling) to *pathos* (affliction) and *paschein* (to suffer), cognate with English "passion" and German *Leidenschaften* (from *leiden*, to suffer) — grounds for doubting any easy equation of pleasure with "the good." He recounts Berlioz's retort when told he seemed too affected by a performance to stay: **"Are you under the impression that I am here to enjoy myself?"** — and reframes King Lear's "Is there any cause in nature that makes these hard hearts?" as, in one sense, answerable: a defect in the right prefrontal cortex — though cruelty as such is not "natural," since only the left prefrontal cortex makes deliberate malice possible, just as only the right makes compassion possible.

## The Self

Conscious self-awareness is evolutionarily late: chimpanzees and orang-utans pass the mirror test, monkeys do not. The right prefrontal region is critical for self-recognition by face or by voice; correct use of the pronouns "I" and "me" — an important marker of self-awareness — is impaired in autism, itself replicating many right-hemisphere deficits. No single hemisphere constitutes the self alone, but McGilchrist distinguishes: the empathically-embedded, continuous sense of self depends more on the right hemisphere, while the objectified self, and the self as an expression of will, depends more on the left. (Split-brain evidence on self-recognition is mixed — some studies favour a right-hemisphere advantage, others find both hemispheres can recognise the self objectively, with the right retaining an advantage specifically for recognising *familiar others*.) The "self-concept" — a personal, interior sense of self with history and emotional memory — is impaired by right-hemisphere injury wherever it occurs, though the right frontal region is most critical; the right hemisphere is more engaged by emotional, autobiographical memory. McGilchrist quotes the summary formulation: **"The sense of self emerges from the activity of the brain in interaction with other selves."**

He traces the self's *development*: the right orbitofrontal cortex (larger in primates than the left) likely expands during infant-mother play in the second half of the first year and into the second year — the period Allan Schore identifies as the emergence of the self, calling this region its "crucible." The right hemisphere matures earlier than the left and dominates early-childhood mental development generally, and social/empathic selfhood specifically; social development proceeds independently of language development, reinforcing its right-hemisphere origin. Self-development and theory-of-mind development are closely linked, both mapping onto the right frontal and right cingulate cortex. The right hemisphere is also described as the "**glue**" holding the self together — evidence from dementia shows it "connects the individual to emotionally salient experiences and memories underlying self-schemas," echoing Douglas Watt's observation that "emotion binds together virtually every type of information the brain can encode … part of the glue that holds the whole system together." The right prefrontal cortex, with its subcortical and cortical connections, underwrites a continuous sense of self over time; right frontal damage impairs precisely this narrative, flow-like sense of self. Sperry hypothesised a right-hemisphere network as the origin of self-awareness itself; a right frontoparietal network (anterior precuneus, posterior cingulate) distinguishes self from other, and viewing one's own face activates the right occipitotemporoparietal junction and right frontal operculum.

Clinically, **asomatognosia** (failure to recognise one's own body parts) occurs in roughly 90% of right-hemisphere strokes and, per Feinberg, in none of 100 known cases of left-hemisphere damage; right frontotemporal damage produces a broader "cognitive detachment from self"; reading first-person (versus third-person) narrative preferentially activates the right temporo-parietal junction. McGilchrist draws a parallel between philosophers and schizophrenics: both, in effect, subject the ordinarily implicit, unconscious, intuitive "life of the right hemisphere" to left-hemisphere scrutiny, and in both cases the normally robust sense of self can come apart under that analysis. He quotes Wittgenstein: "it's strange that in ordinary life we are not troubled by the feeling that the phenomenon is slipping away from us, the constant flux of appearance, but only when we philosophise. This indicates that what is in question here is an idea suggested by a misapplication of our language" — and suggests this "misapplication" may be exactly the error of interrogating the right hemisphere's world from within the left's.

A Wada-test experiment provides especially clean evidence: subjects were shown a morph of their own face with a famous person's face; those who viewed it under right-hemisphere-only conditions later identified the morph as themselves, while those under left-hemisphere-only conditions identified it as the famous person (9 of 10 cases conforming to this pattern). The right frontal region governs self-recognition in other modalities too (e.g. voice); right parietal/medial damage can cause self/other confusion, and right frontal damage disturbs "ego boundaries," normally responsible for "establishing the appropriate relationship between the self and the world" — a breakdown vividly visible in schizophrenia's boundary-melting experiences. Practically, the right inferior parietal lobule is central to both planning and monitoring one's own actions, and — countering the old assumption that the left hemisphere, as seat of conscious will, governs self-initiated action — genuinely self-initiated (versus imitative) practical action is largely right-hemisphere. Independence and motivation associate with the right hemisphere; passivity, with the left, tied to the left hemisphere's "stickiness." This shows up pathologically as **environmental dependency syndrome** (or "forced utilisation behaviour") — reflexively picking up and using any object placed nearby (glasses, a pen, a stethoscope) — along with aboulia (loss of will), akinesia, and impersistence, all more common, per Kenneth Heilman, after right frontal damage; in four of five cases in Lhermitte's original description, the lesion was in the right frontal lobe, with patients explaining "you held out objects to me; I thought I had to use them." (McGilchrist notes his own reading of Lhermitte's fuller data complicates a clean right-lateralisation claim — the syndrome may be roughly as common with either frontal lobe damaged.)

## Coda: The 'Front–Back' Problem

McGilchrist closes with the one *intra*hemispheric distinction he flags as unavoidable: the relationship between the frontal lobes (the most recently evolved, most distinctively human brain regions) and the posterior cortex of the *same* hemisphere, which the frontal lobes exist to modulate. This is usually described as frontal "inhibition" of posterior activity, but McGilchrist prefers **"modulation"** — especially for the right hemisphere — since the inhibitory effect is "significantly more pronounced" in the left hemisphere, consistent with its more "black and white," less integrated style. Modulation means "the imposition of necessary distance, or delay, enabling something new to come forward" — structurally analogous to the interhemispheric relationship itself: neither pure negation nor bland complementarity, but a "dialectical synthesis" producing something genuinely new. On the right side, frontal-lobe distance tempers the right hemisphere's immediate, embodied, "mine"-centred emotional pull, thereby *enabling* — not negating — a broader empathy and the beginnings of altruism. On the left side, the frontal lobe's distancing of the left hemisphere's already-detached, abstracting stance can, pushed further, produce the meditative "emptying out" experts describe as mystical experience. McGilchrist doubts there is any single "God spot," but cites Michael Trimble's cautious review finding evidence linking religious experience to both the posterior right (temporoparietal) hemisphere *and* the left frontal lobe — the latter, tellingly, via its inhibitory power over the posterior left hemisphere, the seat of language and sequential analysis.

## Conclusion

McGilchrist explicitly disclaims exhaustiveness — the aim was to marshal the clearest available evidence to overturn "the entrenched prejudice that, while the right hemisphere may add a bit of colour to life, it is the left hemisphere that does the serious business." With the single major exception of explicit language-based manipulation and serial analysis (the subject of Chapter 3), **the left hemisphere is not the "dominant" hemisphere.** He notes, provocatively, that even those with the highest verbal and spatial ability, and indeed the highest intelligence generally, appear to rely more heavily on the right hemisphere. He offers a final formula for the whole chapter: if the left hemisphere is the hemisphere of **"what,"** the right hemisphere — preoccupied with context, relation, emotion, and nuance — is the hemisphere of **"how."** This, he suggests, explains why mainstream neuroscience (itself, in his view, a largely left-hemisphere enterprise) has focused on locating functions rather than on the qualitatively different *manner* in which each hemisphere performs them, missing the real significance of its own subject matter.

He restates the book's core opposition one last time: one way of being lets things "be present to us in all their embodied particularity, with all their changeability and impermanence, and their interconnectedness, as part of a whole which is forever in flux" — in which we feel *connected*, part of the world, not isolated observers of an objective world; the other steps outside the flow of experience to "re-present" the world in an explicit, abstracted, compartmentalised, fragmented, static, and ultimately lifeless form, more useful for manipulation, from which we feel *detached* but over which we feel *powerful*. **"The right hemisphere pays attention to the Other… the left hemisphere pays attention to the virtual world that it has created, which is self-consistent, but self-contained, ultimately disconnected from the Other."** McGilchrist is emphatic that this is not a brief for abandoning the left hemisphere: its contributions — language, systematic thought, "our talent for division" — are civilisation-making, "second only to our capacity to transcend it, in order to see the whole," and he states he is "passionately opposed" to any siren call to discard clarity and precision. His closing warning is instead about *service*: the left hemisphere's gifts "need to be made in the service of something else, that only the right hemisphere can bring. Alone they are destructive. And right now they may be bringing us close to forfeiting the civilisation they helped to create."
