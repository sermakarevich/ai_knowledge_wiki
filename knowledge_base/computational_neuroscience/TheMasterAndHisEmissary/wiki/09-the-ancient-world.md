# Chapter 8: The Ancient World

Opening Part Two's historical argument, McGilchrist proposes that ancient Greece saw a genuine advance in *both* hemispheres at once, not a simple triumph of one over the other: an initial expansion of bilateral frontal-lobe function created "necessary distance" from the world, which simultaneously forced a huge expansion of the left hemisphere (writing, law, money, philosophy, the concept of the abstract individual) and freed the right hemisphere to deepen empathy, the sense of self, and expressive art (Homeric poetry, tragedy, portraiture). Tracing this through archaic Homeric Greece, the pre-Socratics, Plato, and finally Rome, he argues that the balance—vividly harmonious in the classical "birth of tragedy"—tips decisively and irreversibly toward the left hemisphere by the fourth century BC, a leftward drift that Rome's legalism, bureaucracy, and increasingly abstract, standardised art then carries to its logical, ultimately unsustainable, extreme.

## Faces, gaze, and "necessary distance"

- The chapter opens with Milton Brener's *Faces: The Changing Look of Humankind*, which studies the evolution of facial portrayal in antiquity. Since some 90 per cent of emotional communication is non-verbal and mostly conveyed through the face (Georg Lichtenberg called it "the most entertaining surface on earth"), its virtual absence in prehistoric art is telling: figures are mostly headless, or show only pelvis/buttocks/breasts, and depict "what we know rather than what we see" (e.g. X-ray-style images of bones inside the body)—a style Brener compares to the productions of neuropsychiatric patients relying on the left hemisphere alone, and which dyslexic and prosopagnosic patients (both with right-hemisphere deficits) prefer.
- From the sixth century BC, and especially the fourth, Greek portrayal of the face shifts rapidly from the abstracted, inexpressive gaze of Egyptian and early Greek art toward individualised, emotionally expressive, empathic portraiture—which Brener attributes to a rapid advance in right-hemisphere functioning.
- Supporting evidence cited: **Hans-Joachim Hufschmidt**'s 1980 study of the direction of gaze in **50,000 portrayals** of the human face found that between the sixth century BC and the Hellenistic period, portraits shift from facing the viewer's right to mostly facing the viewer's left—paralleling **Chris McManus and Nick Humphrey**'s 1973 *Nature* study of roughly 1,400 Western portraits (16th–20th century), which found the same left-facing bias, strongest in the 15th century and waning through the 20th century back toward equal right/left profiles. McGilchrist connects this to his broader thesis of a rightward shift at the Renaissance and a leftward one in modernism. Children's spontaneous face drawings still show the "natural" leftward bias; self-portraits show the opposite bias (mirror orientation); a study of painter **Lovis Corinth**'s self-portraits before and after his 1911 right-hemisphere stroke shows he reversed both facial orientation and light-source direction afterward.
- McGilchrist agrees with the right-hemisphere evidence but disputes Brener's straightforward "right hemisphere advances while left lags" framing. Greek civilisation also brought "the beginnings of analytic philosophy, the codification of laws, the formalisation of systematic bodies of knowledge"—unmistakably left-hemisphere achievements arising at the very same time.
- His alternative account: an initial **symmetrical, bihemispheric advance in the frontal lobes**, which supply distance (in space) and delay (in time)—the capacity to "stand back from our world, and from ourselves." This capacity necessitates a huge expansion of the **written word**, diagrams, maps, and records—all left-hemisphere-mediated—to fix and freeze experience. "Such standing back is the essence of analytic philosophy, which is a left-hemisphere function—at least philosophy in the West since Plato and up to the time of Kant." But the same necessary distance also acts as "midwife" to the right hemisphere's expansion: it lets us see the human face and the individual self objectively, enabling the empathy, humour, irony, and expressive art Brener documents.
- Why does hemisphere-difference talk matter if both sides advance together? Because the advance runs in "two diametrically opposed directions at once—towards greater abstraction from the world and, simultaneously, towards greater empathic engagement with the world," accentuating hemispheric divergence rather than reflecting one undifferentiated cognitive advance. McGilchrist speculates Greece may mark the origin, or at least the first clear cultural evidence, of hemisphere specialisation "as we now know it."

## Julian Jaynes and the bicameral mind

- McGilchrist engages **Julian Jaynes**'s *The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind* (then over 30 years old), which argues introspective self-awareness first arose in Homeric Greece: when Iliad heroes (and Old Testament figures) hear the voices of gods, Jaynes takes this literally—their own intuitive (right-hemisphere) thoughts, previously unconscious, were experienced as external voices because the "bicameral" mind was breaking down, with the left hemisphere gaining sudden, disconcerting access to the right's workings. He likens this to auditory hallucination in schizophrenia.
- McGilchrist thinks Jaynes "was near to making a breakthrough—did in fact make one" but got the direction backwards, partly because he relied on an outdated, psychoanalytic view of schizophrenia as regression to a "primitive" mental state. In fact, the evidence suggests schizophrenia is a relatively modern disease (possibly only since the 18th century) marked by "a sort of misplaced hyper-rationalism, a hyper-reflexive self-awareness, and a disengagement from emotion and embodied existence"—the opposite of primitive regression.
- McGilchrist's inversion: the phenomena Jaynes describes arose not from a breakdown/opening of the door between hemispheres, but from a **relative separation of the two hemispheres**—a *closing* of the door. What had been "simply subsumed into action without the necessity of deliberation" became objectified: intuitions no longer felt as unquestionably "mine" were experienced as alien, other, "familiar but alien, wise but uncanny—in a word, divine."
- This separation had both gains and costs: it enabled a "necessary distance" from the everyday frame of reference, deepening empathy (his prime example is the rise of Greek drama, "in which the thoughts and feelings of our selves and of others are apparently objectified, and yet returned to us as our own"), but it also "sowed the seeds of left-hemisphere isolationism." At this stage the two hemispheres still worked largely together, so benefits still outweighed costs—but the imbalance would grow.

## Archaic Greece: the embodied Homeric self

- The Homeric epics (*Iliad*, *Odyssey*), possibly reaching written form around the second half of the eighth century BC, show a highly evolved right hemisphere in their unified narrative, empathy, and insight into character—yet they contain remarkably few descriptions of faces (despite the right hemisphere's specialisation for the face), which McGilchrist attributes to the "relative closeness of the point of view": attending to the face requires a degree of detached observation not yet present.
- Homer has no word for "body," "soul," or "mind" *in the living person*: *sōma* denoted only the corpse left on the battlefield, *psuchē* only what left the dying warrior's lips. Mental life is spoken of via bodily organs—Achilles "consulting his *thumos*." Drawing on **Michael Clarke**'s *Flesh and Spirit in the Songs of Homer*:
  > "rather this thought and consciousness are as inseparable a part of his bodily life as are movement and metabolism."
  Homeric thought is conceived as breath mingling with blood and bodily fluids—flow, melting, coagulation—rather than as a fixed, static thing; "the relationship between the bodily and mental identity of these entities is subtle and elusive."
- Words in the *thumos* "family" (*is*, *aiōn*, *menos*) range fluidly between actor and activity, thing and process—*menos* means force/strength but also semen, and "the force of violent self-propelled motion in something non-human" (echoing Scheler's *Drang*). "The life of Homeric man is defined in terms of processes more precisely than of things," per Clarke.
- By contrast, ***nous*** (or *noos*)—closer to the modern, disembodied idea of "mind"—is rare in Homer and, when it occurs, is already intellectual and non-bodily: "it may be virtually identified with a plan or stratagem," and is "like the flight of an arrow, directional"—i.e., left-hemisphere-congruent. By the late fifth/fourth century, separate concepts of body and soul were "firmly fixed," culminating in Plato's *Phaedo*, where the soul is a prisoner awaiting death's liberation.
- Jaynes noted Homer has no word or concept for "will." McGilchrist finds this too modern a demand: **Christopher Gill**'s analysis shows the Homeric self bound up in "interpersonal and communal dialogue," so that "my" thoughts need not be consciously known as mine; **Michael Clarke**'s "double plane of causation" holds that sudden thoughts/emotions are experienced simultaneously as divine intervention and as one's own psychology, without contradiction—"my" will combined left-hemisphere conscious striving with right-hemisphere intuitive attraction to values, "represented by the voices of the gods." (Bruno Snell's *The Discovery of the Mind* is cited as a companion study of this evolving self-consciousness.)
- Greek vocabulary for **seeing** was, before this era, strikingly rich and relational rather than abstract: Snell counts at least nine Homeric verbs, e.g. *derkesthai* (a fierce, resting glance, as of an eagle's "sharp" gaze), *paptainein* (looking about inquisitively or fearfully), *leussein* (to see something bright, with "pride, joy and a feeling of freedom," typically first-person), *theasthai* (to gaze in astonishment), *ossesthai* (to have a threatening impression, "to suspect"). Only later were the parts of the generic verb "to see" (*horan*, *idein*, *opsesthai*) unified—originally "there were only words for relations with things ... how the 'seer' stood towards the 'seen'," not yet detached observation.
- ***Theōrein*** (root of "theory")—the eye simply apprehending an object—is a much later word, a back-formation from *theōros* ("spectator"), marking a new, more detached "special situation." The prominence of *theōrein* and *noein* after the Homeric era, alongside the *thymos* (instinct/emotion, body-bound) vs. *noos* (reflection, ideas) distinction, shows the Greeks already "making felt distinctions between thought and experience as mediated by the left hemisphere and as mediated by the right."

## Classical Greece: from the pre-Socratics to tragedy

- Around the sixth century BC comes what is conventionally called the birth of philosophy (Bertrand Russell: "philosophy begins with Thales"). McGilchrist locates its common root in "an intellectual sense of wonder at the sheer fact of existence" and a conviction that ordinary ways of construing the world are mistaken—an awareness of "radical inauthenticity" born of newly achieved distance. This could lead either back toward right-hemisphere "presencing" of experience, or further away from the senses into pure LH-mediated contemplation; McGilchrist argues Greek thought does both, in sequence, moving progressively toward the left.
- **Thales** of Miletus (early 6th c. BC), Russell's "first philosopher," predicted a solar eclipse (585 BC) and held water was the *archē* (originary principle) of all things.
- **Anaximander** proposed the *archē* was the "unbounded"/"indefinite" (*apeiron*)—something unqualifiable, without beginning or end, eternally generative rather than a static origin point, and uniquely able to contain opposing principles without their mutual annihilation. Opposites within it "pay retribution to one another": "When things perish, they return whence they come to be, in accordance with necessity, for they give to each other recompense for their injustice according to the ordinance of time." McGilchrist reads Anaximander's emphasis on process-over-thing, and the primacy of the indefinite, as right-hemisphere insight, even while the philosophical project of systematising it may itself be left-hemisphere.
- **Heraclitus** of Ephesus (flourished late 6th c. BC), a wealthy aristocrat contemptuous of the *dēmos*, deposited his book at the temple of Artemis rather than circulate it (per Diogenes Laertius). McGilchrist treats him as the pre-Socratic who most fully embodies right-hemisphere-primacy balance:
  > "He who does not expect will not find out the unexpected, for it is trackless and unexplored."
  > "Nature loves to hide."
  > "War is father of all, and king of all" — the creative power of opposition.
  > "The name of the bow (*biós*) is life (*bíos*); its work is death."
  > "One cannot step twice into the same river."
  Heraclitus held that truth "neither declares nor conceals, but gives a sign" (Charles Kahn: the obscurity of his style mirrors the obscurity of the nature of things—"to speak plainly ... would be to falsify it in the telling"). Logos for Heraclitus is not a hidden power behind appearances but a "phenomenal property," experienced, not merely inferred; he insists experience trumps opinion ("whatever comes from sight, hearing, learning from experience: this I prefer") while stressing that eyes and ears are "poor witnesses for men if their souls do not understand the language." His central image of opposites-in-tension (the bow/lyre's *harmoniē*) and eternal flux ("all things flow"; "even the potion separates unless it is stirred") culminate, for McGilchrist, in the term *anchibasiē*, "stepping near"—which he calls the perfect characterisation of the right hemisphere's approach to truth as against the left's.
- **Parmenides** of Elea (early 5th c. BC), a priest of Apollo and teacher of Zeno, argued the opposite: phenomenal experience is deception, and "thought and being are the same thing." Since motion would require movement into the void (a logical impossibility), motion and change are illusions; only timeless, unchanging stasis truly exists. McGilchrist reads this "prioritising of a logical system over truth to phenomena" as pure left-hemisphere allegiance, and notes the self-undermining consequence Plato later draws out in the *Parmenides* and *Sophist*: if we are wholly cut off from the realm of ideas, "we do not participate in knowledge itself"—so philosophers cannot know anything either. (He is sceptical of Heidegger's claim that Heraclitus and Parmenides ultimately agree, calling it "a sort of sleight of hand.")
- Despite this, Parmenides deeply influenced **Plato**, whose doctrine of the eternal **Forms** holds that unchanging universal categories (the "ideal table") are more real than the changing particular objects of sense-experience, which are mere shadows/copies. Plato's pupil **Aristotle** partly reversed this—finding the universal *in and through* particular instances, as "a true scientist"—but Aristotle's own works were later "frozen" for 1,500 years into an unquestionable authority, "rendering his thought about experience, provisional as it was, static, unchanging, and idealised as infallible, until the Renaissance."
- Bruno Snell's point that Greek's invention of the definite article let an adjective (*kalos*, "beautiful") become an abstract noun (*to kalon*, "the beautiful") is cited as a structural feature of the language itself that "favoured abstraction and idealisation"—an inversion whereby the purely conceptual comes to seem more real than what is experienced. Plato's *Republic* is quoted on astronomy:
  > "The stars that decorate the sky ... are far inferior, just because they are visible, to the true realities ... We shall therefore treat astronomy, like geometry, as setting us problems for solution, and ignore the visible heavens..."
- **Nietzsche**'s harsher reading (via *The Twilight of the Idols*) casts Socrates as culture's "first degenerate," having lost the aristocratic capacity to trust intuition ("Honest men do not carry their reasons exposed in this fashion"); dialectic is "chosen only when one has no other means," and the Greek turn to hyper-rationality betrays "a desperate situation... either to perish or—to be absurdly rational." McGilchrist backs this with a quote from neuroscientist **Panksepp**, warning that "we humans are creatures that can be deceived as easily by logical rigour as by blind faith."
- In the *Republic*, Plato/Socrates has poets and artists banished: their work is "far removed from reality," appeals to "a low element in the mind," has "a terrible power to corrupt even the best characters"; metaphor becomes, in a "clever inversion," a lie rather than a mode of truth; music is heavily restricted (only two permitted kinds). Yet Plato himself relies on myth to convey what resists dialectic (the Cave, the ring of Gyges), and the *Symposium* hints the Forms of the Good and the Beautiful pull us in a way that "transcends the logical." Awaiting death, Socrates' daemon told him, repeatedly, to make music—which Nietzsche reads as "his Apolline insight" and "the only hint of any scruples in him about the limits of logical nature." McGilchrist's verdict: "it is ultimately the left-hemisphere version of the world that Plato puts forward, for the first time in history; puts forward so strongly that it has taken two thousand years to shake it off."
- The corruption of **myth** into "myths" in the modern pejorative sense (false histories) is contrasted with **Malinowski**'s account of myth's true function—"a statement of a primeval, greater, and more relevant reality... the motive for ritual and moral actions"—and **Kerényi**'s description of mythology as "the play of the gods, in which they approach us." When myths instead become surrogate science (illustrated by Plato's *Timaeus* creation-myth of the rolling, limbless head, and by **Empedocles**' image of disembodied heads, arms, and eyes "wandering ... seeking for union" and joining by chance), McGilchrist reads this as the mind coming to picture the body itself as "an assemblage of separate bits ... put together by chance"—unmistakably a left-hemisphere fantasy.
- **Drama and Prometheus.** Greek tragedy is presented as the domain where opposites irreconcilable in explicit philosophical discourse are reconciled through myth. Nietzsche's account of "the tragic chorus" as "the original phenomenon of drama"—"this experience of seeing oneself transformed before one's eyes and acting as if one had really entered another body"—frames tragedy as the site of the West's first fully realised empathy. Athens held a special cult of **Prometheus**, "the god of technical skill and intelligence (though not of wisdom)," who stole fire from Zeus and gave humanity hope of resisting its destruction—an explicit image, McGilchrist notes, of "the emissary taking to himself the power of the Master." In Aeschylus's *Prometheus Bound*, Prometheus claims credit for civilising humanity:
  > "I invented for them Number, chief of all devices, and how to set down words in writing, Memory's handmaid, and mother of the Muses..."
  **Hermes**—"the Master's emissary" in the play, sent by Zeus to torment Prometheus—is in some versions of the myth Prometheus's alter ego, also credited with fire, also god of weights/measures, merchants, and tricksters, paralleling the Egyptian **Thoth** (whom Plato's *Phaedrus* blames for inventing writing). Kerényi calls Prometheus "a cheat and a thief," whose theft "brings immeasurable and unforeseen misfortune."
  **Aeschylus** (generally credited as tragedy's founder, per **A. W. Schlegel**: "Aeschylus is to be considered as the creator of Tragedy; in full panoply she sprung from his head, like Pallas from the head of Jupiter") fought at Marathon and Salamis (his epitaph names the former battle, not his plays), was an initiate at Eleusis who nearly lost his life for allegedly revealing the mysteries in the *Eumenides*, and reportedly began writing tragedy after a dream-visitation from Dionysus (per Pausanias). Sophocles said of him, "Aeschylus does what is right without knowing it"—which McGilchrist calls "a clearer statement of his debt to the workings of the right hemisphere" than could be found anywhere else.
  The Delphic maxim *Gnothi seauton* ("know thyself"), delivered through an intoxicated priestess interpreting ambiguous utterance, is likened to "a self-revealing Rorschach blot," comparable to the *I Ching*. McGilchrist calls Prometheus's story "a tale of two hemispheres"—the mind coming to know itself "without knowing it," the birth of self-consciousness through the paradox of tragic hubris.

## The written word

- McGilchrist identifies four elements in the history of writing, each moving the balance of power further toward the left hemisphere: (1) pictograms → phonograms, (2) syllabic writing → phonetic alphabet, (3) the inclusion of vowels, (4) the direction of writing—all completed in Greece by the fourth century BC.
- **Pictograms to phonograms**: the earliest writing appeared in Sumer c. 3300 BC as pictograms, giving way to more schematic ideograms, then to phonograms—arbitrary signs tied only to sound, no longer even schematically resembling the referent—"moves writing further into the territory of the left hemisphere." Egyptian writing (from c. 3100 BC) used all three forms side by side.
- **Syllabic to phonemic**: syllabic languages (Chinese; or consonantal scripts like Hebrew/Arabic, where changing vowels changes meaning) rely on context to disambiguate, favouring the right hemisphere; purely phonemic languages (Greek, Latin, English) resolve meaning through linear, unidirectional sequence, favouring the left. All alphabetic systems trace to Proto-Canaanite (2000–1500 BC) and Akkadian cuneiform phonograms (from 1500 BC); the Greek alphabet derives from, and is nearly identical to, the Phoenician—but mirror-reversed, probably from around the ninth century BC.
- **Vowels**: their inclusion, first in the Greek alphabet's adaptation of Phoenician script, "further consolidated a shift in the balance of hemispheric power, removing the last unconscious processing strategies from context-based to sequence-based coding."
- **Direction of writing**: the right hemisphere prefers vertical text read top-down; the left prefers horizontal text (read bottom-up if vertical). Nearly all writing systems began vertical, and vertical writing had disappeared in the West by c. 1100 BC. Greek was written horizontally but right-to-left until the seventh century BC; between the eighth and sixth centuries it used ***boustrophedon*** ("as the ox ploughs")—alternating direction line by line; left-to-right became the norm by the fifth century and universal by the fourth. Citing **de Kerckhove**: "95% of phonological orthographies that include markings of vocalic sounds ... are written towards the right, whereas almost all the systems that do not include letters for vowels are written towards the left." De Kerckhove also credits the Greek system's alphabetic abstraction with "the atomisation of speech," which McGilchrist links to **Democritus**'s parallel atomisation of the physical universe; word-spacing itself was not standardised until the Byzantine period.
- McGilchrist explicitly rejects the "strong" literacy thesis of **Ernest Havelock** and **John Skoyles** (that the alphabet's structure itself drove Greek cognitive change), arguing instead that the nature of writing is an *effect*, not a cause, of a deeper hemispheric shift—since non-Western, right-hemisphere-favouring languages (Hebrew, Arabic) are now, in the modern globalised world, processed predominantly by the left hemisphere anyway; Japanese kanji and kana are both processed principally in the left hemisphere (in different regions), and most Chinese processing is left-hemisphere too, though reading Chinese aloud still activates more right-hemisphere network than English does.
- Writing's early function, per **Claude Hagège** ("Writing is an instrument of power; it enables the sending of orders to far-off fiefdoms") and **Fernand Braudel** (early Sumerian tablets are "simply inventories and accounts"; the deciphered Mycenaean **Linear B** records only "palace accounts"), was administrative control—"Empires and organised societies extending over space are the children of writing." McGilchrist suggests this utilitarian character may be peculiar to the West: Hagège notes Chinese writing's origin was "magicoreligious and divinatory rather than economic and mercantile."

## Money, trade, and the meeting of Greece and the East

- Money, like writing, "replaces things with signs or tokens, with representations"—the essence of left-hemisphere activity. **Richard Seaford** argues monetary currency requires an antithesis of sign and substance (the sign becomes decisive over the tangible reality), a formulation McGilchrist notes bears "an uncanny resemblance to Plato's theory of Forms"; he treats both the alphabet (Skoyles) and money (Seaford) not as prime movers of Greek thought but as parallel epiphenomena of a deeper hemispheric shift.
- In Homer, gold and silver are aristocratic gifts tied to deity and immortality, not money—unformed precious metal even carried negative connotations. Pre-monetary exchange emphasised reciprocity: gifts were unique, uncalculated, and valuable for the relationship they sustained. Trade replaces this with instantaneous, competitive, equivalence-based exchange focused on utility/profit; money "homogenises its objects and its users, eroding uniqueness," weakens the need for trust, and "breeds an unlimited greed."
- The first true coins (electrum) appeared c. 685 BC in Lydia, Croesus's realm, though most specialists date a true monetary economy to the fourth century BC and the Hellenistic period. Braudel calls both alphabet and currency "accelerators of change," and traces early Greek exposure to eastern luxury and ideas to the trading post of **Al-Mina** (probably founded 9th century BC, in contact with the Mycenaean world since the 14th century BC)—"the city where Greece met the east," where the Greeks encountered the Phoenician alphabet and where the "orientalising phase" of Greek art began, alongside "the first elements of Greek science," superstitions, and the beginnings of Dionysiac cult.
- Currency, like writing, remained "ambivalently poised" between the hemispheres from the seventh to fifth centuries, only tipping decisively left (widespread use) by the fourth century—coinciding with the pre-Socratics ceding to Plato.
- Reaching back to the Mycenaean world (mid-second millennium BC to c. 1100 BC), McGilchrist reads its art as harmony between the hemispheres: Egyptian "mythology of dread" gives way to Mycenaean "lightness and mirth," and relaxed, egalitarian relationships between men and between men and women appear for the first time in Mycenaean Crete. Braudel is quoted at length on the profusion of naturalistic plant and animal motifs on Mycenaean vases and frescoes. Greek **mimesis**—naturalistic representation—is contrasted with Egyptian convention, where (per **Gombrich**) skin tone followed a fixed code for men versus women regardless of the actual person portrayed.
- "Necessary distance" is named as the mediator of all these benign developments, "what actually brings one into connection with that from which one is appropriately distanced; it is not a distancing that separates." This is illustrated by the Athenians' relationship to their land: quoting Braudel and **Edouard Will**, the polis was inconceivable without its surrounding territory, and during the Peloponnesian War, Athenians retreating to the Pelargicon walls would "watch the enemy arriving in the distance." Their love of native soil—expressed using *himeros*, the word for sexual desire—was taken to an extreme unmatched elsewhere in history, "with the result that love could yield only to hatred."

## The later period: physiognomy, achievement, and Plato's turn

- Braudel (following de Selincourt) held that "everything worthwhile [in Greek culture] had been accomplished" by the time of Plato and Aristotle; Nietzsche located the true peak earlier still, in the age of Aeschylus, before Apollo's "triumph of rationality" and utilitarian democracy set in as symptoms of Greek civilisation's ageing, foreshadowing the modern West. McGilchrist broadly endorses this without fully embracing Nietzsche's Apollonian pessimism.
- Yet the later period also brought positive developments: as distance from self and others deepened, focus shifted to individual uniqueness as expressed in the face. Homer's and tragedy's characters are archetypal, even masked—not through lack of empathy, but through "the degree of fusion between self and other" typical of the archaic period (per Gill). Sculpture, by contrast, creates an image of a person "from the outside," and it is there that facial empathy first becomes explicit.
- **Physiognomy**—reading character from facial features—developed as "a theoretical concern in the philosophy of antiquity" from Phaedo of Elis (4th c. BC) through Aristotle's school to Galen (2nd c. AD); **Polemon of Laodicea**'s *Physiognomy* (2nd century AD) devotes its first of two books entirely to the eye. Around AD 130, sculpture shifted from painted to incised, engraved pupils, expanding expressive range. **Pliny the Elder** records that the painter **Apelles** (4th c. BC) achieved likenesses so exact that a physiognomist reportedly could tell "how long the sitter had to live or had already lived" from the portrait alone.
- The period of peak portrait expressiveness—fourth century BC to second century AD—required not just empathy but generations of accumulated **mimetic skill**, particularly mastery of the complex, independently innervated muscles around the eyes; the underlying empathic sensibility is already visible earlier, in sixth-century-BC lyric poets **Alcaeus, Sappho, and Anacreon**.
- The chapter lists Greece's parallel left-hemisphere-mediated achievements, explicitly framed as evidence of the left hemisphere's *proper*, beneficial role as emissary rather than usurper: "a legal constitution and a body of laws; philosophy; the invention of the idea of, and the study of, history; the formalisation of geographic knowledge and the study of maps; the structuring of a system of education; the invention of the orders of architecture; systematic description of the human body, and of the animal world; geometry; and theories of physics."
  > "these ... demonstrate the power for good that the left hemisphere wields when it acts as the emissary of the right hemisphere, and has not yet come to believe itself the Master."
- Plato is presented as the pivot where this balance tips: his works, though lost to the West for over a millennium (recovered via Arabic translation before the Renaissance), fixed the belief "that truth is in principle knowable, that it is knowable through reason alone, and that all truths are consistent with one another." The chapter's Plato material (Forms, the *Republic* on astronomy and on banishing poets, Nietzsche's and Panksepp's critiques, Plato's own reliance on myth) is detailed above under "Classical Greece."

## The Romans

- Rome's literary golden age, the Augustan era (1st century BC), produced Virgil, Horace, Ovid, Propertius, and Catullus within roughly fifty years of one another—McGilchrist reads their psychological sophistication, and especially Virgil's *amor*, *pietas*, and elegiac pity ("*sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt*"—there are tears for things, and mortal matters touch the mind), as evidence of right-hemisphere-led alliance between the hemispheres. Ovid's *Metamorphoses* is explicitly Heraclitean in theme (flux, endless transformation), quoted at length: "Nothing remains the same: the great renewer, / Nature, makes form from form, and, oh, believe me / That nothing ever dies..."
- Despite this artistic flourishing, Roman civilisation shows an advance toward "ever more rigidly systematised ways of thinking, suggestive of the left hemisphere working alone." As imperial power grew alongside military and administrative success, "the Apollonian left hemisphere begins to freewheel." Braudel describes Roman towns as deliberately uniform, "shaped in [Rome's] own image," faithfully replicating "a model which hardly changed over time and place"; even Rome's celebrated originality in realistic portraiture is traced back to eastern (ultimately Greek) origins. In comedy, Aristophanes' wild, imaginative Old Comedy gives way to Roman New Comedy's predictable stereotypes, descended from **Theophrastus**'s character "types" (the same Theophrastus who, given Aristotle's remark that one swallow doesn't make a summer, dutifully wrote a treatise on precisely how many swallows it would take).
- **Roman law** is singled out as Rome's genuine intellectual triumph ("Without a doubt, Rome's intelligence and genius came into its own in this area," per Braudel)—essential to administering a far-flung empire, but a body of rules that "could only increase over the ages." By the second century AD, per **Charles Freeman**, the Empire had "reached the height of its maturity," relatively peaceful and intellectually free. It did not last: increasing bureaucracy, totalitarianism, and mechanisation in the late Roman period, McGilchrist suggests, represent the left hemisphere's attempt "to 'go it alone.'"
- Roman **architecture**—"the domain in which Rome most rapidly developed its own personality" (Braudel), enabled by the invention of concrete—is McGilchrist's central case study, via **Hans Peter L'Orange**'s *Art Forms and Civic Life in the Late Roman Empire*. Classical form had grown organically "from deep within the object itself" (temples individually oriented, standing with "peculiar recalcitrance" beside one another); under the late Empire's increasingly centralised bureaucracy this gave way to "homogeneity and uniformity," an "unchangeable, firmly crystallised order," with reused building spoils (*spolia*) piled in indiscriminately and column bases even reused as capitals.
- The human **face** undergoes a parallel collapse: portraiture remained lifelike and individual until the end of the third century AD, when (per L'Orange) it shifted abruptly to a "peculiarly abstract," distant gaze—"the features suddenly stiffen in an expressive Medusa-like mask." A technical shift from chisel to running drill contributed to a new harshness and flatness:
  > "There is a movement away from lifelike nature to abstract types, from plastic articulation to conceptual generalisation, from the corporeal to the symbolic ... it is as if the natural objects flee from living perception."
  This shift saw no reversal until the Renaissance: emperors and saints alike become interchangeable symbolic types, the body is treated as corrupt (martyrs and ascetics replacing classical heroes), and myth/metaphor become "an opaque shell of lies." Classical sculptural composition, once an "organic, living group," is by the third century AD "shattered" into overlapping, marionette-like tangles, with L'Orange summarising the whole shift as one "from the complex towards the simple, from the mobile towards the static, from the dialectic and relative towards the dogmatic and the authoritarian, from the empirical towards theology and theosophy."
- **Christianity's role** is debated via **Charles Freeman**'s *The Closing of the Western Mind*, which blames the loss of Greek intellectual openness on Christianity's rise—Constantine's Edict of Milan (313) integrating the Church with state and secular power, and Theodosius's Nicene decree (381) outlawing paganism and enforcing Trinitarian orthodoxy, stifling debate. McGilchrist complicates this via **Mary Beard**'s review, which calls Freeman's "stark opposition between the classical and Christian worlds" the real problem: the Christian world was "positively overflowing with intellectual and rational argument"—just directed at theology rather than the natural world. McGilchrist's own gloss is that what was lost was not reason but *concern with the lived world*, a tendency traceable to Plato himself (the *Republic*'s astronomy passage, and the "first, utterly joyless, authoritarian state" of the *Republic* and *Laws*)—"the passion is for control, for fixity, for certainty; and that comes not with religion alone, but with a certain cast of mind, the cast of the left hemisphere." Aristotle's genuinely empirical, provisional spirit likewise did not survive; his work too was later "frozen" into unquestioned authority.
- The Western Empire's fall: knowledge of Greek was lost in the West, with little mathematical or scientific advance between 500 and 1100 AD; Greek texts filtered back via Arabic translation from the tenth century, preserved paradoxically by a Church that both controlled and enabled the revival of learning. **Bryan Ward-Perkins**'s *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* catalogues 210 proposed causes; his own account (fiscal decline → an underfunded army → civil war → defeat by "barbarians" → collapse) is one McGilchrist finds "compelling" and not in conflict with his own thesis that "the change of mind had started anyway: it is evident in the fabric of the Empire itself."

## Conclusion

- McGilchrist's summary: the starting point was "necessary distance," probably via enhanced frontal-lobe function, which initially produced a period of unparalleled richness—Nietzsche's "union of Apollo with Dionysus," the birth of tragedy—achieved not by compromise or mutual restraint but by both hemispheres going further than ever before, simultaneously, in opposite directions.
- But across philosophy, ways of seeing the phenomenal world, conceptions of soul and body, poetry and drama, architecture and sculpture of the human face and form, and the evolution of the alphabet and of currency, the balance of power shifts steadily and consistently in one direction: "the left hemisphere (Apollo) gradually coming to win the day."
- Final framing image: Greece and Rome together show the left hemisphere (the emissary) expanding the "empire of the mind" precisely by working *in concert* with the right hemisphere (the Master)—faithfully returning its gains to be reintegrated into a richer whole. The empire (not merely the Roman one, "which the world could do without," but the empire of mind more broadly) thrives only as long as this reciprocity holds; the chapter breaks off mid-sentence on the warning that once the left hemisphere comes to believe its own dominion is everything, and hoards the wealth it creates rather than returning it to the right hemisphere's world, the empire cannot be sustained.

## Recap for synthesis
- **Thesis:** Ancient Greece began with a genuine, simultaneous advance of both hemispheres via expanded frontal-lobe "necessary distance," but the balance of power tipped steadily toward the left hemisphere from the sixth through fourth centuries BC, a leftward drift that Rome then carried to a rigid, ultimately unsustainable extreme.
- **Main ideas:**
  - **Necessary distance (frontal lobes)** — the shared root cause: distance in space and delay in time enable writing, law, and abstraction (left hemisphere) while simultaneously enabling empathy, individuality, and expressive art (right hemisphere) — the two "diametrically opposed directions" of Greek advance.
  - **Hemispheric separation, not fusion, explains the "voices of the gods"** — McGilchrist inverts Julian Jaynes's bicameral-mind thesis: intuitions became "alien" voices because the hemispheres were pulling apart, not merging.
  - **The pre-Socratic arc from Heraclitus to Parmenides to Plato** — a real philosophical trajectory from right-hemisphere-congruent attention to flux, paradox, and phenomenal experience (Heraclitus) toward left-hemisphere-congruent logical abstraction and distrust of the senses (Parmenides, then decisively Plato's Forms).
  - **Writing, the alphabet, and money as parallel, mutually reinforcing leftward shifts** — pictograms to phonograms, syllables to a vowelled phonetic alphabet, right-to-left to left-to-right writing, and gift-exchange to abstract currency all replace context-bound, embodied meaning with arbitrary, sequential signs — treated as symptoms, not causes, of the underlying hemispheric shift.
  - **Rome as the leftward endpoint** — law, bureaucracy, standardised architecture, and increasingly abstract, mask-like portraiture (per L'Orange) show the left hemisphere "going it alone," a shift that predates and outlasts the specific causes (Christianity, fiscal collapse) usually blamed for Rome's fall.
- **Notable quotes:**
  - "War is father of all, and king of all." — Heraclitus, on the generative power of opposites.
  - "for thought and being are the same thing." — Parmenides, denying reality to the changing phenomenal world.
  - "these ... demonstrate the power for good that the left hemisphere wields when it acts as the emissary of the right hemisphere, and has not yet come to believe itself the Master." — McGilchrist, on Greece's parallel legal/scientific/historical achievements.
  - "it is as if the natural objects flee from living perception." — L'Orange, on the abrupt turn to abstract, mask-like Roman portraiture after c. AD 300.
- **Key examples/references:** Milton Brener (*Faces*) and Hans-Joachim Hufschmidt's gaze-direction study; Julian Jaynes's bicameral-mind thesis; Michael Clarke (*Flesh and Spirit in the Songs of Homer*) and Bruno Snell (*The Discovery of the Mind*) on the embodied Homeric self; Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, and Parmenides; Plato's *Republic*/theory of Forms versus Nietzsche's critique of Socrates; Aeschylus's *Prometheus Bound* and the Prometheus/Hermes myth; the history of the alphabet (Havelock, Skoyles, de Kerckhove) and of coinage (Richard Seaford); Fernand Braudel throughout on trade, writing, and Roman institutions; Hans Peter L'Orange on late Roman art; Charles Freeman versus Mary Beard on Christianity and the "closing" of the Western mind; Bryan Ward-Perkins on Rome's fall.
- **Critical angle:** McGilchrist's central causal move — that writing, currency, and even literacy's specific structural features are mere "epiphenomena" or "symptoms" of a prior, otherwise unevidenced shift in hemispheric balance, rather than plausible drivers in their own right (contra Havelock, Skoyles, and Seaford, whose causal arguments he acknowledges are serious but sets aside) — is asserted through repeated interpretive readings of texts, art, and etymology rather than independently tested; the neuroscience (face-gaze studies, hemispheric lateralisation of language) establishes correlation with historical periods but the chapter offers no independent evidence that a "hemispheric shift" preceded, rather than resulted from, the social-technological changes (writing, coinage, law) it's invoked to explain.
