# Chapter 12: The Modern and Post-Modern Worlds

The final chapter of Part Two argues that Modernism and Post-Modernism are not a rupture with the past but the culmination of a centuries-long "slippage" toward left-hemisphere domination, now become total and self-undermining. McGilchrist takes Virginia Woolf's claim that "on or about December 1910 human character changed" as his starting point, then builds a sustained, multi-stranded case — sociological (disenchantment, disembedding, loss of belonging), clinical (a systematic parallel between the phenomenology of schizophrenia and the stated aims of modernist art, literature, philosophy and criticism), epidemiological (rising rates of schizophrenia, anorexia, multiple personality and autism tracking industrialisation and urbanisation), and art-historical (movement-by-movement readings of Cubism, Futurism, Dadaism, Surrealism, serialism) — that the modern world increasingly instantiates the world as it would appear to a mind whose right hemisphere has been silenced. Post-Modernism, in his account, is not a corrective to Modernism but its most extreme expression: where the Enlightenment mistook its left-hemisphere simulacrum of the world for reality itself, Post-Modernism denies that any reality exists to be mistaken about — the left hemisphere's "unworlding of the world" completed.

## The disenchantment of modernity

- Woolf: "All human relations have shifted... those between masters and servants, husbands and wives, parents and children. And when human relations change there is at the same time a change in religion, conduct, politics and literature." McGilchrist reads the oft-cited link to Roger Fry's 1910 "Manet and the Post-Impressionists" exhibition (Grafton Galleries, London) as too narrow — the change was "all-encompassing" — and argues the disjunction was less sudden than it appeared: "It was less an avalanche after unexpected snow than a landslide following years of erosion."
- Modernity's social disintegration is traced to the Industrial Revolution and to **Comte's** vision of society as an aggregation of atomistic individuals: rural-to-urban drift, breakdown of social order, scientific materialism and bureaucracy producing what **Max Weber** called "the disenchanted world," and capitalism/consumerism supplanting relationships of "felt connection and cultural continuity" with ones of "utility, greed, and competition."
- **Peter Berger** et al., *The Homeless Mind*: rationalistic, technical, bureaucratic thinking has destroyed the "sacred canopy" of shared meaning about life, death and the world, producing **anomie** and existential angst.
- **Anthony Giddens**, *Modernity and Self-Identity*: globalisation's "**disembedding mechanisms**" separate things from context and people from "**locale**" (uniqueness of place); real experience is replaced by symbolic tokens, local know-how by centralised "expert" systems; media promote fragmentation via random juxtaposition and the "intrusion of distant events into everyday consciousness."
- Belonging (from Old English *langian*, root of "longing") reflects a deep, even pre-human, attachment to place — some animals bond as strongly with nest sites as with mothers. Three modern forces attack it: constant mobility, the extreme pace of environmental change (consumption, transport, agriculture-as-business, urbanisation), and the fragmentation of communal bonds, "devastatingly and meticulously captured" in **Robert Putnam's** *Bowling Alone*.
- McGilchrist frames all this as evidence of "a world increasingly dominated by the left hemisphere, and increasingly antagonistic to what the right hemisphere might afford."
- On **Toulmin's** account of the 17th-century scientific revolution: Toulmin assumes social/religious/political conflict caused the era's "hungering for certainty," but McGilchrist inverts the causality — the humanist age before it had been just as wracked by uncertainty yet held a different attitude toward it. It was the *shift toward left-hemisphere values* that hardened positions into scientism and the Counter-Reformation. Toulmin further shows the 1920s–30s Vienna Circle's demand for "strict rationality" and universal scientific "method" as even more extreme than 17th-century rationalism — but where Toulmin attributes this to anxiety over Fascism and Stalinism, McGilchrist asks instead whether "Fascism and Stalinism were facets of the same mental world as modernism, both of them expressions of the deep structure of the left hemisphere's world."

## Modernism and schizophrenia: the core phenomenology

- McGilchrist recaps the clinical parallel between right-hemisphere damage and schizophrenia established earlier in the book: both show trouble with context, pragmatics, tone, facial expression, presupposition, Gestalt perception, intuition, metaphor, narrative and the natural flow of time (replaced by static moments — the **Zeitraffer phenomenon**); both show deficient sense of the reality/uniqueness of experience and a loss of common sense; both show narrowed, over-explicit, piecemeal attention in place of intuitive global apprehension. One schizophrenic patient (via Sass): "the world consists of tools, and... everything that we glance at has some utilization." Neuroimaging shows schizophrenics with excessive left-hemisphere activation where right-hemisphere activation is expected — even in olfaction (reduced right-hemisphere/rhinencephalon limbic connections, increased left-hemisphere activity) — and antipsychotics work partly by reducing dopaminergic transmission, on which the left hemisphere depends more heavily.
- **Louis Sass**, *The Paradoxes of Delusion: Wittgenstein, Schreber, and the Schizophrenic Mind*, draws parallels between Wittgenstein's philosophy of detached introspection and the psychotic memoirs of **Daniel Paul Schreber** (a German judge, *Memoirs of My Nervous Illness*). Sass's thesis: "madness... is the end-point of the trajectory [that] consciousness follows when it separates from the body and the passions, and from the social and practical world, and turns in upon itself." Wittgenstein: "staring is closely bound up with the whole puzzle of solipsism" — over-awareness alienates us from the world, others come to seem like automata rather than minds (as Descartes also found), echoing Descartes's own cogito. **Engagement reverses it**: as Sass puts it, "the very weight of the object, the resistance it offers to the hand, testify to its existence as something independent of will or consciousness" — recalling Johnson's refutation of Berkeley by kicking a stone: "I refute it thus."
- In *Madness and Modernism*, Sass draws systematic parallels between schizophrenic experience and modernist/postmodernist literature, visual art and criticism — not as value judgment, but as phenomenological description; he explicitly considers (in an appendix, "Neurobiological Considerations") that the underlying cause may be left-hemisphere over-reliance.
- Three interlinked core disturbances, per Sass, Giovanni Stanghellini, Josef Parnas, Dan Zahavi and others:
  - **Hyperconsciousness** — normally intuitive/unconscious elements become objects of detached attention, awareness becomes aware of itself recursively, producing paralysis even in walking ("I have to do everything step by step, nothing is automatic now"). Musil's Ulrich (*The Man Without Qualities*) finds keeping his body vertical in the street a tremendous effort from sheer over-attention. Chris Frith: the core abnormality in schizophrenia is "an awareness of automatic processes which are normally carried out below the level of consciousness."
  - **Loss of ipseity** — loss of the pre-reflective, grounding sense of self; the self must be reconstructed "after the fact," bodily feelings lose their "compelling immediacy," emotions lose directedness toward others and a personal future. This produces an oscillation between **omnipotence and impotence** (no self, or nothing but self) — Sass borrows Heidegger's term for the result: an **"unworlding" of the world**, loss of the overarching context that gives coherence, leaving the world fragmented and meaningless.

## Schizophrenia and modernist art

- The same pattern — hyperconsciousness, self-reflexive collapse — appears at large in modernist culture, not just clinical philosophy: a patient says, "my intellectual parts became the whole of me"; Kafka's diary describes introspection that "will suffer no idea to sink tranquilly to rest but must pursue each one into consciousness, only itself to become an idea, in turn to be pursued by renewed introspection" — a hall-of-mirrors effect destroying spontaneity.
- The **"stare"**: Sass notes schizophrenia-spectrum patients engage in "a kind of fixed, penetrating, over-focused stare that dissolves the more commonly recognised Gestalts in favour of their component parts." **Susan Sontag**: "Traditional art invites a look... [Modernist art] engenders a stare" — an act of alienation, control, or terrified helplessness rather than connection.
- Hyperconsciousness produces flight from body and emotion. Schizophrenics describe words as "an envelope emptied of content." **Antonin Artaud** (himself schizophrenic): "I can't even find anything that would correspond to feelings"; his "theatre of cruelty" was meant, he told Anaïs Nin, to be "like a shock treatment, galvanise, shock people into feeling" — paralleling self-harm used to relieve emotional numbness. Eliot's etherised patient in "Prufrock" is read as prophetic of modernism's anaesthetised state.
- Dehumanisation and loss of the active self appear either as impersonal subjectivism (Virginia Woolf's *The Waves*: "subjectivity without a subject") or extreme objectivism (Robbe-Grillet's "The Secret Room," a static, clinically detached description of a woman's corpse rendered in geometric terms — "Being is not so simple"). Sass's detailed comparison finds shared features: no cohesive narrative, dissolved character, disrupted space-time, broken causal relations, and severed symbol-referent (metaphor) relationships, plus an emphasis on **static objects over dynamic process** — mirroring the left hemisphere's preference for the inanimate and static.
- Disrupted narrative empties action of meaning: per Heidegger, "care" requires temporality directed toward a shared future and grounded in a coherent past. **Walter Benjamin**: "The art of storytelling is coming to an end... experience has fallen in value. And it looks as if it is continuing to fall into bottomlessness." McGilchrist's summary of modernism's core features: excess of consciousness/over-explicitness about what should stay intuitive; depersonalisation and alienation from body and empathy; disruption of context; fragmentation; loss of **"betweenness."**
- This instability between subjectivism and objectivism produces perspectivism/relativism and the belief there is "no true world" — Nietzsche: reality is "a perspectival appearance whose origin lies in us," though Nietzsche himself dreaded the consequence, warning of "that great blood-sucker, the spider scepticism" and that we are "the Don Juans of cognition" whose "knowledge will take its revenge on us, as ignorance exacted its revenge in the Middle Ages."
- **Aesthetic self-referentiality**: art becomes "a form of drama in which consciousness watches itself in action" (Valéry) — attention shifts to the surface (Greenberg's "flatness" of modernist painting) or to the mechanics of creation (Brechtian *Verfremdungseffekt*). Schizophrenics report a parallel loss of visual depth ("like a two-dimensional transparency"). Postmodernist self-reflexivity concentrates on language itself, denying existence beyond it — as **Erich Heller** writes of Nietzsche's "last philosopher": "Nothing speaks to him any more — except his own speech."

## Self-referentiality and the loss of meaning

- **Michael Gazzaniga** calls the left hemisphere "**the interpreter**" — locus of self-consciousness, volition, rationality. An interpreter should mediate, not originate; over-reliance turns it inward until "the interpreter begins to interpret — itself," producing Nietzsche's "speech about speech" and a lonely, self-enwrapped condition.
- Meaning requires "betweenness" with a real world referent; lacking it, both schizophrenics and moderns experience an oppressive, unfocused sense that "something is going on" without ever finding what — the root of **paranoia**, per Wittgenstein's example of a man convinced Chopin is a hidden language people won't explain to him.
- The same fixation can instead **empty** things of meaning entirely (they "lose their place in the order of things"): Alberto Moravia's *La Noia* (*Boredom*) describes staring at a tumbler until it becomes "an absurd object," boredom arising as "a malady affecting external objects... an almost instantaneous loss of vitality."
- **Devitalisation → boredom → sensationalism**: per **Patricia Spacks**, boredom as a named phenomenon began with 18th-century capitalism's "appetite for the new"; by 1990, **23% of French men and 31% of French women** reported being bored during lovemaking ("l'atrophie du désir"). **Anton van Zijderveld** (on cliché): under boredom, "speech becomes gross and hyperbolic, music loud and nervous, ideas giddy and fantastic, emotions limitless and shameless, actions bizarre and foolish." **Scheler** on the "culture of entertainment": "extremely merry things, viewed by extremely sad people who do not know what to do with them." McGilchrist attributes this cycle to an "unplugged" left hemisphere, cut off from right-hemisphere grounding, capable only of **novelty**, not true **newness** (which requires imagination) — hence *The Shock of the New* "should really have been entitled... *The Shock of the Novel*." Example of crude sensationalism-as-shock: Hermann Nitsch crucifying a dead lamb — "flogging a dead horse."
- **Erich Fromm** (*On Disobedience*) describes modern *homo consumens* — "organisation man," obsessed with things over people, flourishing equally under capitalist and communist bureaucracy; McGilchrist reads socialism/capitalism's antipathy as "little more than a farmyard scrap between two dogs over a bone," both materialist in the left-hemisphere sense.

## Representation: things replaced by concepts, concepts become things

- The Cartesian divorce of spirit and matter makes things simultaneously more abstract and more purely thing-like: Duchamp's urinal, Carl André's brick pile — concrete and abstract at once, failing to interpenetrate as true art does. Compare a schizophrenic Rorschach response describing either literal blot-strokes or a vague abstraction like "motherhood" or "democracy."
- Heidegger: the modern world has become a "**world picture**" — "the fact that the world becomes picture at all is what distinguishes the essence of the modern age."
- **D. M. Levin**, *The Philosopher's Gaze: Modernity in the Shadows of the Enlightenment*: re-presentation distances us from the thing, substituting a controlled abstraction; the "ego-logical subject" makes itself "the giver of what it receives," exercising "maximum epistemic control" — the stare as its visual emblem. The final irony: the objectifying subject itself becomes an object, "reduced to the being-available of mere objects" while its inner subjectivity is denied any reality.
- Magritte's *La lunette d'approche* (1963) is cited as visually enacting this collapse of representation into "reality." Borges and Casares's story "On Exactitude in Science" (a 1:1 scale map coextensive with its territory, building on Lewis Carroll's "scale of a mile to the mile" in *Sylvie and Bruno Concluded*) captures the same idea: **the map now threatens to replace the reality** — a reversal of the left hemisphere's proper role (providing "a map of the world").
- McGilchrist's summary contention: "the modern world is the attempt by the left hemisphere to take control of everything it knows so that it is the giver to itself of what it sees" — becoming, self-referentially, Gazzaniga's interpreter interpreting only itself (a role, per William Cowper, "hitherto reserved for God"). Borges's own late essay/parable ("The other one, the one called Borges, is the one things happen to... I do not know which of us has written this page") is read as an unwitting exploration of the two-hemisphere relationship.
- Boredom and anxiety are two faces of one condition. Kafka's stated deepest feelings toward others were "indifference and fear" — per **Elias Canetti**, making him "a representative modern man." A striking number of leading modernists showed **schizoid/schizotypal traits**: Nietzsche, de Nerval, Jarry, Strindberg, De Chirico, Dali, Wittgenstein, Kafka, Bartók, Stravinsky, Webern, Stockhausen, Beckett — contrasted with earlier eras' artists, more often melancholic or bipolar.

## Rise in illnesses characterised by right-hemisphere deficits

McGilchrist's epidemiological argument: if a culture increasingly mimics right-hemisphere deficit, psychopathologies of that same character should rise with it. He surveys several:

- **Schizophrenia**: rare or absent in England before the 18th century, rising sharply with industrialisation, and increasing further and more steeply in the first half of the 20th century (similar trends in Ireland, Italy, the US). Prevalence rises with urbanisation and "developed"/Westernised status; **risk of schizophrenia is nearly double in urban versus rural settings**, and evidence favours environment-causes-psychosis over migration-of-high-risk-individuals. The "**social defeat**" concept explains elevated rates among immigrant populations (e.g., West Indian immigrants to Britain). No descriptions of schizophrenia exist from ancient Egypt, Greece or Rome, unlike melancholia/manic-depression, which are readily recognisable there.
- **Anorexia nervosa**: increased across the 20th century; not fully explained by "glamour of thinness" (a factor more in bulimia). "Holy anorexia" (e.g., St Catherine of Siena) shows a much older, rarer pattern driven by desire for purification/mortification of the flesh — a motivation still voiced by contemporary sufferers in West Africa and, in secular terms, in the West. Body image (right-parietal-dependent) is distorted to a psychotic degree. Striking case: a long-term anorexic patient achieved rapid, total recovery after a **left-hemisphere stroke** affecting the right side of her body — before, "anorexia controlled my life"; after, "I have no feelings of guilt... I can eat out in restaurants now."
- **Multiple personality disorder**: entered popular consciousness in the 1950s, added to DSM in 1980 (rare 19th-century "double personality" case reports before that); involves abdication of responsibility ("it wasn't me — it was my other half!"). Ramachandran describes a patient "halfway between anosognosia and multiple personality disorder syndrome" following two right-hemisphere lesions (right frontal, right cingulate); EEG shows right-hemisphere dysfunction with left overactivation, sharing first-rank symptoms with schizophrenia.
- **Borderline personality disorder**: first clinically described in **1938**, now "certainly one of the commonest psychiatric diagnoses" — right-hemisphere underactivity across many regions, with structural leftward asymmetry deviations in the parietal region most marked in dissociative cases.
- **Autism/Asperger's syndrome**: first described independently by Kanner (**1943**) and Asperger (**1944**), with rates climbing steadily since — a rise McGilchrist judges too large to be explained by awareness alone. Features: lack of theory of mind, poor social intelligence (tone, humour, irony), inability to detect deceit, lack of empathy and imagination, attraction to the mechanical, treating people/body-parts as objects, alienated first-person self-reference, poor eye contact, obsession with detail — "all these features will be recognisable as signs of left hemisphere predominance."
- McGilchrist is careful to note these conditions' right-hemisphere deficits are not identical or confined solely to the right hemisphere — the brain is a dynamic system — but the repeated phenomenological/neurological pattern is right-hemisphere hypofunction plus left-hemisphere over-reliance.

## The self-perpetuating nature of the left-hemisphere world

- Mass technology, urbanisation, mechanisation, alienation from nature, eroded social units and mobility have both increased mental illness and made the isolated "loner" the modern archetype; fragmented information "seepage" between contexts breeds irony, distance and cynicism over empathy; social isolation and violence/aggression reinforce each other.
- People with schizoid/schizotypal traits are disproportionately drawn to — and valued in — science, technology and administration, the very fields shaping the modern world, creating a **feedback loop**: "a culture with prominent 'schizoid' characteristics attracts to positions of influence individuals who will help it ever further down the same path," while technology and bureaucracy erode the integrative, right-hemisphere modes of attention that might otherwise resist them.

## The problem of art in the modern world

- McGilchrist reiterates his thesis that modernism is continuous with, not a rupture from, prior cultural history: late-19th-century **Aestheticism** (Nerval walking a lobster on a string, Huysmans's *À Rebours*, Lautréamont's "chance encounter of a sewing-machine and an umbrella on a dissecting-table") is a precursor, producing novelty through recombination rather than true imaginative revelation ("like those children's books with pages split into three").
- "**Art for art's sake**" both elevates art (denying ulterior purpose) and devalues it (severing its "betweenness" with life). True art requires the artist's focus to be *through* the work, not on the work as art — illustrated by Duccio (devotion) and Degas's *L'Absinthe* (human pity): had either focused on "art for art's sake," neither could have produced a great work. Tristan Tzara (Dadaism) let slip the game: art had become "a private affair — the artist produces it for himself," judgment "completely subjective."
- Modernism's inception: explicit manifestos demanding a total break with the past, belief in remaking man via theoretical ideals, glorification of science/technology and the will, contempt for the past. McGilchrist links this directly to the era's totalitarianisms:
  - Historian **Modris Eksteins**: Nazism is "the very epitome of the modern"; the ties between Marinetti's Futurism and Mussolini's Fascism are undisputed. Futurists: "**War is the world's only hygiene.**"
  - **Roger Griffin**, *Modernism and Fascism*, explores their "profound kinship."
  - Lenin: "art for me is something like an intellectual appendix. And when its use as propaganda... is over, we shall cut it out... snip, snip." **Nadezhda Mandelstam**: dissidents had their "tongues... cut out." **Martin Sixsmith**: suicide was "an astoundingly common outcome" for Soviet poets after 1917 (Mayakovsky, Esenin).
- McGilchrist distinguishes two responses to modernity's felt inauthenticity: re-engaging the right hemisphere's authenticity by patient attention (**Shklovsky's** real intent in "Art as Technique" — "habitualisation devours works, clothes, furniture, one's wife... art exists that one may recover the sensation of life... to make the stone stony" — recovering the *perceived* versus the merely *known*), versus declaring the right hemisphere's world itself inauthentic and trying to sweep it away (the path modernism largely took). **Steiner**: "originality is antithetical to novelty" — true originality requires tradition and intuitive/bodily roots, whereas willed novelty produces banality.
- Movement-by-movement readings as left-hemisphere modes of apprehension: **de Chirico**'s harsh light and irrational shadows (*Turin Spring*, 1914, Fig. 12.1); **Malevich**'s Black Square (1913), Black Circle (1915), White on White (1917) — simple geometric, black-and-white forms; **Cubism** — multiple simultaneous viewpoints (uninhabitable), destroyed depth, schematic reduction to cylinders/cubes/spheres; the **Futurist Manifesto's (1910)** false claim about retinal persistence multiplying moving objects mirrors the Zeitraffer phenomenon of right-hemisphere deficit; **Pointillism** (Gestalt reduced to discrete dots, anticipating digital reproduction); **Dadaism/Absurdism/Surrealism** (disjunction, random juxtaposition, emptied meaning); **Abstract painting**, **Collage** (whole as independent pieces), **Minimalism**, **Functionalism** (Le Corbusier's home as "*une machine à habiter*"; Mies van der Rohe's rejection of local colour), **Futurism**'s preference for future over past.
- The human body/face, right-hemisphere-dependent, is correspondingly distorted: Paul Valéry's essay on the body describes it as alien, asymmetrical, without past, his left hand "an object that is not-I" — read by McGilchrist as confirming a left-hemisphere view of embodiment. Extreme examples: Hans Bellmer's dismembered marionettes; Picasso's *Woman in a Red Armchair* (1932, Fig. 12.2).

## Modernist music

- Music, "the least explicit of all the arts," attained by contrast a new abstraction in the 20th century: Schoenberg — "how the music sounds is not the point." Melodic line was largely abandoned; harmonic structure became conceptually rather than intuitively appreciable.
- Music's physical/embodied power: melody, harmony and rhythm produce involuntary muscular tension/relaxation, affecting respiration and heart rate; music alleviates anxiety, depression and pain. Illustrative case: a Benedictine monastery in southern France curtailed Gregorian chanting during 1960s post-Vatican-II modernisation; monks who thrived on four hours' sleep became "listless and exhausted... like wet dishrags" until **Alfred Tomatis** convinced the abbot to reinstate chanting, after which the community recovered fully by November.
- **Nietzsche** (1878), quoted at length, anticipates the trend: "our ears have become increasingly intellectual... we always inquire after the reason, what 'it' means, and no longer for what 'it' is... **the symbolic replaces that which exists**" — which McGilchrist calls "surely the perfect expression of the triumph of theory and abstraction over experience... of the left hemisphere."
- Harmony and dissonance are not purely culturally constructed: Norwegians and practitioners of Ancient Indian music make the same interval-emotion associations; four-month-old infants prefer consonance and associate minor keys with sadness; harmony slows heart rate and activates pleasure-associated brain regions, dissonance activates noxious-stimulus regions. The **right hemisphere** is more sensitive to harmony/consonance; the **left hemisphere** is linked to dissonance processing. Harmony (like pictorial perspective, its analogue) is right-hemisphere-dependent, developed alongside perspective in the Renaissance, and declined alongside it in modernism (as Picasso disrupted visual perspective).
- Discords (as in Bach, or Byrd's "false relations") are valuable when *resolved* within a wider consonance — context is "absolutely critical... since music is pure context, even if the context is silence." Loss of tonal centres removes listeners' anchor; **Honegger**, after a performance of Stravinsky's *Symphony in Three Movements*: "One has no time to listen or appraise. One is too busy counting eighth notes." **Hindemith** likened serialism to a disorienting fairground "merry-go-round." **Tippett** lamented the loss of melody (Haydn: "the invention of a fine melody is a work of genius"; Mozart likened melodists to "a noble racehorse," mere contrapuntists to "a hired post-hack").
- Not all modern composers followed suit: **Britten, Pärt, Glass, Lauridsen, Tavener, MacMillan** found intuitively grounded, expressive paths continuing the Romantic tradition; **jazz**, less self-consciously novelty-driven, is called "one of the great creations of the modernist era."

## The successes of modernism

- Beauty (organic wholeness/harmony of parts) is a remarkably cross-cultural, non-socially-constructed value: Western audiences respond to Japanese, Indian, Indonesian art and vice versa; untutored populations in Papua New Guinea intuitively grasp the emotional meaning of Mozart with no prior exposure to Western music.
- **Poetry** proved resistant to modernism's anti-meaning experiments because language's conventions (unlike musical notes or colours) carry intrinsic, hard-to-abandon meaning: Kurt Schwitters's nonsense-syllable Dadaist "poems," and even Eliot's *The Waste Land* and Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*, are judged "interesting culturohistorical document[s]... rather than powerful poetry." By contrast, the modernist era from Baudelaire, Verlaine, Mallarmé and Rimbaud through Hardy, Frost, Yeats, Eliot, Auden, Stevens and Larkin produced exceptionally rich poetry — Larkin's preface to his *Oxford Book of Twentieth Century Verse* is quoted noting it represents far more distinct poets than equivalent anthologies of the 18th or 19th centuries, which McGilchrist attributes to modernism's relative freedom from convention.
- **Cinema** is named one of modernism's great achievements — Tarkovsky ("one of the few artists of whom one can genuinely use the term Shakespearean"), Polanski, Paradzhanov — because visual imagery, like language, retains intrinsic meaning that resists full abstraction.

## Post-Modernism

- With Post-Modernism, "meaning drains away" entirely: art becomes a self-referential game pointing only to its own emptiness. Sass's schizophrenic "combination of superiority and impotence" is, per McGilchrist, most fully realised here: post-modern criticism claims words have no referent and reality is pure construct (rendering the critic's own act of writing self-undermining — "why would any solipsist write?"), while simultaneously asserting a superior, decoding stance over the author, now reduced to a puppet of unconscious social forces.
- This "**coded-message**" model (an "axiom... in most versions of structuralism") is, for McGilchrist, the left hemisphere trying — and failing — to grasp right-hemisphere meaning: it senses "more going on... than meets the eye" but converts that sense into a display of its own cleverness rather than genuine understanding. **Severin Schroeder**: "literary value... cannot be reduced to the things that are described and the opinions that are conveyed; it is always a matter of how certain things are presented... And this How cannot be reduced to another What" — a "How" McGilchrist says only the right hemisphere can appreciate. He counsels critics toward the Hippocratic "above all, do no harm."
- The detached, ironic stance toward emotionally charged material is likened to a **psychopath's power game**: the audience's genuine emotional response ("naïve") or forced dissembling ("smiling timidly... at his tormentors") both confirm the artist/critic's control.
- The same "knowing superiority" appears in reductionist scientific discourse and — McGilchrist argues via a close reading of **Nick Humphrey**'s *Seeing Red* — even in confident materialist accounts of consciousness. Humphrey claims consciousness is a "self-resonance" from re-entrant brain circuits that becomes "closed off from the outside world in an internal loop," and that evolution deliberately engineered a "grandiose" illusion of consciousness's importance because believers in it "have longer and more productive lives" — a "deliberate trick" of the genetic "illusionist." McGilchrist compares this manoeuvre to **Philip Gosse**'s claim that fossils were placed by God to test faith: both are ingenious enough to be unfalsifiable. He marshals a chorus of scepticism against reductive confidence about consciousness: Stuart Sutherland ("Nothing worth reading has been written about it"), Thomas Nagel, Nakita Newton, Jerry Fodor ("Nobody has the slightest idea..."), Colin McGinn ("You might as well assert that numbers emerge from biscuits or ethics from rhubarb"), situating this scepticism in a lineage running through Montaigne, the Buddha, Socrates and St Paul.
- **Why Post-Modernism is not right-hemisphere-friendly despite superficially rejecting Enlightenment certainty**: the difference is the *level of consciousness*. The Enlightenment mistook its "clear, orderly, fixed" left-hemisphere simulacrum for reality itself — "as though the frescoes on the wall of an eighteenth-century dining room were taken for the world outside." Post-Modernism, aware of its own awareness, no longer makes that mistake — but instead of returning to a real, if never-fully-graspable, world (the right hemisphere's tentative, "caring" orientation), it denies any reality exists to interpret at all: "The contrast is not between the fixity of the artificial and the fluidity of the real, but between the fixity and the chaos of two kinds of artificiality."
- Closing formulation, via the analogy of believer versus atheist ("unknowing" that looks identical but is oppositely motivated): the right hemisphere's disposition reaches, with **"care,"** tentatively toward something beyond itself, using language and reason but going *through and beyond* them — "this is why the left hemisphere is not its enemy, but its valued emissary." Once the left hemisphere believes itself self-sufficient, it abandons care for what Robert Graves called the "**ecstasy of chaos**." McGilchrist's final line: "One says 'I do not know,' the other 'I know — that there is nothing to know.' One believes that one cannot know: the other 'knows' that one cannot believe."
