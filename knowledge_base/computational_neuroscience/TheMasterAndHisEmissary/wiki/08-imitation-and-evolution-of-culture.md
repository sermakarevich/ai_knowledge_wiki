# Chapter 7: Imitation and the Evolution of Culture

Opening Part Two, McGilchrist proposes that Western history shows a succession of shifts in the balance of power between the two hemispheres over the last 2,000 years, and that the chapters ahead will trace this pattern from Ancient Greece through the Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Romanticism, and modernism/post-modernism to the present, where the balance has swung — "perhaps irretrievably far" — towards the left hemisphere. Before making that historical case, this chapter addresses two prior questions: why should such shifts occur at all, and by what mechanism could a hemisphere's "way of being" actually spread through a culture and down the generations, given that the brain itself cannot be changing on the timescale of centuries? McGilchrist's answer is imitation: a uniquely human, empathic, right-hemisphere-grounded capacity that transmits cultural "ways of being" far faster than genetic inheritance, and which — together with epigenetic mechanisms — explains how a civilisation can be re-modelled towards one hemisphere's values within historical, rather than evolutionary, time.

## Scope and method of Part Two

McGilchrist frames the project as tracing "a succession of shifts of balance between the hemispheres over the last 2,000 years," citing Nietzsche's idea that the Apollonian and Dionysian "drives" exist "side by side, mostly in open conflict, stimulating and provoking one another to give birth to ever-new, more vigorous offspring." The territory to be covered: the rise of the written word, currency, drama, and sixth-century BC Athenian civilisation; then, with more concentrated attention, the Renaissance, the Reformation, the Enlightenment, the transition to Romanticism, and modernism/post-modernism. He explicitly flags the enterprise as "hugely selective" given how vast each topic is, but argues that refusing to look for an overall pattern out of fear of oversimplification is itself a mistake — "there is a 'necessary distance' for such pattern recognition to work," and fine-grained expert analysis, precisely because it is close-up, is poorly suited to seeing patterns at all.

He deliberately excludes non-Western culture in any detail, citing both his own limits and the book's scope, though he notes he will later discuss hemisphere balance in Far Eastern cultures, where he believes "the two hemispheres enjoy... a better symbiosis than they do in the West." He invokes Karl Jaspers' concept of the *Achsenzeit* ("axial age," roughly 800–200 BC), during which a pivotal shift in world-view occurred in Greece, China, and India alike, producing Plato, Buddha, Confucius, Heraclitus, Lao Tzu, the Upanishads, and the Hebrew prophets. Other cultures show partial parallels to Western shifts (he notes text-based, image-proscribing fundamentalism recurs in Islamic history, comparably to the Reformation), but he argues nothing elsewhere matches the West's extreme divarication — no equivalent of the Enlightenment's insistence on "just one, rectilinear, way of conceiving the world," and consequently no need for a Romanticism to redress it. He cites Max Weber's demonstration that only in the West did "unchecked, acquisitive rationalism in science, capitalism and bureaucracy" take hold, and quotes historian of science Stephen Gaukroger on why the Scientific Revolution — an anomalous, "pathological" rate of growth by which science comes to dominate all other cognitive values — happened in the West and not in China, Islam, or medieval Paris/Oxford.

## Why have the shifts of balance occurred?

McGilchrist anticipates the objection that no such pattern exists, since any historical period contains many conflicting factors and individual exceptions — but insists this granular truth doesn't refute the existence of a larger pattern, only makes it harder to see from close up. He then addresses causation directly: historians will point to social and economic factors, and chance, but he argues these exist in "an inextricably involved dynamic relationship" with changes in how we see the world — holding any one set of factors (economic, social, institutional) "steady" can make it look causally sufficient, but nothing can really be held steady, since all factors are "facets of the indivisible human condition." One factor in this dynamic, he proposes, is "the need to resolve the inherently unstable relationship between the worlds delivered by the two hemispheres."

He is careful to note he is not committed to positing literal metaphysical "drives" instantiated in the hemispheres fighting a "cosmic struggle" — the claim is metaphorical, comparable to how Freud spoke of *Triebe* (eros/thanatos), Jung of long-acting historical forces, Nietzsche of the Apollonian/Dionysian, and Scheler of *Drang* and *Geist*. He draws an analogy to Dawkins' "selfish gene": officially a neutral metaphor, but one doing real explanatory work; the same ambiguity applies to talk of the hemispheres' "wills." He states plainly: "I am not committed to the view that the brain is the driver of culture, any more than I am to the view that culture is the driver of brain development. They will inevitably mould each other." Hemisphere differences constrain the range of options available to a culture, even as cultural shifts exploit those options.

The deeper mechanism McGilchrist proposes is a recurring cycle of authenticity and its loss: there is "a continual tendency for the authenticity of right hemisphere 'presencing' to be transformed into an inauthentic 're-presenting' in the left; in essence, what was living becomes a cliché." This can resolve in two ways:

- **Homeostatic (negative feedback):** a natural swing back to right-hemisphere authenticity — which is, in turn, "doomed soon to be co-opted by the left hemisphere and become inauthentic again." A genuine "swing of the pendulum."
- **Non-homeostatic (positive feedback):** rather than a corrective swing back, the right hemisphere's world is rejected outright as *intrinsically* (not just contingently) inauthentic and invalid — producing a loss of homeostasis in which "the left hemisphere's values simply become further entrenched." This, he argues, is why the left hemisphere "necessarily gains ground over time."

He states his view that today "all the available sources of intuitive life – cultural tradition, the natural world, the body, religion and art" have been so conceptualised and "deconstructed (ironised)" by left-hemisphere words, systems, and theories that their power to point beyond that hermetic world is largely drained. He cites Nietzsche, Freud, and Heidegger as having independently noted rationality's gradual encroachment on instinct, and quotes neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp's speculation that cortical evolution may proceed via active inhibition and disconnection of instinctual/emotional processes from cognitive ones — possibly "the path of autism, in its various forms." The overall process, McGilchrist says, is "not... a smooth and even process... more like a tug of war in which the players move back and forth, but ground is continually lost by one side" — the left.

## How have the shifts of balance occurred?

McGilchrist is explicit that he is *not* proposing that recent historical shifts (the last 500 years) reflect measurable changes in brain structure — those would require far longer timescales. He allows it's conceivable that scanning an eighth-century-BC Achaean brain versus a modern one might reveal small structural or functional differences, but insists nothing at the structural level is at stake in swings like the Renaissance or Reformation. Instead: "Our experience of the world helps to mould our brains, and our brains help to mould our experience of the world. Patterns of brain function, if not changes in visible structure, are likely to be involved."

He surveys candidate mechanisms:

- **Classical natural selection** (random mutation plus selective pressure) is too slow for anything but the very longest timescales — plausibly relevant to the arrival of a genetically distinct population in Ancient Greece, or to the disappearance of "whole ways of thinking and being" following the Gothic/Hunnic/Frankish overrunning of the Roman Empire in the 4th–5th centuries AD, but not to anything from the Renaissance onward, given the short timescales and absence of population-scale migration.
- **The Baldwin effect** — assortative mating (an articulate man marries an articulate woman) and niche construction (an articulate society favours articulacy) — is dismissed as "still too slow" to explain the phenomena in question.
- **Epigenetic mechanisms** — DNA methylation, histone alteration, mitochondrial transmission, X-chromosome inactivation — modulate gene *expression* without altering the DNA sequence, analogous to how a kidney cell and a muscle cell share identical DNA but express different portions of it. McGilchrist proposes these as a plausible route by which learned behaviour becomes "cell memory" transmissible to offspring, quoting a claim that "the relationship resembles one of reciprocating interaction, in which culture is generated and shaped by biological imperatives while the course of genetic evolution shifts in response to cultural innovations." Stimulation alters synaptic number and efficiency (a process of "reduction and pruning"), and such nervous-system changes "could then be epigenetically transmitted to the next generation."
- **Memes** (Dawkins) — cultural replicators of "tunes, ideas, catch-phrases, clothes fashions, ways of making pots or of building arches" transmitted mind-to-mind. McGilchrist is critical of the concept as characteristically left-hemisphere: it construes culture "mechanistically," as "replicators" making "perfect copies," breaking culture into "atomistic fragments devoid of context." His counter: "We are imitators, not copying machines."

## Imitation, not mechanical copying

This is the chapter's central positive claim. Human imitation is not slavish reproduction but an imaginative act: "Imitation is imaginatively entering into the world of the one that is imitated." He invokes Ruskin's observation that truly seeing (and thus being able to capture) the life of a single leaf is one of the hardest human achievements — "If you can paint one leaf, you can paint the world" — and the Hegelian idea of *aufgehoben* (taken up and transformed, not just copied) to describe how imitation absorbs and transforms rather than merely duplicates.

Imitation is described as "arguably the ultimately most important human skill" in brain evolution — the route by which humans learned music and language. McGilchrist notes only humans (besides some birds) directly imitate sounds, and only humans imitate another's *means*, not just their goal — other species may adopt the same end and find their own way to it, but do not copy the method. This capacity for mimesis lets us "escape from the confines of our own experience and enter directly into the experience of another being."

Imitation is grounded in **empathy** and the **body**:
- More empathic people mimic others' facial expressions more; in one study, low-empathy subjects reported feeling the same emotions as high-empathy subjects in words, without showing it involuntarily in face or body — "the feelings their conscious left hemispheres knew that they ought to feel."
- Imitating (versus merely observing) emotional facial expressions produces significantly increased right-sided limbic activity.
- In joint-task experiments, two people with independent roles can spontaneously function "as one agent with a unified action plan."
- Children imitate humans but not mechanical devices performing identical actions; adults make spontaneous sympathetic movements watching another person's actions but not when told (falsely, of identical results) that a computer produced them.
- Imitation is intrinsically pleasurable and non-instrumental — babies as young as **forty-five minutes old** can imitate facial gestures.

McGilchrist quotes Michael Taussig (*Mimesis and Alterity*) on "sympathetic magic," and Taussig's citation of Walter Benjamin: "Nature creates similarities... The highest capacity for producing similarities... is man's... Perhaps there is none of his higher functions in which his mimetic faculty does not play a decisive role." He draws the distinction (paralleling metaphor vs. simile) that mimesis is right-hemisphere-based — intentional, aspirational, empathic — while mere "copying" (disembodied procedure/algorithm) is left-hemisphere-based. He cites Steven Mithen's contrast between Early Humans, who had "a capacity for simile... they could be 'like' an animal — but not for metaphor — they could not 'become' an animal," as an illustration of empathic identification versus mere resemblance.

Historical illustrations of this "mythical identification" mode of imitation: Thomas Mann's 1936 lecture "Freud and the Future," describing Alexander imitating Miltiades, Caesar imitating Alexander, and Napoleon declaring "I am Charlemagne" rather than merely comparing himself to Charlemagne — "This is the mythical formula." Bruno Snell on Homeric metaphor: "The warrior and the lion are activated by one and the same force... a man who walks 'like a lion' betrays an actual kinship with the beast." McGilchrist extends this to hunting cultures, where trackers learn to "get inside" the tracked animal's experience, and to Wordsworth's sense of mountains as quasi-animate "huge and mighty Forms," and to a Japanese conception in which "human beings and every natural thing are one body in total."

The neuroscientific grounding: **mirror neurones** make observed action feel as if it is being experienced directly; further, merely imagining an action (with no visual stimulus present) recruits some of the same neurones used in direct perception — imagining, watching, and doing "share important neural foundations." McGilchrist draws a normative conclusion: "we need to be careful of our imagination, since what we imagine is in a sense what we are and who we become" — noting the etymological link between *imago* and *imitari*. He credits Pascal with understanding that virtue is acquired by imitating the virtuous, "the foundation of all monastic traditions."

## The "imitation gene": a thought experiment

McGilchrist constructs a hypothetical to show how selection for a general capacity to imitate could itself be an evolutionarily favoured trait, distinct from selection for any specific skill. Using an invented example (learning to swim):

- If a skill is **wholly inimitable** (only a genetic swimmer can swim), the population converges on the "swimming gene" alone through ordinary selection.
- If a skill is **wholly imitable** (anyone who sees it can do it), the gene for it becomes irrelevant and may die out — everyone acquires the skill by imitation, "a quicker mechanism."
- If a skill is **partially imitable** (realistic case), there is selective pressure both for the skill-gene itself *and* for being "a very good imitator" — i.e., selection for a general **gene for imitation**.
- When a second, similarly advantageous partially-imitable skill appears (e.g., "flying"), those already carrying the imitation gene have a head start, and this second wave of learning spreads faster than the first because it relies on imitation rather than gene transmission — a compounding, accelerating effect as further skills arise.

He draws out the implications: the imitation-gene process only gets started when the relevant behaviour is *partially* imitable; it requires the behaviour to carry real selective/survival weight; and each successive skill is acquired faster than the last because imitation outpaces genetic transmission. Substituting "music" for swimming and "language" for flying, McGilchrist proposes that **imitation — "the meta-skill that enables all other skills"** — may explain the otherwise inexplicably rapid expansion of the hominid brain. Crucially, "what we chose to imitate would govern which epigenetic mechanisms got selected... rather than the genes that got selected dictating what we imitated" — e.g., a culture that teaches people to think of themselves in computer-like terms would select for the "geek" brain. He frames this as a paradox with a positive resolution: from the gene, "the symbol of ruthless competition... and of the relatively atomistic and oppositional values of the left hemisphere," arises a skill — imitation — that enables further evolution to proceed "through empathy and co-operation, the values of the right hemisphere." His summary formulation: "Genes could free us from genes... we can choose who we become."

He also proposes a group-selection rationale specific to language and music: since these are "reciprocal skills, of no use to individuals on their own" but valuable to groups, and since they could be "mimicked by those not genetically related" (eroding pure genetic selection), imitation itself would be selectively advantageous — good imitators bond groups together, cohesive groups out-survive others, and this — plus flexibility from expansion of the (especially right) frontal lobes, "the seat of social intelligence" — was what was truly selected for, not narrow "language genes" or "music genes."

He notes William James saw the human species, contrary to popular assumption, as having "a larger array of apparently instinctual behaviours than any other," a claim consistent with this account of imitation-driven epigenetic inheritance.

## Values, attractors, and the two hemispheres

Pivoting from mechanism to teleology, McGilchrist distinguishes mechanical causation — where "causes antedate their effects... push from behind," leading to closure and determinism — from a phenomenology of felt freedom in which we are "pulled, drawn, attracted forward" by values or "ideals" that function like Jungian archetypes, standing "outside time" unlike efficient causes. He quotes Nietzsche: "'Action at a distance' cannot be eliminated: something draws something else closer, something feels drawn... the mechanistic notion of pressing and pushing is merely a hypothesis based on sight and touch."

He clarifies that by "values" he does not mean principles for resolving moral dilemmas (consequentialist calculus vs. Kantian deontology) but the values themselves being weighed — e.g., whether courage or self-sacrifice has worth *in itself*. He invokes Max Scheler's hierarchical ranking of value-realms, from sensory/utility at the bottom to the holy at the top, and states the chapter's key diagnostic claim: **"the left hemisphere recognises only this lowest rank of value"** (utility), while higher values — bravery, beauty, intelligence, holiness — require the right hemisphere's mode of apprehension, one not reducible to "sequential analytic logic." The left hemisphere's characteristic move is to reduce all higher values back to utility (beauty as mate-selection, bravery as territorial defence, holiness as group cohesion) — arguments that can be constructed but that ultimately "fail to convince," since "rationality is, naturally, reluctant to accept the very possibility of a thing lying beyond rationalistic argument."

He closes this section by describing each hemisphere's characteristic mode of lapsing into inauthenticity, and how each drives the pendulum: the right hemisphere, embedded in "the natural viewpoint," risks lapsing into Heidegger's *Verfallen* through sheer everyday familiarity with the lived world; the left hemisphere, by contrast, enables a wilful, "unnatural" disengagement that lets Dasein "ascend from the gravity of the earth" — but risks its own inauthenticity through the familiarity of cliché and disengaged re-presentation. "Each hemisphere risks inauthenticity from a different source, which is why each is vital to the other." Crucially, the right hemisphere's route back to authenticity must run through "engagement with an attractive power beyond itself" — it cannot self-correct in isolation the way the left can (temporarily) achieve clarity by disengaging.

## Synthesis: from selfish gene to co-operative culture

McGilchrist closes by asking whether the mimesis-from-genetics story itself enacts a hemisphere shift — from left-hemisphere atomistic competition to right-hemisphere contextual co-operation. In the left hemisphere's construal, the individual is a discrete competitor and the group a mere "amalgam of wary aliens" tolerated only for personal benefit ("survival of the fittest," "eat or be eaten"). In the right hemisphere's construal, individuality is only intelligible within the context of the group — what looks to the left hemisphere like loss of identity in the group is really *aufgehoben*, being taken up within a whole to which one belongs. "Out of wary opposition arises empathy: out of the world of 'eat or be eaten' comes a shared meal round the fire." He invokes the Prisoner's Dilemma (we all do better through collaboration) and Heraclitus ("war is the king and father of all things, peace is the queen and mother") to frame this as a move from linear, oppositional striving to "a reverberative process of collaboration."

Even acknowledging that gene survival, anthropomorphised by Dawkins as "selfish," pushes us "from behind" with "no say over where we go," McGilchrist argues that an Aufhebung emerges from this: imitation-driven skill acquisition, in which "our eyes are opened," collaboration plays a part, and — crucially — "there is a degree of freedom, in that we can choose what we imitate." He reiterates, closing the chapter, that the cultural shifts in hemisphere balance he will now trace through history are not to be understood as structural changes in the brain — certainly not at the macro level — but as shifts in patterns of function, transmitted through imitation and epigenetic inheritance, operating on the timescale of cultural history rather than evolutionary time.
