# Chapter 9: The Renaissance and the Reformation

Opening Part Two's historical survey where Chapter 8 left off, McGilchrist first argues the "Dark Ages" (the roughly 700 years between Rome's fifth-century fall and the twelfth-century early Renaissance) were less bleak than their name suggests, then traces the Renaissance as a second great "standing back" of the frontal lobes — this time more self-conscious than sixth-century-BC Athens — which produces an initial, sweeping expansion of the right hemisphere's way of being in the world (perspective, individuality, lived time, embodiment, melancholy, ambiguity, the artist as semi-divine intuitive) before gradually tipping, across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, toward the left hemisphere's abstraction and control. The Reformation is presented as the pivotal hinge: a movement that began, in Luther, as an authentically right-hemisphere plea for inner/outer unity and against empty ritual, but that "slipped" — not through reversal but through a change of meaning — into iconoclasm, literalism, bureaucratization, and the triumph of the word over the image, setting up the narrower, more rigid seventeenth-century turn (Bacon curdling into Descartes) that would become the Enlightenment.

## The Dark Ages reconsidered

- The period from Rome's fall (5th century) to the early Renaissance (12th century) — roughly **seven hundred years** — used to be called the Dark Ages, a term McGilchrist says has fallen into disuse partly because of the quality of surviving craftsmanship, partly because modern historiography has illuminated it, and partly because of its pejorative flavour.
- Even so, he grants it would be "a brave person" who denied the Renaissance was a genuinely unparalleled step forward, comparable in significance to sixth-century-BC Athens.
- He flags a recurring problem of vocabulary: contested terms like "the Renaissance," "the Enlightenment," and "Romanticism" look to the left hemisphere like categories that should be crisply definable, but to the right hemisphere they are "loose constellations of phenomena" with a family resemblance rather than fixed boundaries.

## The Renaissance as a second "standing back"

- The Renaissance looks like a second great insurgence of the right hemisphere — arguably more pronounced than in the Ancient World — but McGilchrist calls this an over-simplification: this "standing back" is more self-conscious than Athens's, evident in the Renaissance's own retrospective gaze toward antiquity (a "second level of self-consciousness").
- **Petrarch**, one of the first great Renaissance writers, is said to be the first person recorded as climbing a hill purely for the view — and tellingly reports not its utility but its *beauty*. This illustrates how these historical turning points begin as symmetrical: the standing-back function itself, mediated by the bilateral frontal lobes, is "hemisphere-neutral," but it forces a sharpened division of labour that both drives left-hemisphere abstraction/generalisation and deepens the right hemisphere's relationship with the world through what McGilchrist calls **"necessary distance."**
- Petrarch's "view" exemplifies a Renaissance characteristic: a sudden noticing of what had always been there but unaccountably neglected — in science, looking at things "as they are" rather than as known to be; in painting, painting what is seen rather than what is known.

## Perspective and individuality

- The rediscovery of **perspective** is central. It was not, contrary to old belief, a Renaissance invention — it was understood by late Greek painters and Roman wall painters — but the faculty had been lost for **over 1,000 years** until **Giotto** (late 13th–early 14th century), often called the first Renaissance painter to use it.
- It was advanced by **Masaccio**, after **Brunelleschi** demonstrated practical perspective in the piazza of Florence's Duomo in **1415**; **Alberti**'s *De Pictura* (**1435**) gave the first systematic treatise on its geometrical basis.
- Depth relies principally on the right hemisphere, but each hemisphere contributes differently to perspective: it can either draw the viewer in through "the pull of the imagination," enhancing the individual's felt standing within the world, or turn the individual into a detached geometer's "observing eye" onto the world.
- Perspectival space is bound up with individuality — a view from one particular place and time rather than a God's-eye "view from nowhere" — and this duality mirrors the rise of the individual, which permits both empathy (understanding others as feeling as one does) and, at its worst, a detachment tending toward autism.
- Example: **Ghirlandaio**'s Adoration scene shows perspectival depth in both space and time simultaneously — the infant Christ lies beside a crib that is a Roman sarcophagus, while the Magi are dressed as contemporary Florentines, spanning a 1,500-year perspective in one image.

## Lived time, memory and poetry

- A right-hemisphere-derived sense of **"lived time"** is distinguished from the medieval *ubi sunt* motif ("Where now is Alexander the Great...?"), which taught contempt for earthly things via an abstract, impersonal awareness of universal mutability. Lived time instead mourns the *irreparable loss of particular individuals* — valuing the transitory rather than dismissing it.
- **François Villon**'s *Ballade des dames du temps jadis* begins in the conventional *ubi sunt* form but its refrain — *mais où sont les neiges d'antan?* ("but where are the snows of yesteryear?") — carries an intimate, melancholic note beyond moralising. Johan Huizinga (*The Waning of the Middle Ages*) is quoted on a related poem by Martial d'Auvergne: "it is still the briefness of joy that is deplored, and with the grave tone of the memento mori is mixed the regret for lost beauty."
- Villon (who died, it is thought, in his early thirties) writes his own epitaph in *Ballade des pendus*: *"Frères humains qui après nous vivez..."* ("Brothers, fellow-men, you who live after we are dead, do not harden your hearts against us...").
- McGilchrist identifies **three kinds of remembering** entrained in Villon's poetry: the long historical past peopled by real suffering humans; a forward projection to being remembered after death; and remembrance of his own personal losses — anticipating Ronsard's *Quand tu seras bien vieille* ("Ronsard sang my praise in the days when I was beautiful").
- Villon is one of the first writers to appear to the reader as an individual — Wordsworth's "a man speaking to men" — as later true of Chaucer; imperfection becomes part of what individuates and binds people, not a lapse from an ideal.
- **Memento mori** culture: Erasmus and Thomas More each kept a skull on their desks; Holbein's *The Ambassadors* hides a distorted skull, visible only from one particular staircase angle, behind two confident young men surrounded by symbols of worldly knowledge.
- **Sir Thomas Wyatt** (early-16th-century) is cited for vivid personally remembered emotional scenes — his poem on losing Anne Boleyn's love ("They fle from me that sometime did me seke...") — and for a lesser-known poem imagining pain from his beloved's point of view, which McGilchrist calls "an achievement of the right frontal lobe if ever there was one."
- On the **"betweenness" of emotional memory**: quoting Scheler's point that "our thoughts are ours" no more than our feelings are, McGilchrist argues feeling arises out of "what I feel for what you feel for what I feel about your feelings about me," binding people together even as it depends paradoxically on their being separate, distinct individuals.

## Drama, Shakespeare and the whole greater than its parts

- Drama flourishes at exactly the moments of "necessary distance" — detached enough to observe one another, not so detached as to become objectified or alienated.
- Shakespeare's plays exemplify the right hemisphere's rise: characters subvert their own stereotyped literary/historical sources through sheer individuality — Richard II a self-absorbed poet ill-suited to kingship, Macbeth wracked by scruple, Antony undone by love, and **Barnardine** (*Measure for Measure*), a condemned prisoner who simply refuses to "arise and be hanged" on cue, forcing the plot to find another head.
- Shakespeare "confounded genres" (comic scenes in tragedies, Jacques in a comedy) and resists standing in judgement of characters, even Shylock, inherited as a stock villain — "the web of our life is of a mingled yarn, good and ill together."
- Citing **Maurice Morgann**'s insight, McGilchrist singles out **Falstaff** as a figure incomprehensible when analytically decomposed (a coward, braggart, buffoon on paper) yet forming a coherent, Gestalt-like living whole whose bravery and generosity transform rather than merely offset his flaws.
- **Caricature** is offered as a related mystery of whole-vs-parts: gross distortion of every individual feature remains compatible with instant recognition of the whole. Ancient caricature (Egypt, Greece) exaggerated *types*; **Annibale Carracci** (1560–1609), who coined "caricatura," was the first to caricature individuals: "A good caricature... like every work of art, is more true to life than reality itself." **Gombrich and Kris** are cited for the insight that caricature reveals "similarity is not essential to likeness," calling the caricaturist's art "still somewhat akin to black magic."

## Portraiture, light and lateral symbolism

- Newfound expressiveness runs through all Renaissance arts, from troubadour songs (Adam de la Halle) and Christine de Pisan's love poetry to painters like **Masaccio** and **Gozzoli**, who gave new weight to expressive human faces even in grouped scenes.
- Research by **Hufschmidt, Grüsser, Latto** and others found a peak in **left-facing (right-hemisphere-favouring) profiles** in Renaissance portraiture, alongside a trend, beginning in the 14th century and increasing through the Renaissance, for painted light sources to fall in the **left visual field** — a tendency that declined from the 18th century and vanished in the 20th century alongside the disappearance of illusionary depth/perspective in modern art. (Left-dominant light direction is also noted in the wall paintings of Pompeii and Herculaneum and the Byzantine mosaics of Ravenna.)
- **James Hall** is cited on a parallel shift in symbolism: the traditionally "sinister" left side of the body came to be seen, at the Renaissance, as more beautiful, gentle, truthful, and feeling — "the superior beauty of the left hand was an important component of the courtly love tradition." McGilchrist reads this as too early to be influenced by actual knowledge of hemisphere lateralisation — "another possible instance of the brain intuitively cognising itself."

## Melancholy, wit and mixed emotion

- Renaissance music brought an "astonishing efflorescence of polyphony," expressive melody and, for the first time, complex harmony (false relations, suspensions); its greatest works are melancholic (Requiems, Passiontide devotional music, lute songs and madrigals of unrequited love), though wit and self-mocking humour also run throughout.
- Melancholy was linked to wit, intelligence and wisdom, culminating in **Burton**. **Jennifer Radden** notes Renaissance writers took up Aristotle's view that groundless melancholy runs deep in thoughtful temperaments, emphasising its **"uncausedness"** — a theme she says declined in the 18th century but returned in the 19th. Shakespeare's Antonio opens *The Merchant of Venice*: *"In sooth I know not why I am so sad... What stuff 'tis made of, whereof it is born, / I am to learn."*
- McGilchrist reads this uncaused melancholy as intrinsic to a right-hemisphere-dominant way of being, not a reaction to any explicable event — consistent with the pattern he argues recurs across later chapters (receding in the 18th century, resurging in the 19th).
- **William James** is quoted: "melancholy... constitutes an essential moment in every complete religious evolution," with the "completest religions" being those where pessimism is most developed — evidence, McGilchrist argues, of a shared right-hemisphere root linking religion, music and melancholy in this period. He flags tears as a related motif worth study — in Shakespeare, Dowland's songs, and, more detached, in Donne, Marvell ("the Tears do come / Sad, slowly dropping like a Gumme"), and Crashaw ("watery diamonds," "portable and compendious oceans").
- The Renaissance is also the period when **mixed and ambiguous emotion** first become fully embraced: while Metrodorus anciently noted pleasure mixed in sadness, Seneca thought the idea "quite immoral," and (per Snell) even Homer lacked the language for "half-willing, half-unwilling" — only with Sappho does "bitter-sweet Eros" appear. Renaissance poetry (Michelangelo's *"la mia allegrezz'è la malinconia"*) reiterates the union of pleasure and pain throughout. McGilchrist frames this as consistent with the right hemisphere's tolerance for ambiguity and unresolved contradiction, as against the left hemisphere's need for explicit resolution.

## Longing versus desire

- McGilchrist distinguishes true **longing** from mere wanting, and objects to the modern habit of dismissing such longing as merely "Romantic" (implying a short-lived, sentimental, roughly 50-year cultural fad) — arguing longing is in fact ancient: present in **Odysseus**'s *nostos* (return) + *algos* (pain) = **nostalgia**, in the Hebrew psalms ("like as the hart desireth the waterbrooks..."), and in the Anglo-Saxon *The Wanderer* and *The Seafarer*.
- Etymology: in Anglo-Saxon, Old Saxon, Old High German and Old Norse, "to long" relates to "to seem/be/grow long," hence "to reach out" — grammatically *impersonal* ("it longs me [of]," not "I long for"), unlike the clear, willed, goal-directed structure of wanting. Longing instead is something that "happens" between the self and an Other, a desire for (re-)union rather than acquisition, "an elastic tension... as in a bow string (*die Bogensehne*)" — punning on *die Sehne* (sinew/tendon/bowstring) and *die Sehnsucht* (longing).
- The Renaissance, in this frame, "starts with the deepest of longing" — courtly love's worship of an unattainable ideal, Arcadian/pastoral imagery, the search for a lost Golden Age, and (in England) elegiac mourning for the old Catholic order in the Tudor settings of the Lamentations.
- Quoting **A. N. Whitehead**: "Great art is the arrangement of the environment so as to provide for the soul vivid, but transient values," such that "something new must be discovered... the permanent realization of values extending beyond its former self" — art's effect reaches beyond the transient work itself, which McGilchrist calls "the mode of the right hemisphere."

## The artist as intuitive, semi-divine maker

- **Castiglione**'s *Book of the Courtier* advises *ars est celare artem* ("skill lies in hiding one's skill") — not, McGilchrist argues, cultivated deceit, but respect for the fact that mastery must become intuitive and implicit (right-hemisphere) to avoid failing in performance, "as Hazlitt said of the Indian jugglers."
- The Renaissance is when the artist first becomes a kind of **hero**: anecdotes have the Emperor Maximilian order a nobleman to hold Dürer's ladder, and Charles V reputedly stoop to retrieve Titian's paintbrush — deference, McGilchrist argues, to the "divine" inspiration believed to work through the artist via the right hemisphere's implicit, unwillable skills.
- Drawing on **Kris and Kurtz**'s *Legend, Myth, and Magic in the Image of the Artist* (1934), he surveys recurring Renaissance (and cross-cultural) tropes: *poeta nascitur, non fit* ("a poet is born, not made"), illustrated by the legend of **Giotto**, a shepherd boy discovered by Cimabue after drawing lifelike pictures on a rock (paralleled by the Japanese painter **Maruyama Okyo**, discovered painting a pine tree on a sack); the use of chance to bypass conscious intention — **Leonardo** advising painters to take inspiration from damp stains on a wall, echoed by the 11th-century Chinese painter **Sung-Ti**'s advice to **Ch'ên Yung-chih** about a tumbledown wall ("the result will be heavenly and not human"); the belief that first ideas are best — **Ben Jonson** reporting that Shakespeare "never blotted out a line" (Jonson's retort: "would he had blotted a thousand"), and **Vasari**'s claim that **Fra Angelico** never reworked a painting; and *naturam imitandam esse* — imitate Nature, not other artists, paralleled in **Han Kan**'s reputed claim that imperial horses, not painters, were his teachers ("when Han Kan painted horses, he himself became a horse").
- This gives rise to myths of the artist's **magic power** — a divine conduit (*divino artista*) able to bring stone to life, or, conversely, a trickster/deceiver (rumours that **Michelangelo** tortured a man to death to sculpt his likeness). McGilchrist reads both myths as the left hemisphere's characteristic response — paranoid, magical-thinking attribution to "alien forces" — to intuitive right-hemisphere processes it cannot itself control, comparing it to schizophrenic paranoia.

## The rehabilitation of the body and semi-transparency

- The Renaissance rehabilitates embodied, sense-mediated existence against medieval derogation of the flesh. **Montaigne** (echoing Erasmus) insists soul and body must not be sequestered but "coupled and joined... closely together," the soul commanded to "rally to [the body] and cherish it."
- **Fulke Greville** ("She speaketh in our flesh...") and **Marvell**'s *A Dialogue between the Soul and Body* (where the last word goes to the Body) exemplify an inversion of the assumption that soul is wiser than body.
- McGilchrist introduces **"semi-transparency"**: the world is seen not as a brute fact nor a snare to be resisted, but as something seen *through* to a meaning beyond, "like a myth or metaphor." Metaphysical poets — **Donne**, **Herbert** (*The Elixir*), **Traherne** (*Shadows in the Water*) — use images of window-glass, mirrors, and pools of water for this "seeing, but seeing through."
- This respect for context and multi-level meaning underlies the Renaissance ideal of interconnected knowledge — **"Renaissance man,"** echoing Heraclitus's "enquirers into many things indeed" — and explains why the revival of antiquity was prized not for fact-finding but for exemplars of wisdom, virtue and statecraft, spurring true biography and autobiography.
- **Pope Pius II** (Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini)'s self-portrait is cited as a landmark of self-exploration and love of nature for its own sake (against Jacob Burckhardt's claim that Italians were uniquely first to see nature as beautiful) — vivid passages describing Siena's hills and Viterbo's flowering broom lead to his verdict, dated **May 1463**: "Nature is superior to any art."

## The Reformation

- McGilchrist frames the Reformation as illustrating the second of two possible responses to the "inauthenticity of re-presentation": rather than redressing the loss (the Renaissance's response), it *rejects* the right hemisphere's world outright as itself inauthentic — producing not a corrective swing of the pendulum but a **loss of homeostasis** and positive feedback that entrenches left-hemisphere values further.
- The Reformation is conventionally dated to **Luther's Ninety-Five Theses**, nailed to the door of the Schloßkirche in Wittenberg in **1517**.
- Luther himself is presented as a **tragic figure**: tolerant, conservative, concerned with authenticity and experience over authority, and not opposed to images per se (only their "functionalist abuse") — yet he unleashed destructive forces beyond his control. Erasmus is quoted on the fanaticism this provoked: "I have seen them return from hearing the sermon, as if inspired by an evil spirit, the faces of all showing a curious wrath and ferocity." McGilchrist draws an explicit parallel to **Heidegger**, whose subtle "problematising" of objective truth was likewise hijacked into anarchic relativism by followers — in both cases an authentic right-hemisphere impulse annexed by the left hemisphere's agenda "not by revolutionary inversion, but by a slippage of meaning."
- Luther's own insight — that inner and outer, mind/soul and body, must be unified, or the outward show says nothing of the inward — was itself continuous with the Renaissance's "seamlessness of the incarnate world." But followers took this to mean the outer world was *in itself* empty, collapsing the fused inner/outer into a strict either/or and reducing images to mere signifiers (a left-hemisphere "re-presentation") rather than living fusions of inner and outer.
- The **Reformation is called the first great expression of the search for certainty in modern times**. Quoting **Schleiermacher**: the Reformation and the Enlightenment share the trait that "everything mysterious and marvellous is proscribed" — both attack the visual image (vehicle of the right hemisphere) in favour of the word (stronghold of the left) in pursuit of unambiguous certainty.
- This was not the first iconoclasm: the **Byzantine Iconoclasm** (730–843, over 100 years, with one brief hiatus) banned religious images, likely in response to Arab military pressure (three sieges of Constantinople) from a religion that itself proscribed images. But Reformation iconoclasm, coming right after the greatest outpouring of religious art in history, was "something quite extraordinary."
- **Joseph Koerner**'s work on Reformation image-theology, politics and philosophy is named as McGilchrist's major source. Koerner frames the Reformation's core problem as one of **"either/or"** — "a hatred based on the absolute distinction between truth and falsehood." Statues had to be reduced to "mere wood," even though — per Koerner — the supposed "idolaters" never actually believed they worshipped statues; that accusation was the iconoclasts' own "self-serving fiction," since they could not grasp that divinity resides *between* the object and the beholder rather than fixed in the object. Luther himself: "I believe that there is no person... who does not understand that the crucifix that stands over there is not my God... but rather only a sign."
- McGilchrist connects this to the medieval scholastic doctrine of **transubstantiation**: the same left-hemisphere either/or (bread/wine is either literally body/blood or "mere" representation) that produces iconoclasm at the Reformation had already forced the metaphorical Eucharistic understanding into a literalist strait-jacket in Catholic theology — the alternative being to see "this is my body" as an incarnate metaphor sustained by the whole context of the Mass, not a punctual verbal transformation.
- Koerner's analysis of Reformation-approved imagery finds a striking **anticipation of postmodern self-reflexivity**: pictures depicting the setting they hang in, containing further depictions of themselves in infinite regress. **Cranach**'s work and **Göding**'s *Mühlberg Altarpiece* (**1568**) — contrasted with the **van Eyck brothers**' *Ghent Altarpiece* / *The Adoration of the Lamb* (**1432**) — exemplify images that, per Koerner, yearn "not toward a real presence... but toward an infinity endlessly repeated and deferred," "keep[ing] us shuttling between signifiers" rather than transporting us from signifier to signified.
- The phrase *verbum Domini manet in aeternum* ("the word of the Lord shall endure for ever") was abbreviated to the acronym **VDMIE**, reproduced endlessly until — despite Reformist intent — it became a "totemic, apotropaic" talisman itself, "the word freeze[zing] into an idol" (Kriss-Rettenbeck). Koerner: "sacrament becomes information-transfer... immutably conveyed regardless of the form they take."
- McGilchrist quotes **Samuel Johnson**'s "words are the daughters of earth" (whereas "things are the sons of heaven") to frame how words acquired thing-like status in Protestant culture through "aggressive material inscription."
- **Church architecture and social order**: Koerner notes "sacred centres... gave way to centres of attention" — the pulpit displacing the altar, positioned high above a congregation seated in geometric ranks (Fig. 9.3, the Reformed community of Stein near Nuremberg, c. 1620) — which McGilchrist reads as strongly left-hemisphere in its abstract, measured, symmetrical use of space, versus the lived, bodily experienced space of a standing pre-Reformation crowd. **Nietzsche** is quoted describing the Reformed church as "one speaking mouth [the preacher] and very many ears [the congregation]," with the state standing behind "in order... to recollect that it is the purpose, goal, and model" of the whole procedure. **Ernst Troeltsch** is cited on how Lutheranism transferred the church's authority to the civil administration, which thereby "accrued a semi-divinity."
- The Lutheran church's fixed seating (replacing the mobility of Catholic ritual) became, per Koerner, "a new, lucrative and continuous resource," funded by parishioners' desire to distinguish themselves by seating position even as the doctrine preached such distinctions were meaningless.
- Rejection of the body (Christ's suffering flesh judged unimportant, "a Manichaeism... which rejects the body") pairs, McGilchrist argues, with the general left-hemisphere tendency to abstraction and rule-generation: making the invisible Church coextensive with everywhere paradoxically renders holiness meaningless everywhere ("if everything and everywhere is holy, then nothing and nowhere is holy") — a dynamic he explicitly likens to the 20th-century art-world's "everything is art."
- He restates his Part One distinction between the left hemisphere's **parts→generalised abstraction** and the right hemisphere's **individual entity→whole-it-belongs-to**: generalities are built from fragments and can never recover the *haeccitas* (thisness) of what they're generalised from — echoing Genesis's God who creates by *dividing* (earth/heavens, sea/land, night/day).
- Citing **Max Weber**, McGilchrist links Lutheran bureaucratic categorisation to the wider connection between **Protestantism, capitalism, and bureaucratisation** — all instruments of the left hemisphere's will to power, sharing an "exaggerated emphasis on individual agency" over communion. **David Bakan** is quoted: "Agency manifests itself in isolation, alienation, and aloneness: communion in contact, openness and union. Agency manifests itself in the urge to master: communion in non-contractual co-operation." Material success became read as a sign of spiritual election.
- McGilchrist lists common threads across the otherwise diverse strands of the Reformation (Elizabethan settlement, Calvin's Geneva, the Puritans): preference for the clear/certain over the ambiguous; for the fixed/systematised over the fluid/contingent; word over image, literal over metaphorical meaning; hostility to tradition's implicit wisdom in favour of explicit, secular rational order; in its Puritan strains, an attack on music; and violent iconoclastic desecration.
- The chapter's central formula for the Reformation: the cardinal Christian tenet — **"the Word is made Flesh"** — becomes reversed, so that **"the Flesh is made Word."**

## The beginnings of Enlightenment

- **Montaigne** resists splitting the "divine" from the "earthy": "A fine thing to get up on stilts, for even on stilts we must ever walk with our legs! And upon the highest throne in the world, we are seated, still, upon our arses."
- **Stephen Toulmin**'s *Cosmopolis* identifies two distinct phases of modernity's origin: an early tolerant, literary, humanistic phase (Erasmus, Rabelais, Shakespeare, Montaigne — the age of the explorer, "a revelling in difference"), followed by a rigid, dogmatic scientific/philosophical phase that Toulmin says amounted to "a reversal of Renaissance values" in the 17th century.
- McGilchrist uses this to reframe the **Galileo affair**: contrary to received "hagiography," Galileo's ideas were not dismissed by the Pope or cardinals (who admired his work); his house arrest owed more to his personality, and the Church's greater intolerance actually came later, during the **Counter-Reformation** — itself a reaction against Reformation excesses, at a time when philosophy and science were also becoming more rigid.
- Toulmin traces a **narrowing** from 16th to 17th century — from Pantagruel to *Pilgrim's Progress*, Shakespeare to Racine, Montaigne to Descartes — a shift from oral/reciprocal to fixed/written modes, local/particular to general, concrete to abstract, time-bound to timeless, where formerly both poles (right and left hemisphere) had been held in equilibrium and now only the second became acceptable. Aristotle is quoted against this: "that which lasts long is no whiter than that which perishes in a day."
- The 16th century is framed as the age of autobiography and self-portraiture (Montaigne, Dürer) and the rise of household mirrors — an increase in self-awareness that, McGilchrist stresses, is not yet objectification of the self but the achievement of **"necessary distance,"** enabling empathic understanding of others as fellow beings. Montaigne's account of catching others' coughs and diseases through sheer imaginative empathy is cited as anticipating modern mimesis/empathy research "more than 400 years before the experiments were done."
- **Donne** is presented as the writer in whom this equilibrium visibly strains: his *Meditations* describe reading his own state of mind through his physician's face during illness ("I observe the physician with the same diligence as he the disease..."); his poems repeatedly use the conceit of the eye and the etymology of **"pupil"** (from Latin *pupilla*, "doll," the tiny inverted self-image reflected in another's eye) — as in *Witchcraft by a Picture* ("My picture drown'd in a transparent teare...").
- McGilchrist revisits **T. S. Eliot**'s concept of the "unified sensibility" of the Metaphysical poets and its subsequent "dissociation," arguing Eliot was right that something crucial was lost later in the 17th century but wrong to treat the Metaphysicals as simple unification — Donne's very self-consciousness about needing to hold two modes of attention together (as in *The Relique*, which breaks off rather than fully articulate its own miracle) is itself evidence the dissociation was already underway, though — like Bach's music — his poems could still "lightly unpick parts of the whole without losing the Gestalt."
- The sense of a world coming apart is explicit in Shakespeare (Hamlet's "the times are out of joint"; Ulysses in *Troilus and Cressida*: "Take but degree away, untune that string, / And hark what discord follows...", ending "an universal wolf... [must] last eat up himself") and in Donne's own poetry ("'Tis all in pieces, all coherence gone... this / Is crumbled out againe to his Atomis").
- Quoting **Peter Hacker**, McGilchrist notes the scientific revolution properly gathered pace only in the early 17th century, "after the flowering of the Renaissance was over" (fitting Galileo's *Dialogue*, 1632) — but its spirit still grew out of Renaissance respect for the natural world, with science not yet distinct from art.
- **Francis Bacon** is defended against a common misreading: though he coined "knowledge is power," this referred (per McGilchrist) only to human dominion over our own creations (machines), never over Nature herself; the popular image of Bacon advocating "putting Nature on the rack" is traced to a casual remark by **Leibniz**, perpetuated by **Ernst Cassirer** — not to Bacon, who instead wrote "Nature to be commanded must be obeyed... The subtlety of Nature is greater many times over than the subtlety of the senses and understanding." (Aubrey's detail: Bacon died pursuing an early experiment in refrigeration — trying to freeze a chicken.)
- **Descartes**, by contrast, soon claimed science would make us "the lords and masters of nature," discarding Bacon's caveat about Nature's subtlety — leading to what McGilchrist calls "the fallacy that was to derail the next three centuries of Western thought": that "the things we conceive very clearly and very distinctly are all true."

## Conclusion

- McGilchrist again finds a non-random pattern of hemispheric correlation: an early Renaissance phase of fruitful hemispheric balance (perspective, individuality) gives way, "for the most part," to the salience of the right hemisphere's world — an opening of the eyes to personal experience over received theory, respect for the *quiddity* of individual things and people over categories, faithful attention to nature and to other times, and an insistence on the fullest possible context (body and soul together, spatial and temporal depth, empathy, expressive arts, polyphony, wit-and-melancholy, tolerance of mixed emotion and *coniunctio oppositorum*, and the "semi-transparent" world pointing beyond itself) — all read as evidence of an initial, fertile right-hemisphere expansion that accounts for the era's astonishing richness and breadth ("Renaissance man").
- But as the Renaissance progresses, emphasis gradually shifts toward the left hemisphere: individuality curdles into atomistic ambition and competition, and "originality" comes to mean not creative possibility but the right to "free thinking" — throwing off tradition as tyrannical and irrational rather than treasuring it as inexhaustible wisdom. This shift, McGilchrist concludes, becomes the basis of "the hubristic movement which came to be known as the Enlightenment."
