# Introduction: The Master and His Emissary

This introductory chapter sets out the book's central question — why is the brain divided into two asymmetrical hemispheres, and does this asymmetry mean anything? — and states McGilchrist's thesis that the hemispheres deliver two fundamentally different, opposed versions of reality rather than dividing labor by subject matter. It surveys the troubled history of "hemisphere difference" research and its debasement into pop-psychology cliché, explains why the author nonetheless believes the asymmetry is meaningful, previews the two-part structure of the book (Part I on the brain, Part II on Western cultural history), enters methodological caveats about generalization and individual variation, and closes with the Nietzsche-derived parable that gives the book its title and organizing metaphor: a wise Master usurped by his own clever emissary.

## Opening question: why is the brain so divided?

McGilchrist opens by framing the book as a story "about ourselves and the world, and about how we got to be where we are now" — nominally about brain structure, but ultimately an attempt to understand the structure of the world the brain has helped create. He asks bluntly: why is the brain so clearly and profoundly divided, and why are the two cerebral hemispheres asymmetrical?

He traces the history of hemisphere-difference research since it was first noticed in the **mid-nineteenth century** that the hemispheres are not identical:
- First it was believed language was the defining specialization of the **left hemisphere (LH)**, with the **right hemisphere (RH)** considered essentially "silent."
- Then it was found the RH was better equipped for visual imagery — the "words in the left, pictures in the right" formula.
- That too proved unsatisfactory: both hemispheres handle both words and images, "in different ways," and evidence accumulated that virtually every human activity is served at some level by both hemispheres — "vast redundancy." Enthusiasm for finding the key to hemisphere differences waned, to the point that it became professionally disreputable for a neuroscientist to theorize on the subject.

He attributes this partly to the debasement of the idea in popular culture: the cliché that the LH is "gritty, rational, realistic but dull" and the RH "airy-fairy and impressionistic, but creative and exciting" — which he compares to Sellar and Yeatman's parody (in *1066 and All That*) of the Roundheads as "Right and Repulsive" and the Cavaliers as "Wrong but Wromantic." He calls the popular idea that the LH is somehow male and the RH somehow female "perhaps the most absurd" of these misconceptions, noting that what evidence exists tends to point the other way, if anywhere — though he declines to pursue that question in the book.

Despite this discouraging climate, McGilchrist argues the differences are not simply random or due to contingent factors like the need for space — a claim he supports by citing three authorities:
- **Joseph Hellige**, whom he calls "arguably the world's best-informed authority" on the subject, describes "very striking" differences in the information-processing abilities and propensities of the two hemispheres.
- **V. S. Ramachandran** concludes that despite the debased pop-culture version of the idea, "the notion that the two hemispheres may indeed be specialised for different functions" should not be dismissed.
- **Tim Crow**, described as "one of the subtlest and most sceptical of neuroscientists researching into mind and brain," has written that "except in the light of lateralisation nothing in human psychology/psychiatry makes any sense."

McGilchrist states his own position: "I believe there is, literally, a world of difference between the hemispheres." Understanding this has required a journey through neurology, psychology, philosophy, literature, the arts, archaeology, and anthropology — crossing disciplinary boundaries even at the risk of being, at best, "an interested outsider," at worst "an interloper condemned to make mistakes."

**The thesis**, stated directly: "there are two fundamentally opposed realities, two different modes of experience; that each is of ultimate importance in bringing about the recognisably human world; and that their difference is rooted in the bihemispheric structure of the brain." The hemispheres need to co-operate, but McGilchrist holds they are in fact engaged in "a sort of power struggle" — one that explains much of contemporary Western culture.

## The Structure of This Book

The book, "like the brain it describes," is divided into two parts.

**Part I** focuses on the brain itself: its evolution, its divided and asymmetrical nature, the development of music and language, and what actually happens on each side. McGilchrist previews his key methodological move here: almost everything once thought to be the exclusive province of one hemisphere is now known to occur in both — so the pursuit of hemisphere difference is not derailed by this, but "right on track," because the real difference lies not in *what* each hemisphere does but in *how* — not "the means by which" (a machine-model formulation) but "the manner in which," a distinction "no one ever asked of a machine." He states plainly: "I am not interested purely in 'functions' but in ways of being, something only living things can have."

He previews a cluster of questions Part I will address: why language centers ended up predominantly in the LH; why language nonetheless also depends on the RH; whether music is a mere spin-off of language or something more profound; why humans are right- or left-handed rather than ambidextrous; whether the body is essential to our way of being or just a "fuelling and locomotor system"; whether emotion is merely an aid to cognition or something more fundamental; and why it matters that one hemisphere sees things in context while the other removes them from it.

He singles out one of the more durable generalizations in the literature: the LH tends to deal with pieces of information in isolation, while the RH deals with the entity as a whole — the **Gestalt** — possibly underlying the verbal/visual dichotomy, since words are processed serially while pictures are taken in all at once. He argues this distinction has been systematically undervalued, treated as trivial ("like finding that cats like to have their meat chopped up into small bits, whereas dogs like to wolf their meat in slabs"), when in fact — if one hemisphere understands metaphor and the other does not — "this is not a small matter of a quaint literary function," but goes "to the core of how we understand our world, even our selves."

A central methodological claim follows: "Things change according to the stance we adopt towards them, the type of attention we pay to them, the disposition we hold in relation to them." The most fundamental difference between the hemispheres, he argues, lies in **the type of attention they give to the world**. He positions this against a false dichotomy he sees as widespread — either naïve realism/scientific materialism (things exist "out there," unaltered by our examination of them) or naïve idealism/post-modernism (things are purely subjective creations we're free to treat as we wish). McGilchrist rejects both: "there is something that exists apart from ourselves, but that we play a vital part in bringing it into being." The kind of attention we pay "actually alters the world: we are, literally, partners in creation" — which he says confers "a grave responsibility." Part I will close with reflections on the relationship between the hemispheres themselves, which "coexist together on a daily basis" yet hold "fundamentally different sets of values" and are therefore likely, over the long term, to come into conflict.

**Part II** turns to the history of Western culture read through this hemispheric lens. McGilchrist previews that it begins with the development of writing and currency in **Ancient Greece**, and the flowering of science and the arts (especially theatre) at that time — which he links to the growth, via enhanced frontal lobe function, of a "**necessary distance**" from the world that in turn allowed the hemispheres greater independence and, for a time, harmonious co-operation. Over time, he argues, growing self-consciousness has produced increasing difficulty in that co-operation, manifesting as historical swings between more extreme positions. Crucially, he asserts the pendulum has not swung neutrally: "the balance of power has shifted where it cannot afford to go — further and further towards the part-world created by the left hemisphere." The book follows these swings from the Renaissance onward to the present.

He states the stakes explicitly: although both hemispheres are essential and each needs the other, the relationship is **not symmetrical** — the LH is "ultimately dependent on, one might almost say parasitic on, the right," though apparently unaware of this and "filled with an alarming self-confidence." His hope is that "awareness of the situation may enable us to change course before it is too late."

The **Conclusion** ("The Master Betrayed," previewed here though not yet titled in this Introduction) will argue that the LH has effectively sealed off the exits from its own "self-reflexive virtual world" — a "hall of mirrors" — that could otherwise lead back to a reality the RH could disclose. Historically, this tendency was checked by forces outside the self-conscious mind: cultural history, the natural world, embodied existence, the arts, and religion. McGilchrist argues each of these counterbalances has now been subverted, producing "an increasingly mechanistic, fragmented, decontextualised world, marked by unwarranted optimism mixed with paranoia and a feeling of emptiness" — the signature, he says, of "the unopposed action of a dysfunctional left hemisphere."

McGilchrist forestalls a likely misreading: he is not anti-reason or anti-language. "Both are seriously under threat in our age, though I believe from diametrically opposed factions." He criticizes post-modern theorists who he says misappropriate Heidegger's "careful anti-Cartesian scepticism" into "an anarchic disregard for language and meaning," while equally criticizing those who traduce language by never questioning it and who claim truth too easily. He states his quarrel is only with "an excessive and misplaced rationalism which has never been subjected to the judgment of reason." Likewise he clarifies he is not opposed to science — "which, like its sister arts, is the offspring of both hemispheres" — only to "a narrow materialism, which is not intrinsic to science at all." Science, he writes, "is neither more nor less than patient and detailed attention to the world."

## Why Is the Structure of the Brain Important?

McGilchrist addresses a plausible objection: isn't linking philosophy and the arts to brain structure reductive? He argues it is not — partly because even if mind were "reduced" to matter, this would force us to upgrade our conception of what matter itself is capable of being (namely, something as extraordinary as mind). More directly: the way we experience the world, and even what there is to experience, depends on how the brain functions — some potential objects of experience (e.g., sounds at frequencies audible to bats or bears) are simply unavailable to us because our brains don't process them. Losing parts of the brain means losing a corresponding "chunk of available experience" — evidence, he notes, that experience cannot be located solely "in the brain," since removing brain tissue demonstrably removes access to a world that must exist independently.

He addresses why brain *structure* specifically matters (as opposed to just "mind"): we can inspect the brain only from outside, but the mind only from within — so studying brain structure is simply the more tractable route to understanding mental experience, given that structure and function are closely related.

Two historical touchstones are invoked:
- **Freud** anticipated that neuroscience would eventually allow the mental structures he described — the **id, the ego, and the superego** — to be identified with specific brain structures, believing the brain not merely mediates experience but shapes it.
- **Jung** is quoted at length on the mind's evolutionary layering: "Just as the human body represents a whole museum of organs, with a long evolutionary history behind them, so we should expect the mind to be organized in a similar way … We receive along with our body a highly differentiated brain which brings with it its entire history, and when it becomes creative it creates out of this history — out of the history of mankind … that age-old natural history which has been transmitted in living form since the remotest times, namely the history of the brain structure."

McGilchrist notes a key structural principle: unlike most bodily organs, later brain developments do not replace earlier ones but add to and build on top of them — the cortex arose atop older subcortical structures governing unconscious biological regulation, and the frontal lobes (the most recently evolved neocortical region, proportionally much larger in humans than in other animals) grow forward "on top of" the rest of the cortex and mediate planning, decision-making, perspective-taking, and self-control. The upshot: brain structure reflects its own evolutionary history, "an evolving dynamic system, in which one part evolves out of, and in response to, another." He closes this section with a claim he will return to throughout the book: "the brain is — in fact it has to be — a metaphor of the world."

## The Importance of Being Two

McGilchrist notes the brain is estimated to contain more connections within itself than there are particles in the known universe, yet the densest interconnections form locally, between adjacent structures — making the brain resemble "a huge country," a nested hierarchy of villages, towns, districts, counties, regions, and semi-autonomous states, corresponding to nuclei, ganglia, gyri, sulci, lobes, and ultimately the two cerebral hemispheres. If consciousness arises from or is mediated by the density and complexity of these interconnections, then the fact that the brain is divided in two at its highest level of organization must be significant.

He invokes physiologist **Sir Charles Sherrington**, who a century earlier described **"opponent processors"** as a basic principle of sensorimotor control — illustrated by the everyday example of one hand steadying and counterbalancing a delicate movement of the other. McGilchrist aligns himself with **Marcel Kinsbourne**'s view that the brain as a whole is a system of opponent processors, containing mutually opposed elements whose contrary influence enables finely calibrated responses. Kinsbourne identifies three such oppositional pairings likely to be significant:
1. **Up/down** — cortical inhibition of subcortical automatic responses
2. **Front/back** — frontal lobe inhibition of the posterior cortex
3. **Right/left** — the mutual influence of the two hemispheres

McGilchrist states the book concentrates on the third pairing, the hemispheric duality, though the other two will surface where relevant — especially since the hemispheres differ in their relationships to subcortical structures and the frontal lobes. This primary duality, he argues, "underlies a conflict that is playing itself out around us" and has "recently taken a turn which should cause us concern."

## Differences Are Not Absolute, But Even Small Differences Get to Be Amplified

McGilchrist enters the first of two methodological caveats. When he says "the left hemisphere does this" or "the right hemisphere does that," both hemispheres are in fact actively involved in almost all mental processes; information passes between them constantly, potentially several times a second. What shows up on a brain scan is partly an artifact of where the activity threshold is set — low enough, and one sees activity almost everywhere. At the level of experience, though, the world we know is synthesised from the work of both hemispheres, asymmetrically, and which hemisphere's version predominates at a given moment determines what we consciously experience.

Two mechanisms explain how small underlying differences produce large downstream effects:

1. **Winner-takes-all dynamics** (citing **Ornstein**): if one hemisphere is, say, 85% as efficient at a task as the other, the brain does not split the work 0.85:1.00 — it tends to hand the whole job to whichever hemisphere is better. And if the "wrong" hemisphere happens to seize control first on a low-demand task, it will likely continue to dominate even where the other hemisphere would have been the better choice from the outset — possibly because the cost of transferring control exceeds the cost of continuing suboptimally. (McGilchrist notes he returns to hemisphere "working relationships" in the last chapter of Part I.)

2. **Snowball/entrenchment effects**: repeated preferential use of one hemisphere for a task reinforces the routing of related information to that hemisphere in future, so that "small initial differences between the hemispheres could compound during development, ultimately producing a wide range of functional asymmetries, via a 'snowball' mechanism." The hemispheres are thus "involved in differentiating themselves."

He also addresses how to interpret data of this kind, using an analogy: average temperatures in Iceland and Indonesia differ enormously and explain real differences in vegetation, culture, and economy — even though the lowest average annual temperature in Indonesia is still lower than Iceland's highest, and both vary considerably by month, day, and location. "The nature of generalisations is that they are approximate, but they are nonetheless of critical importance for understanding what is going on. A misplaced need for certainty may stop the process altogether."

This leads to a warning against simplistic dichotomizing: generalizations about hemispheres "can never be rules," and nothing is confined entirely to one hemisphere or the other. McGilchrist invokes **Descartes** — dualist not just of mind and matter but of "two types of thinking, two types of bodily movement, even two types of loving," and famously of two types of people ("the world is largely composed of two types of minds") — as a foil, aligning himself instead with the joke that "the world is divided into two types of people, those who divide the world into two types of people, and those who don't. I am with the second group." His point: "Nature gave us the dichotomy when she split the brain. Working out what it means is not in itself to dichotomise; it only becomes so in the hands of those who interpret the results with Cartesian rigidity."

## Brain Organisation Varies From Individual to Individual

The second caveat concerns individual variation in hemisphere dominance and laterality. McGilchrist gives the population statistics underlying his use of "the right hemisphere" and "the left hemisphere" as generalizations:

- In the West, about **89%** of people are broadly right-handed, and the vast majority of these have speech and semantic language centers in the LH — the **"standard pattern."**
- Of the remaining **11%** who are broadly left-handed, about **75%** still have speech centers in the LH, following the standard pattern.
- This leaves only about **5%** of the population overall who do not lateralize speech to the LH. Logically, this remaining group must follow one of three configurations: the standard pattern, a simple inversion of it (in which case one should simply read "right" for "left" throughout the book, with "little significance" otherwise), or some other rearrangement.

It is this third group — a subset of left-handers, plus some individuals with conditions such as (probably) **schizophrenia** and **dyslexia**, and possibly **schizotypy**, some forms of **autism**, **Asperger's syndrome**, and some "savant" conditions — who may show a partial inversion of the standard pattern, with functions lateralized in unconventional combinations, potentially conferring special advantages or disadvantages.

McGilchrist poses a specific question about this group: if language migrates into what would normally be RH territory (or vice versa), does the coexisting function get "reinterpreted" toward the mode of its new hemisphere, or does it transform that hemisphere's other functions toward its own mode instead? He illustrates with an image: does placing a maths professor in a circus troupe produce "a flying mathematician, or a bunch of trapeze artists who can no longer perform unless they have first calculated the precise trajectory of their leap"? He suggests both outcomes likely occur in different individuals, and speculates this may explain the puzzle of why genes associated with major mental illness (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) and developmental conditions (autism, Asperger's) persist in the population despite their costs to fertility — if such genes, via effects on lateralization, sometimes confer extraordinary talent (especially in relatives carrying only some of the genes), Darwinian selection would preserve them. He also notes unconventional lateralization "may be associated with homosexuality," which he says is thought to involve a higher-than-usual incidence of abnormal lateralization.

Setting these atypical cases aside as beyond the book's scope, McGilchrist states his approach: the book deals only with "the typical cerebral organisation, the one that has greater than 95 per cent currency and which, by the same 'winner takes all' argument, has universal applicability to the world in which we live for now."

## Essential Asymmetry

The chapter's closing section opens with a quotation from the French poet **Paul Valéry**: "The universe is built on a plan, the profound symmetry of which is somehow present in the inner structure of our intellect." McGilchrist calls this "at one and the same time a brilliant insight into the nature of reality, and about as wrong as it is possible to be" — because the universe, he argues, exhibits not profound symmetry but **profound asymmetry**. He cites **Louis Pasteur**: "Life as manifested to us is a function of the asymmetry of the universe … I can even imagine that all living species are primordially, in their structure, in their external forms, functions of cosmic asymmetry." He notes physicists have since deduced that a fundamental matter/antimatter asymmetry was necessary for the material universe to exist at all, and that unidirectional processes like time and entropy may be further expressions of this asymmetry. The human intellect, he concludes, is itself asymmetrical "in a sense that has enormous significance for us."

He restates the thesis of two fundamentally opposed realities rooted in the bihemispheric brain, but stresses that the relationship between them is **not symmetrical** — "no more symmetrical than that of the chambers of the heart — in fact, less so; more like that of the artist to the critic, or a king to his counsellor."

This sets up **the book's central parable**, adapted from a story in **Nietzsche**: a wise spiritual master ruled a small, prosperous domain with selfless devotion to his people. As his people flourished and the domain's bounds grew, he came to depend on emissaries to govern its increasingly distant reaches — not only because he could not personally attend to everything, but because he wisely understood he needed to keep his distance from, and remain ignorant of, such concerns. He carefully trained his emissaries to be trustworthy. But his cleverest and most trusted vizier "began to see himself as the master," used his position to enrich himself and expand his own influence, saw the master's temperance as weakness rather than wisdom, and grew contemptuous of him. "And so it came about that the master was usurped, the people were duped, the domain became a tyranny; and eventually it collapsed in ruins."

McGilchrist states directly that this story helps explain something happening inside our own brains and played out in Western cultural history "particularly over the last 500 years or so" — the subject of the whole book. Like the Master and his emissary, the hemispheres *should* co-operate but "have for some time been in a state of conflict," a conflict recorded in the history of philosophy and in the major shifts of Western culture. He states where he believes things currently stand: "At present the domain — our civilisation — finds itself in the hands of the vizier, who, however gifted, is effectively an ambitious regional bureaucrat with his own interests at heart. Meanwhile the Master, the one whose wisdom gave the people peace and security, is led away in chains." The Introduction ends on the book's title thesis stated as a single sentence: **"The Master is betrayed by his emissary."**
