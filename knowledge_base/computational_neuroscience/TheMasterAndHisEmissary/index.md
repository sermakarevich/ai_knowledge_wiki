# The Master and His Emissary

Iain McGilchrist's 2009 book (751 pp., Yale University Press) argues that the brain's hemispheric asymmetry is not a division of subject matter but of two rival modes of attending to the world — and that Western civilisation's cultural history, from Ancient Greece to Post-Modernism, traces a repeated, and increasingly one-sided, swing toward the narrow, abstracting, mechanistic mode of the left hemisphere. It was worth ingesting for its sustained, interdisciplinary argument (neurology, philosophy, art history, literary criticism) about *how a mode of attention shapes the reality it attends to* — a claim with direct bearing on questions of embodiment, meaning, and mechanisation that recur in AI/agent-engineering debates about what gets lost when cognition is reduced to explicit, formalisable procedure.
