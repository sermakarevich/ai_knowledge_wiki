# The Tasteful Agent: Measuring and Improving Taste in Long-Horizon Tasks

**Paper:** [The Tasteful Agent: Measuring and Improving Taste in Long-Horizon Tasks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25804) — arXiv, September 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Good engineering judgment is like picking the right hiking trail at a fork when both paths look equally inviting — you only learn which was better miles later, after hours of walking. This paper gives AI agents a test for exactly that kind of foresight: it rewinds real agent work to the moment before a fateful choice, hides everything that happened next, and asks which direction would have paid off. Even the best models pick correctly only about 60% of the time on a two-way choice, and thinking longer does not help, much like staring harder at the trailhead sign cannot reveal what lies around the bend. The encouraging news is that taste can be taught: showing a smaller model how an expert reasons about past successes and failures makes it choose better on brand-new tasks and finish more real software jobs end to end.

## TL;DR

The paper formalizes agent taste as long-horizon judgment — choosing the better direction at a decision fork before its advantage is visible — and introduces Taste-Bench, a 502-question benchmark mined automatically from software-engineering and ML-research agent trajectories by freezing runs at forks and labeling the better direction with recorded hindsight outcomes. Two complementary constructions cover mistakes the agent never noticed (parallel attempts at the same task with opposite outcomes) and mistakes it later corrected (detours within a single run), with strict filtering so every released question is non-trivial yet unanimously decidable from the full record, a property confirmed by near-unanimous human review. Frontier models score poorly, with the best reaching only 59.7% under a both-orders-correct binary-choice protocol, errors concentrating on long-horizon forks and resisting larger reasoning budgets, while Taste-Bench separates top models that end-to-end benchmarks compress and correlates only partly with SWE-bench Verified. Taste proves trainable: distilling the reasoning of a privileged teacher that has seen the supported direction into a Qwen3.6-27B student via LoRA improves held-out judgment substantially and lifts end-to-end success on unseen SWE-bench Pro tasks from 14.6% to 33.7% when the student's judgments are injected as advice.

## Problem & Motivation

Long-horizon agent runs — evolving large software projects across releases, conducting ML research from idea to paper, refining scaffolds during deployment — increasingly turn on single decisions whose costs surface only much later, after large budgets are spent. A clean implementation passes the same tests as a hasty one on the day it is written, and its advantage appears only when every later change becomes easier; likewise one training recipe looks equivalent to another until final losses diverge. Existing benchmarks measure only whether the agent eventually completes the task and provide no read on the quality of the decisions along the way, so there is no measure of agent taste. Direct measurement is hard because the payoff of a long-horizon choice is invisible at decision time and because judging quality demands deep domain expertise that is expensive to scale. The paper's key observation is that the later part of a trajectory already contains hindsight evidence for its earlier decisions: independent attempts at the same task diverge mid-run with recorded outcomes identifying the better branch, and single runs contain detours where the agent takes a wrong direction, hits a wall, and recovers, so trajectories themselves supply labeled comparisons without expert annotation.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Taste as hindsight-labeled fork choice.** Taste is defined as the ability to make good long-horizon decisions whose influence extends beyond the current step, and operationalized as picking the supported candidate at a decision fork: each question is a tuple of task, shared trajectory prefix, and two candidate directions, with the label set by whichever branch's recorded evidence scores higher and everything after the fork hidden at test time. Model taste is then simply the fraction of questions answered with the supported candidate, evaluated under a both-orders-correct protocol that defeats position bias.

2. **Taste-Bench mined from parallel and detour forks.** The benchmark is built by two complementary constructions over real trajectories: parallel forks align pairs of attempts at the same task with opposite outcomes at their divergence point, while detour forks locate a commit-to-poor-approach, observed failure, and recovery-to-success triple inside one completed run and test recognizing the failure earlier than the acting agent did. A generator proposes thousands of candidate forks under a strict rubric requiring causal attribution, neutral parallel wording, verbatim evidence quotes, and rejection whenever the fork is not clean, and two judge-panel filters then drop trivial questions answerable from candidates alone and keep only questions every judge agrees with given the full record, yielding 502 released questions across engineering and research domains.

3. **Time-horizon analysis of taste failures.** Every fork is annotated by how far past the fork an observer must look before the supported direction is clearly justified, from evidence already visible in the prefix through inferable-from-hints, next-observation, and substantial-future-work levels. This turns a vague sense that some decisions are harder into a measured gradient showing accuracy collapsing as the deciding evidence recedes into the future, with models reasoning longest precisely where they score worst.

4. **Taste distillation from privileged reasoning.** Rather than fitting bare binary labels, which would invite memorization, the paper distills complete reasoning sequences from a privileged teacher — the same base model shown the supported direction — into a student that sees only the benchmark context, using token-level forward-KL distillation over teacher-sampled continuations plus a light calibration step, trained and evaluated on task-disjoint folds. The student's judgments are then written as pitfall-note advice into the context of a fixed executor, showing that better judgment transfers to unseen tasks and converts into end-to-end task success.

## Key Findings

Frontier models show limited taste: across 14 contemporary models the best average reaches only 59.7% on binary choices, with per-model strengths diverging across research and engineering subsets and detour forks proving harder than parallel forks by a wider margin than the domain gap. Errors concentrate on long-horizon forks, with mean accuracy falling from 62.3% at the in-prefix level to 21.0% at the more-work level, near the 25% random-guessing rate of the both-orders-correct metric. A larger reasoning budget does not improve taste: moving from the lowest to highest effort settings shifts accuracy by fractions of a point with overlapping intervals at every horizon, and the models emit the most reasoning tokens at the level where they score lowest, suggesting the deciding evidence genuinely appears only in later work. Taste-Bench is not a restatement of end-to-end ability: it correlates only partly with SWE-bench Verified, explains a minority of cross-model variance, and spreads the top compressed models widely apart, while human reviewers agree with mined labels on 98.8% of retained judgments. Taste is trainable and transfers: the distilled student rises from 30.0% to 47.9% held-out accuracy under the strict protocol with large single-order gains, selects the supported candidate on a large majority of forks, and its advice lifts a fixed executor on 41 unseen SWE-bench Pro tasks from 14.6% to 33.7% success, close to the 39.0% upper bound of always-correct advice.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The paper itself points toward using Taste-Bench as a practical basis for both measuring and training the judgment of long-horizon agents, with released data, evaluation code, and recorded results supporting follow-up work on the benchmark's harder detour and far-horizon slices. Natural extensions include training larger and more diverse students, distilling research-domain as well as engineering-domain taste, and tightening the loop between judgment training and agentic execution so improved fork choices compound over full runs. Open methodological directions include richer horizon-aware evaluation, better handling of position sensitivity and unparseable outputs, and mining additional trajectory pools beyond the SWE-bench Pro and RE-Bench/HCAST sources used here. The authors also flag dual-use considerations around measuring long-horizon judgment and note that continued investment in trajectory-grounded, outcome-labeled supervision offers a scalable alternative to expensive expert annotation.
