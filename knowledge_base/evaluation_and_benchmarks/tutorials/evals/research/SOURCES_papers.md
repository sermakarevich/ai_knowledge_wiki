# Sources: LLM Evaluation Research Literature & Benchmark Methodology

Compiled 2026-09-03. Covers LLM-as-a-judge, benchmark methodology/statistics, RAG evaluation, agent
evaluation, standard model benchmarks, and human evaluation/annotation. All arXiv IDs and URLs were
checked live via web search on the compile date; PDFs for the ~12 highest-priority papers are saved
under `research/` (plus `2411.00640` and `2404.12272`, already present in the parent directory).

### Human evaluation & annotation references (background, not separately downloaded)

- Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) and Krippendorff's alpha (Krippendorff, 1980/2004) — standard inter-annotator agreement statistics; kappa for 2 annotators/nominal categories, alpha generalizes to multiple annotators, missing data, and ordinal/interval scales.
- Pairwise preference collection methodology and Bradley-Terry/Elo rating conversion, as operationalized by Chatbot Arena (arXiv:2403.04132) — the standard way human A/B preference votes become a single leaderboard score with confidence intervals.

## Key findings a tutorial must teach

- **A single LLM judge is a biased, noisy instrument, not ground truth.** Position bias can flip verdicts in pairwise comparisons (Wang et al., 2305.17926), and judges systematically prefer longer answers unless corrected (Length-Controlled AlpacaEval, 2404.04475) and prefer outputs from their own model family (self-preference bias, documented across the LLM-as-judge survey literature, 2411.15594 / 2412.05579).
- **Panels beat single judges.** A panel of diverse, even smaller/cheaper, LLM judges (PoLL, 2404.18796) correlates better with human judgment and is materially cheaper than one large judge — teach "jury," not "judge," as the default pattern.
- **Judges need their own benchmark.** JudgeBench (2410.12784) shows frontier LLM judges are barely better than chance on genuinely hard, objectively verifiable cases — never trust a judge's accuracy claim without testing it against known-correct labels first.
- **Judge reliability is still an open, actively studied problem in 2026.** Swapping the judge model changes which system "wins" (2607.08535); large-scale audits find weak domain-specific calibration and no agreed meta-evaluation standard (2606.19544); calibration techniques like linear-probe uncertainty estimation (2512.22245) are emerging but not yet standard practice.
- **Every eval number needs an error bar.** Miller (2411.00640) shows most reported score deltas between models are within measurement noise; report variance/CIs (e.g., clustered SEs across questions), not just point estimates.
- **Benchmark contamination and overfitting are real and measurable.** GSM1k (2405.00332) shows some model families lose up to 8 points on a held-out, matched-difficulty test set versus GSM8K, with a measurable correlation to memorization probability — always ask "could this model have seen this benchmark?"
- **The metric you choose determines whether "emergence" appears at all.** Schaeffer et al. (2304.15004) show apparent emergent capability jumps are often an artifact of discontinuous metrics (exact-match); smooth per-token metrics reveal smooth scaling — pick metrics deliberately, not by convention.
- **Small implementation details silently change scores by double digits.** The lm-evaluation-harness paper (2405.14782) documents how prompt formatting, few-shot example choice, and answer-extraction regexes can swing results — always report exact eval harness/version/prompt template alongside a score.
- **Benchmarks saturate and need replacement on a cadence.** A 2026 systematic study (2602.16763) finds roughly half of 60 widely used benchmarks are highly saturated, with older benchmarks saturating faster; MMLU/GSM8K/HumanEval have effectively been retired by frontier labs in favor of HLE, GPQA Diamond, SWE-bench Verified, LiveCodeBench, and τ/τ²-bench.
- **RAG evaluation should be reference-free where possible but grounded where it matters.** RAGAS's triad (faithfulness, answer relevance, context relevance/precision, 2309.15217) avoids needing gold answers; ARES (2311.09476) adds statistical rigor (prediction-powered inference, confidence intervals) using a small human-labeled seed set — teach both the cheap reference-free approach and the more rigorous statistically-calibrated one.
- **Faithfulness/hallucination detection has a spectrum of cost vs. rigor**: FActScore-style atomic-fact decomposition (2305.14251) is thorough but expensive; SelfCheckGPT (2303.08896) needs no external knowledge but needs multiple samples; MiniCheck (2404.10774) and LettuceDetect (2502.17125) give fast, cheap, near-GPT-4-quality fact-checking suitable for production gating.
- **Agent evaluation must score trajectories, not just final answers.** τ-bench introduced pass^k to measure reliability across repeated trials rather than single-shot success (2406.12045); AgentLens (2605.12925) shows agents can reach correct final states via "lucky" degenerate paths; TRAJECT-Bench (2510.04550) and AgentAtlas (2605.20530) argue for scoring tool-call correctness, step efficiency, and pass@1-vs-pass@k consistency as first-class metrics.
- **Dual-control/multi-turn agent settings are much harder than single-control ones.** τ²-bench (2506.07982) shows agent pass^1 drops by roughly 20 points when the agent must coordinate with an active user rather than act autonomously.
- **Coding-agent benchmarks decay fast.** SWE-bench Verified became the default 2024–2025 capability metric after OpenAI's human-reviewed 500-problem curation, but by 2026 OpenAI itself stated it no longer discriminates between frontier models, illustrating the saturation lifecycle in real time.
- **Human evaluation still needs its own rigor**: report inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa for 2 raters, Krippendorff's alpha for more raters/missing data/ordinal scales), and when converting pairwise human preferences into a leaderboard, use a Bradley-Terry/Elo model with confidence intervals, as Chatbot Arena does (2403.04132) — Arena's own 2026 leaderboard shows top models within ~20 Elo points of each other, i.e., statistically indistinguishable rank differences.

## Open questions / contested points

- **No agreed meta-evaluation standard for judges.** Multiple 2025–2026 papers (2606.19544, 2607.08535) explicitly call out that the field lacks a standard protocol for validating a judge before trusting it in production; practices vary widely across labs.
- **Outcome-only vs. trajectory-based agent scoring.** There is active disagreement about whether final-task-success is sufficient or whether trajectory-level scoring (tool-call correctness, step efficiency, "lucky pass" detection) should be mandatory — 2026 papers (AgentLens, AgentAtlas, TRAJECT-Bench) argue the former overstates real capability, but trajectory scoring is more expensive and less standardized.
- **When has a benchmark "saturated," and what should replace it?** The 2602.16763 saturation study gives a quantitative framework, but there's no consensus threshold, and replacement benchmarks (HLE, GPQA Diamond, τ²-bench) are themselves young enough that their own contamination/saturation timelines are unknown.
- **Is crowdsourced human preference (Chatbot Arena/Elo) a valid ground truth, or itself biased?** Arena voting is subject to selection effects (who chooses to vote, which prompts get submitted) and is sensitive to response style/length in ways similar to LLM judges; the field has not settled whether Arena Elo should be treated as a gold standard or as one more biased signal to be combined with others.
- **SWE-bench Verified's declining discriminative power** (OpenAI's own 2026 statement that it no longer separates frontier models) versus the lack of an agreed successor — Terminal-Bench, SWE-bench-CL, and others are candidates but none has yet achieved SWE-bench's earlier status as the default reported number.
- **Reference-free vs. reference-grounded RAG metrics.** RAGAS-style reference-free scoring is cheap and popular but its correlation with human judgment of faithfulness is debated; ARES's prediction-powered-inference approach is more statistically defensible but requires a human-labeled seed set that many teams skip in practice.
- **GSM1k-style overfitting evidence is mixed across model families** — some frontier labs (Gemini, GPT, Claude per the original paper) show little to no drop, so contamination is not uniform, complicating any blanket claim that "benchmark X is contaminated for all models."
