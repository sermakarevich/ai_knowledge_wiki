## Key ideas a tutorial must teach

- **Evals ≠ benchmarks.** A benchmark measures a foundation model in general; an eval measures *your* pipeline on *your* task with *your* data. Generic public benchmarks tell you nothing about whether your specific RAG app or agent is failing (Hamel & Shreya, AI Evals FAQ; Hamel, "Your AI Product Needs Evals").
- **Look at your data — first, always, and continuously.** The single highest-leverage practice across every source is manually reading a large sample of real (or synthetic) traces before writing any automated metric. Friction in this loop (no custom viewer, generic dashboards) is the #1 reason eval efforts stall (Hamel, "Field Guide"; Hamel, "Your AI Product Needs Evals").
- **Error analysis via open coding → axial coding.** Read traces and write open-ended notes on failures (open coding); then cluster those notes into a small taxonomy of recurring failure modes (axial coding); only then build automated checks/graders for the modes that actually occur, in order of frequency/severity (Hamel & Shreya, AI Evals FAQ; Hamel, "Field Guide").
- **Three levels of evaluation, run at different cadences.** Level 1: fast, cheap unit-test-style assertions (regex, schema checks) run on every change; Level 2: human- and LLM-graded evaluation over logged traces run periodically; Level 3: A/B tests run only after a change ships (Hamel, "Your AI Product Needs Evals").
- **Binary pass/fail beats Likert scales for most grading.** Binary judgments are faster to produce, more reproducible between human raters, and easier to align an LLM judge against than 1-5 Likert scores, which practitioners tend to compress toward 3-4 anyway (Hamel & Shreya, AI Evals FAQ; Hamel, "Field Guide"; Anthropic docs also default to binary/discrete outputs for LLM-graded evals for the same reason of scalability, though its worked examples still use Likert/ordinal scales for tone and context — a real tension, see Disagreements below).
- **Always align your LLM judge against human labels before trusting it.** Treat the judge itself as a classifier and report TPR/TNR (or precision/recall) against a human-labeled sample, not just raw agreement, since class imbalance makes raw agreement misleading (Hamel, "Your AI Product Needs Evals"; Shankar et al., EvalGen/"Who Validates the Validators").
- **"Criteria drift" is real: grading data helps you write your rubric, and writing your rubric changes how you grade.** Expect several rounds of revising your eval criteria as you look at more graded examples — this isn't a process failure, it's expected (Shankar et al., EvalGen).
- **Custom, purpose-built data viewers/annotation tools outperform generic evaluation dashboards.** Teams that build a lightweight internal tool (Streamlit/Gradio/etc.) tailored to their own trace schema iterate dramatically faster than teams relying on off-the-shelf observability UIs alone (Hamel, both posts).
- **Statistical rigor is usually missing and should not be.** Report confidence intervals / error bars on eval scores, use clustered standard errors when an eval has multiple correlated questions per example, and do a power calculation to decide how many test cases you actually need before concluding "model A beats model B" (Evan Miller, arXiv 2411.00640).
- **RAG evaluation splits into retrieval quality and generation/response quality**, and each needs its own metrics (e.g., retrieval: recall@k/precision@k/MRR against a labeled relevant-doc set; response: groundedness/faithfulness to retrieved context, answer relevance) (Arize AI guide; Anthropic Claude docs' "context utilization" criterion).
- **Agent evaluation is qualitatively different from single-turn LLM evaluation.** You must define tasks, run multiple trials (agents are stochastic — track pass@k and pass^k, not just a single pass/fail), evaluate the full transcript (tool calls, retries, reasoning) not just the final message, and combine code-based, model-based, and human graders depending on the agent archetype (coding, conversational, research, computer-use) (Anthropic, "Demystifying evals for AI agents").
- **Grading-method hierarchy: prefer the fastest reliable method first** — code-based/exact-match > human grading (flexible but slow/expensive, avoid at scale) > LLM-based grading (fast, flexible, needs validation) — and only reach for LLM-as-judge once code-based checks are exhausted (Anthropic Claude docs; Braintrust).
- **LLM-as-judge has well-documented, predictable biases**: position bias (order of options), verbosity bias (preferring longer answers), self-preference bias (judge favors outputs resembling its own family's style), and difficulty generalizing across tasks — mitigate via randomized ordering, length-controlled prompts, and reference-based/grounded grading rather than open-ended preference (Eugene Yan, "Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators"; Braintrust).
- **Prioritize eval volume (many automated, slightly-noisy checks) over a handful of high-quality hand-graded evals** once you have a validated grader, because volume gives you statistical power and catches regressions across a wider slice of real usage (Anthropic Claude docs; Hamel & Shreya AI Evals FAQ).
- **Build the eval infrastructure to double as debugging and fine-tuning-data-curation infrastructure** — trace logging, filtering, and pass/fail flags used for evals are the same assets you need to debug production issues and to curate a fine-tuning dataset (Hamel, "Your AI Product Needs Evals").

## Disagreements / caveats between sources

- **Binary vs. Likert/ordinal grading.** Hamel & Shreya's course materials argue strongly for binary pass/fail as the default because it's faster to align a judge against and more reproducible. Anthropic's own Claude docs, by contrast, present Likert-scale and ordinal-scale LLM-graded evals (1-5) as first-class worked examples for tone/style and context-utilization criteria, without flagging the reproducibility risk. A tutorial should teach binary-first but note that ordinal scales remain common in vendor documentation and are acceptable for exploratory/diagnostic scoring, less so for gating CI/CD decisions.
- **How much statistical rigor is realistic.** Evan Miller's paper argues most published and internal evals under-report uncertainty and calls for confidence intervals and power analysis as standard practice. In contrast, the more product-oriented practitioner sources (Hamel, Eugene Yan, vendor docs from LangSmith/Braintrust/Promptfoo) rarely mention statistical significance at all and instead emphasize qualitative error analysis and "look at your data." Both are valid at different maturity stages: error analysis first to find what to measure, statistics later once you're comparing model/prompt variants at scale — but a reader could come away thinking these are competing rather than sequential practices.
- **LLM-as-judge reliability claims vary widely by source and are not directly comparable.** LangSmith's 2025-2026 marketing content cites "~85% judge-human alignment, exceeding ~81% inter-human agreement" from an internal dataset; Eugene Yan's survey cites task-specific Spearman correlations (e.g., ~0.6-0.67) from academic papers using different tasks and metrics. These numbers should never be quoted as a general "LLM judges are as good as humans" claim — alignment must be re-measured per task, per rubric, per model, as EvalGen (Shankar et al.) explicitly warns.
- **Framework consolidation/version drift.** Anthropic has merged what used to be two separate docs ("Define your success criteria" and "Create strong empirical evaluations") into a single page, and the dedicated "Claude Console evaluation tool" doc URL now redirects to that same merged page even though the Console evaluation *feature* (side-by-side comparison, 5-point quality grading, CSV import) still exists as a distinct product surface per Anthropic's `claude.com/blog/evaluate-prompts` announcement. Treat "Claude Console eval tool" as a product feature, and the merged docs page as covering the conceptual/API side — don't assume the docs page is a complete Console walkthrough.
- **openai/evals vs. the newer OpenAI Evals API.** The original `openai/evals` GitHub repo (CLI-based, YAML eval registry) is the historically cited open-source project, but OpenAI's 2025-2026 developer-facing guidance (`developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders`, `.../evaluation-best-practices`) describes a newer, platform-hosted Evals/Graders API that is not the same codebase. A tutorial referencing "OpenAI evals" should disambiguate which one it means, since instructions/CLIs differ.
- **RAG-specific guidance is comparatively thin in the "core" evals-methodology sources.** Hamel explicitly defers RAG evaluation detail to a separate (Jason Liu) post rather than covering it in "Your AI Product Needs Evals," and Eugene Yan's task-specific-metrics post does not treat RAG as its own category. The retrieval-vs-generation split used in this tutorial should be sourced primarily from Arize's guide and Anthropic's "context utilization" criterion, cross-checked against the general error-analysis method from Hamel/Shreya rather than treated as equally canonical.
