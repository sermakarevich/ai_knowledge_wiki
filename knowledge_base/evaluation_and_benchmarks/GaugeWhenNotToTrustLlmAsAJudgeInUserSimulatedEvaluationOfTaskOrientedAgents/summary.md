# GAUGE: When Not to Trust LLM-as-a-Judge in User-Simulated Evaluation of Task-Oriented Agents

**Paper:** [GAUGE: When Not to Trust LLM-as-a-Judge in User-Simulated Evaluation of Task-Oriented Agents (Bodhwani et al., 2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12191)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a restaurant that judges its chefs by asking diners how friendly the waiter was, instead of checking whether the right dish arrived. Most diners leave happy even when the kitchen sent the wrong order, so a chef who charms but miscooks keeps getting promoted. This paper runs exactly that audit on AI customer-service agents: it shows the cheap automated taste-test ranks chefs well when one is obviously better, but flips a coin when the two finalists are close — and that politeness scores say almost nothing about whether the job got done.

## TL;DR

GAUGE (Grounded Audit of User-simulator-and-judge Gate Evaluation) audits the de facto offline release gate for task-oriented agents, in which persona-conditioned LLM (Large Language Model) user-simulators converse with each candidate agent and an LLM-as-a-judge scores the transcripts to decide promotion. Across 25 agents from six providers, four judges, two substrates (τ2-bench retail and airline with a verifiable non-LLM oracle reward, plus SimulatorArena math tutoring), and about 3,700 transcripts, the audit separates two validities that release practice conflates: ranking validity (ordering agents like the verifiable reward) and construct validity (certifying against human satisfaction). The gate's agent ranking matches the verifiable reward broadly (ρ=0.94), yet satisfaction is decorrelated from task success (57.5% of human-satisfied conversations failed the task, ρ=−0.147), close-pair decisions disagree 31% of the time, same-family judges inflate their own provider's agents by about +0.75 on a 7-point scale, and a satisfaction-anchored accept/reject bar admits agents that fail 48–60% of the time.

## Problem & Motivation

Developers of agentic task-oriented LLM agents for customer service, tool-use assistance, and tutoring face three constraints that make evaluation hard: there is no clean held-out test set capturing live user experience, per-candidate human studies are prohibitively slow, and the configuration space of model, prompt, policy, and tools changes fast. The field's answer has become the composite simulator-plus-judge gate: drive each candidate against persona-conditioned user-simulators, score the transcripts with an LLM-judge, and promote the higher-scoring variant, running in CI (Continuous Integration) at a few cents per transcript with no labeled production data. This gate rests on one assumption teams almost never measure, namely that it ranks variants the way a grounded evaluation would. Known judge biases such as self-preference, position bias, and inconsistency are documented in the literature, but their magnitude at the release-decision unit against a verifiable reward was unquantified, and the deeper hazard is that a judge can be human-validated yet mis-anchored when the satisfaction signal it reproduces does not itself track task success. GAUGE is built to measure exactly this, because teams optimize against gates and tune toward satisfaction, which makes a decorrelated anchor an optimization hazard in the sense of Goodhart's law rather than a harmless imperfection.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **GAUGE audit protocol at the release-decision unit** — The paper defines a reusable, fully offline, fine-tuning-free protocol that evaluates agents, not transcripts: for each agent aggregated over personas and domains, it compares the agent-level gate score against a simulator-independent verifiable non-LLM reward (deterministic DB-state and action checks on airline, DB-gated checks on retail), alongside a process-blind LLM human-proxy and a blind three-person human panel, reporting agent-level Spearman correlation with cluster-bootstrapped intervals, broken-vs-working AUC (Area Under the Curve, a discrimination score), and power analysis.

2. **Separation of ranking validity from construct validity** — Borrowing construct validity from measurement theory, GAUGE distinguishes ordering agents correctly (ranking validity) from certifying the right outcome (construct validity), showing a gate can reproduce human satisfaction faithfully (judge–human ρ≈0.83–0.85, near the human–human ceiling of 0.85) while satisfaction itself carries no information about success, so human validation alone cannot bless the gate.

3. **Policy-aware gate versus process-blind proxy contrast** — Two deliberately disjoint rubrics isolate what makes judging work: a policy-aware operations-supervisor judge that reads the full transcript including tool calls, goal, authentication, confirmation, and task resolution, versus a satisfaction-only first-person shopper proxy that sees only user-visible turns and rates how the conversation felt. Only the former halves failure risk (20.0% failures among accepted vs. 40.2% base, AUC 0.73), while the proxy behaves like raw satisfaction (AUC 0.49), proving evaluator reliability comes from evidence access rather than model capability.

4. **Decision-disagreement rate on near-equal pairs** — Instead of reporting only aggregate correlation, the paper counts the fraction of agent pairs where the gate promotes the lower-reward agent, split into near-equal pairs (reward gap below 0.1) versus wide pairs. This exposes that the headline ρ=0.94 is capability dominance over a wide ladder, while the comparisons real releases actually make fail 31.0% of the time against 0.9% on wide pairs.

5. **Calibrate-then-trust with a judge-free tripwire** — The remedy is a cadence rather than a new judge: run the verifiable audit once on a representative benchmark to learn the gate's trusted operating region, then use the cheap gate in CI only inside that region and re-audit on configuration change. A zero-cost judge-free completion bit serves as a truncation-specific tripwire for budget-starvation regressions (ρ=0.87 on degraded configs), while out-of-sample recalibration is shown not to transfer.

## Key Findings

Aggregating over all 25 agents, the gate's ranking matches the verifiable reward with ρ=0.94, and this ordering is robust: it holds with an out-of-family judge (Opus-4.8 vs. GPT-5.5 agreement ρ=0.92; all four judges recover the reward ordering at ρ=0.84–0.94), it is not a capability artifact since the weakest judge-as-agent is a tied-best ranker, it is stable across reruns (test–retest ICC, intraclass correlation, of 0.87), and it survives a full simulator swap from Sonnet-4.5 to GPT-5.4 (ordering preserved at ρ=0.93 over 2,850 re-run transcripts). Yet high aggregate ranking coexists with unreliable resolution of near-equal candidates: on the 87 near-equal pairs the primary judge promotes the worse agent 31.0% of the time (95% CI [11.6, 50.0], still ~13× the 0.9% wide-pair rate), the top-11 strong-agent slice shows non-significant correlation for every judge, no ensemble of 21 cheaper or combined signals beats the best single judge, and the errors are signal-specific rather than shared noise. The satisfaction–success gap is stark and general: 57.5% of conversations the blind panel rated satisfied had failed the task, indistinguishable from the 57.3% base failure rate (AUC 0.44, ρ=−0.147), holding across five rater populations, all five subjective dimensions (respect, clarity, helpfulness, would-return all |ρ|≤0.17), both τ2 domains and SimulatorArena tutoring, every annotator left out, and both simulator providers. Concrete cases make the mechanism vivid: a retail agent scoring 7/7 everywhere while leaving a pending order unmodified, an airline rebooking that felt perfect but missed the required end-state, a math tutor rated 10/10 by humans while its own shown work contradicted its answer, and a step-starved degraded config with zero reward but the highest satisfaction in its tier because truncated trajectories read as helpful turn-by-turn. Same-family self-preference is isolated at +0.75/7 without changing the ranking, and a satisfaction-anchored threshold admits agents failing roughly half the time, confirming the paper's refrain that subjective approval is decorrelated from success and the gate is valid only within a bounded operating region.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The paper's central prescription is calibrate-then-trust: teams should run the verifiable audit once on a representative benchmark to map where the gate agrees with grounded reward, use the cheap simulator-plus-judge gate in continuous integration only within that trusted region, and re-audit whenever the agent configuration, simulator, judge, or domain changes rather than on a fixed schedule. Practically, it recommends policy-aware rubrics that check authentication, confirmation, tool discipline, and whether the task resolved, instead of satisfaction-only scoring, since evidence access rather than judge model strength determines reliability. It scopes the judge-free completion bit strictly as a truncation-regression tripwire for inference-budget starvation (token caps, step caps, error aborts) rather than a general quality detector, because on the naturalistic grid where most failures terminate normally it collapses. It warns that out-of-sample recalibration does not transfer, that averaging correlated judges does not fix close-pair errors, and that satisfaction thresholds should never serve as absolute ship/no-ship bars. Natural follow-ups include extending the audit to more substrates and failure modes beyond truncation, designing close-pair-sensitive evaluation instruments, and building gates whose trusted regions are characterized before they are used as optimization targets.
