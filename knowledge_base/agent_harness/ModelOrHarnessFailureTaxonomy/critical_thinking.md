# Critical Analysis: Model or Harness? An Interaction-Centric Taxonomy for Localizing Agent Failures

## Claims vs. evidence

**"Independent LLM judges recover the human-assigned labels."** Suggestive, not strong. The headline numbers (category κ up to 0.76, exact-match accuracy up to 0.80, judge-judge κ up to 0.84) are computed on **40 examples** — the same corpus the authors used to iteratively build and then freeze the taxonomy (wiki 02: "the example set... doubles as the evaluation set for §6"). That is not a held-out test; the category boundaries were reshaped whenever a construction-phase case "exposed an overlap or unclear boundary" until the categories fit the cases cleanly. Good agreement on the cases the taxonomy was tuned against tells us less than agreement on unseen cases would. No confidence intervals are reported for κ at n=40 — several of the 10 edges have only 1–2 examples (Peer: 1, Local Env.: 1, Memory: 2), so per-edge accuracy is fragile.

**Judge independence.** Weak. Three of the four judges are Claude Opus point releases (4.6/4.7/4.8) sharing base architecture and training lineage; only GPT-5.5 is a genuinely different lineage. The highest pairwise judge-judge κ (0.84) is between two Opus versions — plausibly shared model family bias, not independent convergence on ground truth. The paper's own epistemic argument (judge-judge ≈ judge-human agreement implies "shared structure, not idiosyncrasy") is undercut by its own case study: the Harbor-Mix example shows judges share a systematic bias (default to blaming the model) that is unrelated to the human label. Shared bias inflates judge-judge agreement without validating the taxonomy.

**"Most failures are model-side" (36/41 modes, 32/40 worked examples).** The authors themselves flag this as an artifact of a deliberately permissive attribution rule ("model-side whenever a more capable model could have recovered"), not an empirical finding — this is a genuine strength: they do not oversell their own skewed distribution. But it also means the reported 0.80 category accuracy for GPT-5.5 is suspiciously close to the 32/40 = 0.80 base rate of model-side outcomes in the same catalog; no baseline (e.g., "always predict the modal fault side") is reported, so it's impossible to tell how much of judge accuracy is genuine edge-discrimination versus majority-class matching.

**"The taxonomy generalizes across agent architectures."** Unsupported as stated. The 40 examples are explicitly "illustrative rather than exhaustive," and the authors caution against using them to estimate prevalence — so breadth of anecdote substitutes for systematic cross-architecture testing.

## Genuinely new vs. repackaged

Genuinely new: the explicit **edge + fault-side** pairing as the atomic unit of labeling, the 8-component vocabulary that promotes memory/tools/graders/users/environment to first-class fault-bearing components (a real conceptual move against Zhu et al. 2025a, which kept these as internal modules of the model), and the Figure-4 disambiguation rules that operationalize hard cases.

Repackaged/adopted: the backward causal-trace rule ("earliest unrecovered failure") is lifted wholesale from Barke et al. (2026)'s "critical failure" concept — the paper says so directly. The producer–consumer fault-boundary idea is credited to Shah et al. (2026), who "frequently identify causes" there already; this paper's claimed advance is only "making the two endpoints explicit," i.e., formalizing an already-observed pattern rather than discovering it. Against flat lists (Vinay 2025) the advance is mostly organizational (structure vs. no structure). Against Cemri et al. (2025), the paper's own framing is "complementary" — Cemri's withhold/ignore/lose-context mechanisms map onto this paper's fault-side splits on the same edge, meaning much of the taxonomy's granular content on multi-agent failures is a relabeling of Cemri's categories rather than new phenomena.

## Weaknesses and blind spots

Unaddressed by the authors: the construction/validation circularity above (they discuss the taxonomy needing to "expand" for future architectures, a forward-looking caveat, but never flag that the validation set overlaps the construction set). Also unaddressed: cost and latency. Each label requires four separate agentic judge runs with WebSearch/WebFetch/Bash/Read/Grep/Glob access and GPT-5.5-xhigh or Claude-Opus-max-thinking effort per turn, across a 3-turn protocol — no token, latency, or dollar cost is reported anywhere in the wiki pages, despite this being the central practical question for anyone wanting to deploy the validation pipeline at scale.

Acknowledged by the authors (wiki 06): the taxonomy is descriptive, not a base-rate estimator; labels require evidence-rich traces that terse incident reports often lack; and ensembling judges trades coverage for precision (unanimity: 96% category precision but only 68% coverage) — meaning the hardest, most valuable-to-automate cases are exactly the ones the ensemble abstains on.

On falsifiability: the attribution rule ("would a more capable model have avoided/recovered from this") is a counterfactual judgment call with no operational test — "more capable" names no specific model or capability axis. The Figure 4 rules are a list of precedent-based "this, not that" exceptions, not a mechanistic decision procedure, so the rule is a codified judgment call rather than a falsifiable test, and its gravitational pull toward the model side (36/41 modes) is consistent with a rule that can always imagine a hypothetically more capable model.

## Applicability

The paper is explicit that root-cause labeling requires trace-level evidence — tool-call arguments, intermediate reasoning, environment state — and that "brief reports or model system cards may omit details needed to identify a unique root cause" (wiki 06), which the authors illustrate concretely with E4 (200+ deleted emails, genuinely ambiguous between context- and model-side from the source text alone). This bounds the taxonomy to settings with full, logged agent trajectories, not postmortems or system-card summaries. Replicating the validation at the reported quality (κ ≈ 0.7–0.8) required 40 hand-curated, well-documented cases and four frontier-scale agentic judges; no evidence is given on how the approach degrades with fewer judges, a cheaper judge model, or a smaller example set. Transfer to genuinely novel harness designs (e.g., architectures without a clear context/memory/tool separation) is untested — the component vocabulary itself presupposes something close to today's LLM-agent harness shape.

**Relevance to my work** — (1) The edge + fault-side vocabulary is directly usable as a lightweight labeling rubric for triaging fleet's coder-worker failures (model vs. orchestrator/beads-sync vs. external tool/API), without needing the full 4-judge pipeline. (2) Given the modest marginal gain from ensembling (κ 0.70–0.76 across single judges vs. 0.84 peak pairwise), a single strong judge with the 3-turn protocol is a more realistic starting point than replicating all four judges — the cost/benefit of the full ensemble looks weak for a small team. (3) The Figure-4 disambiguation rules and the OWASP/ASI overlay are reusable prompt-engineering artifacts worth borrowing even if the underlying validation numbers are not fully trusted. (4) Treat the reported κ/accuracy figures as an upper bound, not a expectation — Elisity's own logs will lack the curated evidence density of the 40 worked examples, and the paper's own "evidence-dependent labeling" limitation predicts worse performance on messier real traces.

## What this changes

If the claims hold at face value: failure triage stops being "the agent failed, retrain/prompt-tune it" and becomes routing — post-training for model-side faults, scaffolding fixes for harness-side faults, tool/API/eval fixes for environment-side faults — which is a real efficiency gain for anyone currently blanket-retraining or blanket-reprompting after every failure. It would make flat "agent failed" bug reports obsolete as a triage artifact and enable a genuine harness-vs-model cost/benefit analysis for eng teams. Framework builders, eval teams, and ML engineers debugging production agents are the direct beneficiaries.

Given the validation weaknesses above, what survives even if the quantitative reproducibility claim is discounted: the component vocabulary, the edge/fault-side representational trick, and the disambiguation heuristics remain useful as a shared labeling language for human annotators and as prompt scaffolding for an internal judge — independent of whether a 4-judge ensemble hits κ = 0.76 on any particular corpus.

## Verdict

The taxonomy itself is a well-organized, honestly self-critical piece of conceptual engineering — the authors' admission that the model-side skew is a rule artifact, and their own Harbor-Mix case study exposing systematic judge bias, are exactly the kind of self-undermining detail a stronger paper would suppress. But the quantitative validation is thin for the weight the paper puts on it: n=40, likely-overlapping construction/validation data, correlated judges, and no baseline ablation against a naive majority-class classifier. Take the taxonomy and its vocabulary seriously as a labeling tool; take the κ/accuracy numbers as a demonstration of feasibility, not a proven reproducibility guarantee. **trial** — the component/edge/fault-side framework is worth adopting as a triage rubric now, but the 4-judge validation pipeline should be piloted on your own traces before trusting its reported precision/coverage numbers.
