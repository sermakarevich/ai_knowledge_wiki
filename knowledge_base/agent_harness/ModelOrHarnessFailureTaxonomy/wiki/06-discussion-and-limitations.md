# Discussion and Limitations

**In one sentence:** The taxonomy earns its keep by pointing each failure toward the correct fix (model, harness, or environment), the model-side skew it finds is an artifact of a deliberately conservative attribution rule rather than proof that models are the dominant bottleneck, and the agent-as-a-judge experiments show the labels are recoverable from evidence rather than being annotator idiosyncrasies — but the whole framework remains a qualitative map, not a frequency estimate, and its judge-based validation trades coverage for precision.

## Key points

- **Fault localization is practically load-bearing, not just descriptive bookkeeping.** The same surface-level failure (e.g., the agent gets stuck in a loop, or produces a wrong answer) can have a model-side root cause, a harness-side root cause, or an environment/evaluation-side root cause, and each of those three sides calls for a categorically different fix: post-training or model swaps for model-side faults, changes to scaffolding (prompts, tool schemas, memory management, control flow) for harness-side faults, and fixes outside the model entirely (fixing the tool, the sandbox, the grading rubric) for environment/evaluation-side faults. Without a taxonomy that separates these, teams risk mis-directed effort — e.g., fine-tuning a model to work around a broken tool API.
- **The taxonomy also reveals how responsibility is distributed across the system**, not just how individual failures should be labeled. Aggregating labels (as in Figure 2) turns a bag of per-case root-cause calls into a system-level picture of where the weight of failure currently sits.
- **Most failure modes are attributed to the model side, but this imbalance is partly an artifact of the attribution rule, not a direct measurement of model versus harness quality.** The rule used throughout the paper marks a failure model-side whenever a more capable model could plausibly have prevented or recovered from it — a counterfactual, model-centric default. Because most harness-level breakdowns (bad prompts, missing tools, race conditions, ambiguous instructions) are at least in principle recoverable by a sufficiently capable model noticing and compensating for them, the rule tends to pull ambiguous or jointly-caused failures toward the model side, inflating that category relative to a rule that credited harness robustness more symmetrically.
- **The non-model failures that remain after applying this conservative rule are informative precisely because they survived it** — they are cases where the authors judged that no plausible improvement in model capability alone would have fixed the problem, meaning the fix genuinely has to happen in the harness, the environment, or the evaluation setup. These are the failures where "just use a better model" is not a valid mitigation.
- **The agent-as-a-judge experiments (§6) were designed to test whether the fault-side and failure-mode distinctions are consistently applicable by someone other than the original human annotator, using only the same definitions and evidence.** The reported result — that independent judges often recover the human-assigned labels, and that judge-judge agreement is comparable to judge-human agreement — is offered as evidence that the categories capture shared, definition-driven structure in the failure evidence, rather than reflecting quirks or private heuristics of whoever happened to label the case.
- **Limitation 1 — the taxonomy is descriptive rather than quantitative.** It organizes failures into categories and assigns responsibility (fault side, failure mode) for each labeled case, but it does not, by itself, estimate how often each failure mode occurs in the wild, across models, or across deployment settings. It is a classification scheme, not a base-rate study, and it is derived from the specific corpus of cases the authors reviewed — so it may need to expand or be revised as agent architectures and harness designs evolve beyond what was observed.
- **Limitation 2 — the labels are only as good as the available evidence.** Assigning a unique root cause requires enough detail in the trace/report to disambiguate between competing explanations; brief incident reports or terse model system cards can omit exactly the details (tool call arguments, intermediate reasoning, environment state) needed to pin down a single root cause, which caps the taxonomy's applicability on sparse evidence.
- **Limitation 3 — the judge framework that validates the taxonomy has its own accuracy ceiling, and the standard fix (ensembling) is a trade, not a free win.** Judge accuracy is described as limited, particularly for the finer-grained failure-mode labels (as opposed to the coarser fault-side call). Ensembling multiple judges was tried as a mitigation and does raise precision, but the paper reports this comes at the cost of lower coverage — meaning the system ends up abstaining on the cases where fault attribution is hardest, which are arguably the cases where automated judging would be most valuable if it worked.

## Discussion

**Why fault localization matters practically.** The paper's central practical claim in §7 is that knowing *where* a failure originates changes what you do about it. An agent that fails is, from the outside, just a failure — a wrong final answer, a stalled task, a broken tool call. But the same externally-visible failure can arise from at least three structurally different causes: the underlying model reasoned or planned incorrectly (a model-side fault); the harness — the scaffolding, prompts, memory management, or tool-calling logic wrapped around the model — set the model up to fail even though the model's own reasoning was locally reasonable (a harness-side fault); or something outside the model-harness pair broke, such as a flaky tool, an unstable environment, or a flawed grading/evaluation setup. Each of these calls for a different remedy: model-side faults are candidates for post-training (more data, RLHF, distillation from better models, etc.); harness-side faults call for engineering changes to the scaffolding itself (better prompts, added tools, improved control flow, memory fixes); and environment/evaluation faults require intervention entirely outside the model, such as fixing a tool's API, stabilizing a sandbox, or correcting a grading rubric. A taxonomy that can consistently sort failures into these buckets is therefore not just a labeling convenience — it is what lets an engineering or research team route each observed failure to the team or workstream that can actually fix it, instead of defaulting to "train a bigger/better model" for problems a better model cannot solve.

**What the aggregate picture (mostly model-side) means, and why it should not be over-read.** §7 reports that, in the corpus reviewed, most failure modes end up assigned to the model side (as visualized in the paper's Figure 2, not reproduced on this page). The paper is explicit that this skew is *partly* an artifact of the attribution rule rather than a clean empirical finding that models are responsible for most agent failures in some rule-independent sense. The attribution rule says: label a failure model-side if a more capable model could have prevented it or recovered from it. This is a deliberately permissive, counterfactual test for the model-side bucket — many harness-level shortcomings (an ambiguous prompt, a missing tool description, a race condition in scheduling) are, in principle, things a sufficiently capable model could notice, work around, or recover from (e.g., by asking a clarifying question, retrying sensibly, or inferring the tool's correct usage despite an incomplete description). Because the rule credits the model side whenever such a counterfactual recovery is plausible, it will tend to classify many failures that have a proximate harness-level trigger as model-side anyway, since the model "should have" handled it. This is why the observed imbalance is described as *reflecting the attribution rule* rather than being read as a direct measurement of "models cause most agent failures." A different, less model-centric attribution rule (e.g., one that credited the harness for being fragile even in the face of a capable model) would likely produce a less lopsided distribution from the exact same underlying corpus of failures.

**Why the residual non-model failures matter more than their small share suggests.** Precisely because the attribution rule is tilted toward the model side, the failures that still end up classified as harness-side, environment-side, or evaluation-side have survived a fairly strict filter: they are cases where the authors judged that no plausible improvement in model capability would have prevented or fixed the failure. These are, in effect, the "no amount of a better model saves you here" failures — cases where the fix has to be structural (better scaffolding, more robust tools, more stable environments, better-specified evaluation criteria). The practical value of isolating this residual category is that it tells practitioners which fraction of their observed failures will *not* improve simply by upgrading to a stronger frontier model, and therefore where investment in harness engineering or environment reliability is non-optional.

**What the agent-as-a-judge results actually demonstrate.** §6's agent-as-a-judge experiments are brought back into the discussion as the paper's answer to an implicit objection: "isn't this taxonomy just one team's subjective judgment calls?" The test constructed is a consistency test — take the taxonomy's category definitions and the same evidence used by the human annotator, hand them to independent LLM judges who did not produce the original label, and see whether those judges converge on the same label. The reported finding is twofold: (1) independent judges often recover the human-assigned label from the shared definitions and evidence, and (2) the level of agreement *between different judges* is comparable to the level of agreement *between a judge and the human annotator*. The second point is the more important one epistemically: if judge-human agreement were much higher than judge-judge agreement, that could indicate the judges were each independently converging on something idiosyncratic to the human labeler (e.g., picking up on annotation habits) rather than on the underlying evidence. Instead, judges agreeing with each other about as much as they agree with the human suggests all parties — human and machine alike — are responding to genuine structure in the definitions and evidence, not to labeler-specific quirks. This is offered as evidence (not proof) that the taxonomy's categories are intersubjectively applicable rather than being an artifact of who happened to annotate a given case.

## Limitations

**Descriptive, not quantitative.** The taxonomy's job is to organize failures into a structured set of categories and assign responsibility (which side, which specific failure mode) for each case that gets reviewed. It is explicitly *not* designed to answer "what fraction of real-world agent failures are model-side versus harness-side?" in any statistically representative sense. Any counts or proportions that fall out of applying the taxonomy to a particular corpus (such as the corpus behind Figure 2) describe that corpus, not the general population of agent deployments — the taxonomy has no built-in sampling guarantee that the reviewed cases are representative of failures at large. This matters for how readers should use the paper: it is a classification instrument and a conceptual map of the failure space, not a benchmark result that says "X% of agent failures today are the model's fault." Practically, this also means the taxonomy is a living artifact — since it was built from the specific set of cases the authors happened to review, novel agent architectures, new harness designs, or new deployment patterns may surface failure shapes that don't fit the existing categories cleanly, and the authors anticipate the taxonomy will need to expand to keep pace with how agents and harnesses evolve.

**Evidence-dependent labeling.** Correctly assigning a failure to a fault side and a specific failure mode requires enough detail in the record of what happened — full traces, intermediate reasoning, tool call arguments and results, environment state — to distinguish between multiple plausible explanations for the same observed breakdown. Real-world incident reports and model system cards are frequently much terser than the full traces the taxonomy was designed against; they may report only the final outcome or a brief summary rather than the step-by-step evidence needed to determine whether, say, a failure was caused by the model misreading a tool's output versus the tool itself returning malformed data. When the necessary evidence is missing, the taxonomy cannot be applied with confidence to assign a *unique* root cause — multiple category assignments might be equally consistent with the sparse evidence available. This is a real ceiling on the taxonomy's applicability to secondhand or lightly-documented failure reports, as opposed to cases where the analyst has direct access to full agent traces.

**Judge accuracy and the precision-coverage tradeoff.** The agent-as-a-judge framework that the paper uses to validate the taxonomy (§6, discussed above) is itself imperfect and, per the authors, may be difficult to deploy as a production-grade automatic labeling system. Judge accuracy is reported as limited overall, and notably worse for the fine-grained failure-mode labels than for the coarser fault-side (model vs. harness vs. environment) distinction — finer distinctions are harder for a judge model to get right consistently. The authors' attempted mitigation is ensembling: combining the outputs of multiple judges to get a more reliable consensus label. This does raise precision (when the ensemble commits to a label, that label is more likely to be correct), but the reported cost is lower coverage — the ensembling procedure is more likely to produce no confident, agreed-upon label at all, effectively causing the system to abstain rather than output a possibly-wrong one. The practical implication is a real tradeoff rather than a solved problem: a production system built on this validation approach will either (a) accept lower-confidence, higher-volume automatic labeling, or (b) accept an ensembled, higher-confidence but lower-volume labeling process that abstains most often on exactly the ambiguous, evidence-thin, or genuinely borderline cases where fault attribution is hardest and automated help would be most valuable. In other words, the cases the field most needs automated fault attribution to resolve are disproportionately the cases where the current validation framework is least willing to commit to an answer.
