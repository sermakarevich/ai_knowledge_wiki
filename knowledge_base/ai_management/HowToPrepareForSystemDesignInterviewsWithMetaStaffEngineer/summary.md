# How to Prepare for System Design Interviews w/ Meta Staff Engineer

**Video:** [How to Prepare for System Design Interviews w/ Meta Staff Engineer](https://youtu.be/Ru54dxzCyD0) — Hello Interview

## Human Readable TL;DR

Preparing for a system design interview can feel like being asked to plan a whole city without ever having built one, but this video breaks it into small, repeatable steps. Think of the interview itself like sketching a map with boxes and arrows instead of writing code, where you first agree on what to build and then draw a simple version before adding details. Learning the basic building blocks is like learning a few kitchen ingredients that show up in almost every recipe, so you can mix them in new ways instead of memorizing every dish. The main advice is to practice just ten common problems in a set order, like learning songs that share the same chords, and to try each one yourself with a timer instead of only watching someone else play.

## TL;DR

Former Meta (a large social media and technology company) Staff engineer Evan presents a four-step preparation strategy for system design interviews: first understand what the interview really is, then refresh six core fundamentals, then learn the basic components found in almost every system, and finally work backwards through ten common problems in a fixed order to build pattern recognition. The interview itself is a whiteboard exercise with boxes and arrows and no code, usually lasting 35 minutes to an hour of talking, and it follows a fixed delivery framework from requirements through core entities and APIs (Application Programming Interfaces, the plugs that let software parts talk to each other) to a simple high-level design and then deep dives. Evaluation focuses on problem solving, solution design, technical excellence, and communication, while most of the learning is meant to come from active timed practice rather than passive watching.

## Problem & Motivation

System design preparation feels incredibly overwhelming because online lists of topics seem endless and candidates often do not know what interviewers actually expect. The video clarifies that the most common interviews ask the candidate to architect (design the structure of) a complex, scalable system that solves a real-world problem, such as Ticketmaster or Uber, using boxes and arrows on a whiteboard with no code. It covers two heavily overlapping buckets, namely product problems where you design an everyday app or website such as Dropbox, Uber, or Netflix, and infra (infrastructure, the behind-the-scenes systems) problems such as a rate limiter, a message queue, data processing pipelines, or an ad-click aggregator. The motivation is to replace random memorization with a clear format, a small set of fundamentals and components, and pattern-based practice similar to how LeetCode (a popular coding practice platform) is used for coding interviews.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Four-step prep roadmap in fixed order:** understand what a system design interview actually is, then refresh college-level fundamentals, then get familiar with the basic components present in almost every design, and only then work backwards from common problems to build deeper understanding, with the promise that the exact problem order comes later in the video.

2. **Fixed five-step delivery framework:** start with requirements covering both functional requirements (what features users get, such as booking a ticket) and non-functional requirements (quality goals such as low delay and large scale), then define core entities that usually map to database tables such as user, event, and ticket, then outline basic APIs that are REST (Representational State Transfer, a common web style) endpoints nine times out of ten, then draw a simple high-level design that meets the functional requirements, and finally do deep dives that satisfy the non-functional requirements.

3. **Four-part evaluation lens:** problem solving means recognizing and prioritizing the core challenges such as the booking flow for Ticketmaster rather than side topics like authentication, solution design means weighing trade-offs and reaching a high-level design that meets functional requirements, technical excellence means going deep in some but not all areas with specific technologies and failure points, and communication means clearly explaining complex ideas while talking for most of the session.

4. **Six fundamentals that cover more than ninety percent:** storage basics including relational tables, document stores with nested JSON-like (JavaScript Object Notation, a text data format) structures, key-value (simple key lookup) stores, plus ACID (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability, strict guarantees for transactions) versus BASE (Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent, looser guarantees for distributed databases); scalability through vertical scaling with beefier machines and horizontal scaling with more machines, plus storage partitioning, sharding, and consistent hashing (a method for spreading data evenly); networking limited to three OSI (Open Systems Interconnection, a layered network model) layers, namely the application layer with REST versus GraphQL (a query language for APIs) versus gRPC (a fast remote-call system) plus real-time options like websockets or server-sent events, the transport layer with TCP (Transmission Control Protocol, reliable delivery) versus UDP (User Datagram Protocol, faster but less reliable), and the network layer with basic load balancing and access control; latency (delay), throughput (work done per time), and performance including rough latency numbers, capacity planning, and fixes like adding a cache; fault tolerance and redundancy (backup copies) through replication and failure detection across servers, racks, and data centers; and the CAP (Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance) theorem, where partition tolerance is treated as guaranteed so the real choice is between availability and consistency.

5. **Six everyday components with selection rules:** a database where data lives when the power goes off, such as Postgres for strict tables, MongoDB for document blobs, or Redis for fast key lookups, chosen by how the app reads and writes rather than by the old SQL (Structured Query Language) versus NoSQL (non-relational) debate; a cache as short-term memory, such as Redis, that makes repeated reads lightning fast but adds freshness and eviction complexity; message queues as texting instead of calling, such as Kafka, RabbitMQ, or SQS (Simple Queue Service), that smooth traffic spikes and survive crashes but need delivery-semantics handling because messages can arrive once or multiple times; a load balancer as traffic control that spreads requests across horizontally scaled servers; blob storage such as S3 (Simple Storage Service) for unstructured large files and media, because forcing them into a database bloats it and hurts queries and backups; and a CDN (Content Delivery Network, a worldwide cache) such as Cloudflare that copies images, videos, and static files closer to users so a user in India pulls from a nearby server instead of America.

6. **Ten problems in pattern order with an active practice cycle:** work just ten problems that cover the overwhelming majority of concepts, starting with Bitly, then Dropbox and Ticketmaster, and ending with Post Search, where each problem introduces techniques that reappear later, and switch from passive watching for the first roughly three to timed solo attempts on an online whiteboard for the rest, using Google or ChatGPT (a conversational AI assistant) for known unknowns and then watching the breakdown to catch unknown unknowns before repeating through all ten.

## Key Findings

The video argues that candidates who already recognize the fundamentals and components are ready to start solving problems, because most deeper learning comes from working through core problems rather than collecting more theory. Storage choice should follow access patterns and consistency needs from the requirements, scaling combines stronger machines with more machines and smarter data distribution, and networking preparation can stay narrowly focused on the few layers and trade-offs that actually appear in interviews. Common components repeat so often that understanding their roles and challenges matters more than brand names, whether it is cache freshness, queue delivery guarantees, the structured-versus-unstructured split between databases and blob storage, or the CDN as simply a global cache. The shared pattern across the ten problems is the central finding: techniques learned early keep reappearing, so ordered practice builds reusable judgment about trade-offs, bottlenecks, and failure handling.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The suggested next step after the basics is to close remaining gaps with linked videos and written resources and then spend most time on the ten ordered problems using the timer-plus-whiteboard cycle of attempt, search, compare with the breakdown, and repeat. For the first few problems passive video use is acceptable, but after that the emphasis is strongly on trying first and watching only afterwards to reveal what was missed. The speaker points to detailed Hello Interview breakdowns and recorded videos for each of the ten plus more than fifteen others, including guided practice that walks through the delivery framework with inline feedback plus text, with Ticketmaster free to everybody. Because system design is also a presentation, the closing advice is to practice out loud with friends, colleagues, or peer mocks, or with professional mocks featuring interviewers from the target company such as an active interviewer at Meta.
