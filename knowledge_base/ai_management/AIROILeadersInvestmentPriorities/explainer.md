# How AI ROI Leaders Prioritize Investments for Real Business Outcomes -- In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Imagine two neighbors who both bought treadmills last January. One treadmill sits in the corner under a pile of laundry. The other neighbor pays a trainer, blocks 6am on the calendar every day, and has a friend who checks in when they skip a session. Same machine, wildly different results a year later. This is a report about companies and AI, and it turns out companies split the same way.

Google Cloud asked 2,403 executives around the world -- department heads, VPs, C-suite -- how AI investments are paying off for their companies. Most of them, 84%, say the returns keep growing. But when you ask a sharper question -- is the payoff getting *faster* each year, or just steadily ticking up -- only about a quarter of them, 26%, can say yes, it's accelerating. Google Cloud calls this quarter "AI ROI Leaders," and the report spends most of its pages asking what these leaders are actually doing differently.

The answer is not a better AI model or a bigger checkbook. It's closer to the trainer-and-schedule story: leaders put one named person in charge of AI decisions instead of leaving it to committee, they weave AI into the actual daily work instead of running it as a side experiment nobody depends on, and they treat learning to use AI as part of the job description rather than an optional lunchtime webinar. Everyone has roughly the same tools available; the gap is in how deliberately those tools get managed.

## Why does it matter?

Most organizations buying AI tools are stuck at "we bought the treadmill." They see some benefit -- a report gets written faster, a question gets answered without waiting for a human -- but the benefit plateaus. It doesn't compound. Meanwhile the bills for AI keep arriving, and it becomes harder to tell leadership whether the spending is actually working or just feels like it should be.

The problem in everyday terms: buying a tool is easy, but making an organization actually change how it works around that tool is hard, unglamorous, and requires someone to own it. Nobody gets promoted for scheduling training sessions or writing down who's allowed to approve a new AI use case. So many companies skip that part and wonder why nothing sped up.

What changes if this works: the payoff doesn't just show up once and flatten out -- it keeps growing year over year, because the organization gets structurally better at finding and capturing new value, not just luckier on one project. That's the difference between a one-time discount and compound interest. It also changes who benefits: instead of a narrow productivity bump for a few power users, the report's leaders say the biggest gains show up in faster decisions and more capacity for the whole workforce, not just cost-cutting.

## How does it work?

Based on what the report says separates AI ROI Leaders from everyone else, the mechanism runs like a repeatable chain, not a one-time event. Think of it the way you'd set up a new habit you actually intend to keep, rather than a New Year's resolution you'll drop by February:

1. **Pick where AI goes first.** Don't spray it everywhere at once. Leaders look for a specific, real bottleneck -- a slow approval process, a mountain of documents nobody has time to read -- and start there, the way you'd start a new exercise habit with one specific goal (walk 20 minutes) rather than a vague one (get fit).
2. **Give one person clear authority to decide.** Almost half of AI ROI Leaders (48%) say ownership and decision rights for AI projects are "extremely clear" -- one name, one desk, one person who says yes or no. Compare that to a household chore with no assigned owner: it doesn't get done, because everyone assumes someone else has it.
3. **Wire it into the real workflow, not a side pilot.** Nearly half of AI ROI Leaders have built AI directly into core business processes -- the way work already happens -- rather than running it as a demo project that lives in a slide deck. This is the difference between a gym membership you use on your way home from work anyway, versus a special trip you have to plan for and therefore skip.
4. **Train everyone, as part of the job, not as an option.** 38% of AI ROI Leaders have made ongoing AI skill-building a required part of people's roles, not a one-off workshop people forget. It's the difference between a single fitness class and a standing weekly appointment that's just part of your week now.
5. **Measure what actually came back.** The report finds that once AI is embedded this way, agents (see jargon decoder) are the single biggest driver behind the financial gains organizations report -- and the outcomes people can actually point to are things like faster decisions and more capacity per employee, not just a lower bill.
6. **Reinvest in what worked.** 97% of all surveyed executives -- almost everyone -- plan to spend more on AI next year. For AI ROI Leaders, that reinvestment is targeted at whatever step 5 showed was actually working, restarting the loop at step 1 with a sharper aim.

The two real-world anchors that make this concrete: Tata Steel didn't buy one big AI system, it stood up more than 300 separate specialized AI agents across its operations in nine months -- that's step 1 through 3 repeated at scale, fast, with clear ownership at each one. Highmark Health, a healthcare system, reports its AI assistant delivered $27.9 million of self-reported value in 2025 by automating research work and answering internal staff questions -- an example of step 5 and 6 in action, at least according to the company itself.

## Where can this be used?

The report is about corporate AI budgets, but the underlying pattern -- name an owner, build it into the routine, make skill-building mandatory, measure it, reinvest -- has nothing specifically to do with AI or with big companies. It shows up anywhere a group of people adopts a new tool or habit and some groups get much more out of it than others:

- **A hospital department adopting a new record-keeping system.** The department that names one nurse manager as the go-to decision-maker, builds the new system into the actual shift handoff routine, and requires every new hire to be trained on it will get more mileage than the department that lets each doctor use it or not as they please.
- **A school district rolling out new classroom software.** Same lesson: one person accountable for the rollout, the tool built into lesson planning rather than "extra credit if you try it," and training built into onboarding rather than an optional after-school session.
- **A small business adopting any new piece of software** -- a scheduling tool, a bookkeeping app, a customer-message inbox. The owner who assigns one employee to "own" the new tool, makes it the default way of doing that task (not a backup), and walks every new hire through it will see it stick. The owner who just turns it on for whoever wants it will see it quietly abandoned within a month.
- **A personal habit or workflow** -- learning a language, using a budgeting app, exercising. The treadmill-versus-trainer story applies directly: naming a specific goal, putting it on a fixed calendar slot, building it into a routine you already have (like a commute or a lunch break), and checking in on whether it's actually working are the same five moves, just applied to one person instead of one company.

## Conclusions & takeaways

A month from now, the one sentence worth remembering: the gap between companies that get more out of AI every year and companies that plateau is management discipline -- clear ownership, real integration into daily work, and mandatory training -- not a fancier model or a bigger check. The same recipe works for a hospital ward, a classroom, a small shop, or your own morning routine.

Honest limitations, stated plainly:

- **This is a survey of opinions, not an audit.** The 84%, 26%, and dollar figures all come from executives describing their own projects to a survey company. Nobody independently checked the books. "We report $27.9 million in value" is a company's own claim, not a verified financial statement.
- **The report is published by the company selling the AI.** Google Cloud runs this survey and also sells the AI platforms the case-study companies used. That doesn't make the findings false, but it means the people telling you AI works are the same people who profit if you believe them.
- **Doing what successful companies do is not the same as knowing it caused their success.** The report shows that AI ROI Leaders tend to have clear ownership, embedded workflows, and mandatory training. It does not prove that adopting those three things will make any given company a leader too -- maybe leaders are just better-run companies in general, and would have outperformed with or without AI.
- **Planning to spend more is not evidence that the spending is working.** 97% of executives say they'll increase AI spending next year -- but intending to spend more tells you what people believe, not what actually happened to their bottom line. A person can keep paying for a gym membership they never use.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | Plain meaning |
|------|---------------|
| ROI (return on investment) | How much benefit you got back compared to what you spent -- did the money you put in pay you back, and by how much. |
| Agentic AI / AI agent | An AI system that doesn't just answer a question once but takes a series of actions on its own toward a goal, like a digital assistant that can actually do the task, not just describe how. |
| Workflow | The actual sequence of steps people follow to get a piece of work done -- e.g., how a purchase gets approved from request to payment. |
| Pilot | A small trial run of a new tool with a limited group, before (or instead of) rolling it out everywhere. |
| Core business process | The central, everyday activities that keep a company running and earning money, as opposed to a side project or experiment. |
| Cost-efficient growth | Making more money or doing more work without your costs rising at the same pace -- growing without the expenses growing just as fast. |
| Margin | The slice of revenue left over as profit after costs are subtracted; a "margin improvement" means more of each dollar earned is kept as profit. |
| Self-reported data | Information that comes from the people or companies being studied describing themselves, with no outside party checking it. |
| Correlation vs. causation | Two things happening together (successful companies also do X) does not prove that X is what caused the success -- something else might explain both. |
| Token efficiency | Getting more useful output per unit of computing cost from an AI model -- roughly, using the AI's "fuel" wisely instead of burning through it. |
| Pharmacovigilance | The ongoing work of monitoring medicines after they're on the market to catch side effects or safety problems. |
| Customer lifetime value | An estimate of the total money a business expects to earn from one customer over the entire time that customer keeps buying from them. |
