# Retrieval Practice: How AI ROI Leaders Prioritize Investments for Real Business Outcomes

Answer from memory before opening any answer. Run sessions with `kb show summary/quiz`.

### Q1. In the survey's segmentation of all 2,403 executives, what specifically makes an organization an "AI ROI Leader" rather than part of the 58% "steadily increasing" group?

> [!tip]- Answer
> An AI ROI Leader is one of the 26% of the total 2,403-executive sample whose AI returns are accelerating year over year, not merely rising -- a distinct, mutually exclusive category from the 58% whose returns are just "steadily increasing." Together the 58% and 26% slices sum to the survey's 84% headline "increasing returns" figure. See [[wiki/01-the-ai-roi-leader-segment|The AI ROI Leader Segment]].

### Q2. The article's prose says "84% report increasing returns... among this cohort, 26% report returns that are accelerating," which reads as 26% of the 84%. Why does Figure 1's own donut chart support a different reading of that 26%?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Figure 1 splits the full 2,403-respondent sample into four mutually exclusive slices -- 58% steadily increasing, 26% accelerating (AI ROI Leaders), 16% inconsistent/plateauing, under 1% declining -- and 58% + 26% exactly equals the 84% headline. That arithmetic only works if the 26% is a share of the total sample sitting alongside the 58%, not a sub-slice drawn from within the 84% cohort; read as a share of that cohort it would be roughly 31%, not 26%, and "steadily increasing" would need to be about 62% rather than the 58% actually shown. See [[wiki/01-the-ai-roi-leader-segment|The AI ROI Leader Segment]].

### Q3. What three practices does the survey report as common among AI ROI Leaders, and what proportion of that subgroup exhibits each, where a figure is given?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Clear ownership -- 48% institute "extremely clear" decision-making authority for AI and agentic initiatives; embedding AI into core workflows and revenue streams -- reported only qualitatively as "almost half," with no exact percentage published; and mandated, ongoing AI-fluency training -- 38%. All three figures describe the 26% AI-ROI-Leader subgroup only, not the full 2,403-executive sample. See [[wiki/02-three-practices-of-roi-leaders|The Three Practices of AI ROI Leaders]].

### Q4. These three practices are reported only inside the 26% AI-ROI-Leader subgroup, with no equivalent figures published for the 58% or 16% groups. Why does that gap keep the finding from proving the practices cause faster returns?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Without figures for how common ownership clarity, workflow embedding, and fluency training are among the non-leader groups, there is no baseline showing these practices are actually rarer outside the leader segment, and the article itself reports no causal test isolating any one practice's effect. It is a correlation observed only inside a self-selected high-performing subgroup, which fits reverse causation just as well as forward causation -- organizations already capturing returns can more easily afford clear governance structures and mandatory training programs. See [[wiki/02-three-practices-of-roi-leaders|The Three Practices of AI ROI Leaders]].

### Q5. The published chart caption states that 94% of respondents report AI agents contributing to revenue and cost savings. What does that 94% actually aggregate on the underlying five-point scale, and why does that make it a weaker finding than the headline suggests?

> [!tip]- Answer
> The scale runs no / minor / moderate / significant / transformational contribution, and 94% is simply the complement of "no contribution" -- it counts anyone who picked even "minor" impact, making it close to a floor rather than a strong result. The real high-conviction signal, significant-or-transformational contribution, is only about 58% for revenue growth and about 64% for cost savings. See [[wiki/03-agents-and-where-roi-shows-up|Agents and Where ROI Shows Up]].

### Q6. Highmark Health's $27.9 million and Elanco's $1.9 million are the only two dollar figures among the nine enterprise case studies. What critical piece of information is absent from both that keeps them from being read as net ROI?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Neither figure comes with a disclosed cost base, investment amount, baseline, or calculation methodology -- Highmark's is labeled "value" and Elanco's "estimated ROI," but both are self-reported customer claims whose underlying math is undisclosed, so neither can be verified as a net financial return. See [[wiki/04-enterprise-case-studies|Enterprise Case Studies]].

### Q7. The article treats 97% of executives planning to increase AI spend as "the biggest signal" that AI is delivering ROI. Treating that as a cautionary example, how would you actually measure whether one specific agentic workflow is paying off, in a way that isn't vulnerable to the same weakness?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Track real inputs and outputs for that one workflow specifically -- actual operating cost such as compute/token spend and staff time, before and after deployment, against a pre-defined outcome metric with a stated baseline -- rather than relying on an aggregate spending trend or a self-reported perception survey. Budget intent alone says nothing about return because it is equally consistent with genuine gains or with sunk-cost and competitive-pressure spending; only a workflow-level cost/benefit comparison with a disclosed baseline closes that gap. See [[wiki/05-future-investment-and-organizational-change|Future Investment and Organizational Change]].

### Q8. The critical analysis argues the three-practices finding (ownership, embedding, fluency) is not a genuinely new discovery about AI. What older pattern is it actually restating, and why does that mean the finding doesn't depend on this survey's specific percentages being accurate?

> [!tip]- Answer
> It restates the long-standing IT-productivity-paradox and change-management finding that returns track organizational complements -- clear ownership, real process integration, workforce capability -- rather than the technology itself, a pattern established independently across decades of prior computer and enterprise-software research. Because that organizational-complements thesis is corroborated by that separate body of evidence, it would still hold even if the survey's 48%/38%/26% figures turned out to be pure noise. See [[critical_thinking|Critical Analysis]].
