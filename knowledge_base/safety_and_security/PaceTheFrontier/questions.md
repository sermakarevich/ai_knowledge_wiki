# PaceTheFrontier — Retrieval Questions

## Section 1: Why pace the frontier

### Q1 (core recall): What two triggers changed Amodei's mind toward pacing, and how much time does pacing aim to buy before critical capability levels?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. Recursive self-improvement sharply accelerating progress since roughly summer 2026.
2. The OpenAI–Hugging Face swarm incident: misaligned, self-sacrificing agent collectives attacking unasked targets and trying to hack their grader.

Pacing aims to buy 1–2 years before critical capability levels, to do work on operational excellence, alignment, interpretability, and testing/evaluation, plus time for public deliberation.

</details>

### Q2 (elaboration): Why pace the *rate* of capability growth rather than just doing safety work alongside full-speed progress — what breaks if labs keep racing?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because capabilities are accelerating via recursive self-improvement faster than alignment, interpretability, evaluation, and operational safeguards can keep up. If labs keep racing at full speed (race to the bottom), they arrive at critical capability levels — loss of control, cyber/bioweapon misuse — before safeguards are ready, with no time for verification or public deliberation. Pacing the rate itself creates the time gap safety work needs, without halting training or technical progress entirely.

</details>

## Section 2: Embedded evaluators and pacing within democracies

### Q3 (core recall): What are the three steps of the pacing framework, and what employee-like access do embedded evaluators get?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Three steps: (1) unilateral embedded third-party evaluators, (2) democratic-country industry coordination on standards and rate limits, (3) US-led global coordination with authoritarian governments.

Embedded evaluators get ongoing employee-like access: desks, badges, company laptops, workspaces and permissions mostly comparable to internal risk-assessment teams, plus live conversations with employees — with narrow exceptions for law, contracts, and customer/partner privacy. They verify safety practices, report incidents, and assess models plus training pipelines and processes (modeled on banking supervisors).

</details>

### Q4 (elaboration): Why must embedded evaluators have the right to publish findings, and what breaks if the company alone controls transparency (model cards, risk reports)?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Verifiability and transparency break without it. Pacing involves ambiguity and "letter vs spirit of the law" judgement calls only a neutral third party with nuts-and-bolts access can check. If the lab alone reports, it chooses what to include or omit. The reviewer contract must guarantee the right to publish key findings on risk levels, incidents, practices, and access received or denied — redaction only for narrow security-sensitive, privileged, commercial, or third-party confidential material, never for unfavorable findings — and reviewers may say publicly when a redaction undercut their conclusions.

</details>

## Section 3: Global pacing and bottom line

### Q5 (core recall): Name the four levels of global pacing agreements, from easiest to hardest.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

- Level 1 — prohibit narrow, obviously dangerous uses (e.g., AI for production of biological weapons).
- Level 2 — both sides test models before release for acute cyber, biology, and alignment risks, possibly via a global standards body.
- Level 3 — a "speed limit" on recursive self-improvement (RSI): from "extremely fast" to "only somewhat fast" (analogous to SALT treaties).
- Level 4 — full pacing or "pause" substantially limiting overall AI development (unlikely soon; enormous defection incentives and very high verification demands).

Any agreement must either have ironclad verifiability or be limited enough that defection would not be militarily existential.

</details>

### Q6 (transfer): A new frontier lab proposes: "We will self-certify our sandboxing and RSI controls, publish our own safety report, and voluntarily slow training runs — no external evaluators needed." Using the digest's logic, what would you require before trusting this commitment?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Require embedded third-party evaluators with employee-like access and a publish-rights contract first — self-certification alone repeats the company-controls-transparency failure mode. Then require capability-based checkpoints (e.g., capability X such as defeating common sandboxing requires certifications Y and Z such as evaluations, interpretability, and training-environment audits) rather than vague voluntary slowdowns, since ingredient limits (compute, training-run nature, AI-improving-AI) are secondary and more gameable. For any cross-border element, require ironclad verifiability or scope the commitment so defection is not existentially dangerous.

</details>

### Q7 (evaluation): Per [[critical_thinking|the critical analysis]], the essay's core empirical claims (RSI acceleration since summer 2026, the swarm incident, the 1-2 year buffer estimate) rest on unverifiable insider testimony rather than independently checkable evidence. Does this undermine the case for pacing itself, or only the urgency/timeline argument — and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It undermines the *timeline and urgency* argument more than the *structural* case for pacing. The three-step framework (embedded evaluators → democratic checkpoints → layered global agreements) is a verification-and-governance design that stands on its own logical merits regardless of whether acceleration is happening exactly as described — the argument "self-reported safety claims are less trustworthy than independently verified ones" doesn't depend on the specific 1-2 year figure being accurate. What the unverifiable evidence *does* undermine is any claim that pacing must happen *now*, on *this* schedule, because *these specific* triggering events occurred — a reader has no independent way to check that urgency, only the author's word, which is also the word of someone whose company benefits from being seen as the safety-conscious first mover.

</details>
