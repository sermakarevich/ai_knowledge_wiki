# Embedded evaluators and pacing within democracies

**In one sentence:** The essay proposes starting with embedded external evaluators who have employee-like access to verify safety practices, then using that verifiability to pace frontier AI within democracies through regulation, voluntary standards, and capability-based checkpoints while defending the democracies' lead over authoritarian regimes.

## Key points

- Embedded evaluators with employee-like access are the unilateral first step, making all later pacing commitments verifiable, transparent, and informed by an independent second opinion.
- Verifiability matters because pacing involves ambiguity and "letter vs spirit of the law" judgement calls that only a neutral third party with nuts-and-bolts access can check.
- Transparency changes because evaluators — not the company alone — surface what is happening, ending the dynamic where the lab chooses what to include or omit in model cards and risk reports.
- Anthropic's concrete offer is desks, badges, company laptops, workspaces and permissions mostly comparable to internal risk-assessment teams, plus live conversations with employees, with narrow exceptions for law, contracts, and customer/partner privacy.
- The reviewer contract must guarantee the right to publish key findings on risk levels, incidents, practices, and access received or denied, with only narrow redaction for security-sensitive, privileged, commercial, or third-party confidential material — never for unfavorable findings — and reviewers may say publicly when a redaction undercut their conclusions.
- Once embedded evaluators operate across a critical mass of US labs, pacing becomes viable: ideally through regulation covering all frontier companies (transparency plus third-party auditing, permanent embedded evaluators), and in parallel through voluntary industry standards mediated or antitrust-waived by government.
- The preferred pacing logic is capability-based checkpoints — e.g., capability X such as defeating common sandboxing requires certifications Y and Z such as evaluations, interpretability, and training-environment audits — with ingredient limits (compute, training-run nature, AI-improving-AI) as a secondary, more gameable option.
- Pacing within democracies is bounded by the US lead over the CCP: democracies must widen that lead via chip export controls plus smuggling crackdowns, anti-distillation enforcement, and weight-theft security, creating breathing room so pacing does not hand authoritarian projects a destabilizing lead.

## Embedded evaluators

The first step in the three-stage plan — and the one Anthropic commits to unilaterally — is embedded evaluators with employee-like access to verify safety practices and report incidents.

Embedding evaluators may sound boring or procedural, but the essay calls it radical, going far beyond current industry practice. Three benefits are named:

### Verifiability

Embedded evaluators check at the level of nuts and bolts whether the company actually follows the training, deployment, operational, and safeguards practices it claims. Pacing commitments will involve ambiguity, judgement calls, and "letter of the law vs spirit of the law" disputes, so a neutral third party that can see the details is vital.

### Transparency

The public deserves to know what is going on. Anthropic notes its support for transparency legislation and its hundreds of pages of model cards and risk reports — but the company still chooses what to include and omit. Embedded evaluators change that dynamic.

### Second opinion

Beyond verifying commitments and informing the public, evaluators provide a second opinion free of commercial incentives. Safety gains can come simply from evaluators spotting something employees had not considered but are happy to fix once aware.

Because of these benefits, any pacing proposal works better starting from embedded evaluators.

### What Anthropic intends to offer

Anthropic plans to invite an embedded external review team with ongoing access, permissions, and tools similar to internal employees doing comparable risk assessments:

- Desks in Anthropic offices, access badges, and company laptops.
- Access to workspaces, tools, and permissions mostly comparable to internal risk-assessment teams, with exceptions only where law or contracts require it or to protect customers' and partners' private information, plus strong internal norms reinforcing access to relevant information including live conversations with employees.
- A contract balancing these complexities: reviewers have the right to publish key findings about risk levels, incidents, practices, and the access they received or did not receive — without editorial control by Anthropic.
- Narrow redaction rights only: Anthropic may redact security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information, but cannot redact findings just because they are unfavorable, and reviewers may say publicly if a redaction removed something important to their conclusions.

The essay calls this an unusual step for a company, framed as proving out the concept, and urges other frontier companies to follow suit.

## Pacing within democracies

Once embedded evaluators operate within a critical mass of US AI companies, verifiable pacing becomes viable — in particular pacing based on detailed properties of models or training pipelines.

### Regulation as the most effective route

The most effective pacing method is regulation targeting all US frontier companies, since it covers even unwilling cooperators. Anthropic's long-standing position is support for sensible, targeted regulation focused on transparency and third-party auditing. The essay calls on all frontier labs to partner with government to formalize permanent embedded evaluators — to better prevent and document internal alignment incidents like those of recent months — and to implement regulation keeping capabilities in balance with safety.

### Voluntary standards in parallel

Laws take time and AI advances quickly, so companies should voluntarily set standards in parallel — a process that works better with the verifiability of permanent embedded evaluators. For antitrust reasons, the US government should mediate or at least enable these discussions (a narrow waiver for safety conversations, without needing to participate), possibly via industry groups with government association such as the mechanism suggested by Demis Hassabis. Either way, discussions should move quickly.

### Capability-based checkpoints

The essay is most enthusiastic about pacing based on what a frontier system can do and how safe it is observed to be. One possible scheme is a series of "checkpoints": if models have capability X, they need certifications of alignment properties Y and Z — some combination of evaluations, interpretability analyses, and audits of training environments — demonstrating their alignment. The worked example: X might be "the model is capable of escaping or defeating most common sandboxing methods" and Y whatever is required to make it very unlikely the model has a propensity to break out and take over many computers.

### Ingredient-based limits as a secondary option

Pacing could also limit inputs to frontier models: training compute, the nature of training runs, or internal use of AI to improve AI. The essay worries these are more "gameable" than external behavior, but treats them as worth discussing with embedded evaluators.

### The bound: preserving democracies' lead

Pacing within democracies is limited by the US companies' lead over authoritarian regimes, chiefly the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). Slowing by more than that lead lets unpaced CCP-associated projects pull ahead — a national security risk. The essay agrees with Secretary Bessent that a Chinese lead would pose grave danger: CCP projects would run the alignment risks US companies prevent and could militarily dominate democracies (e.g., with AI-driven drones). So pacing within democracies requires keeping the democracies' AI lead as large as possible to create breathing room.

Three defensive steps are listed:

- Do not sell powerful AI chips or semiconductor manufacturing equipment to China, and crack down on chip smuggling operations and remote access to data centers outside China — chips being the main determinant of China's AI strength.
- Crack down on unauthorized distillation by companies in authoritarian countries, since distillation lets laggards narrow the gap at a fraction of independent development cost.
- Strengthen security at AI companies and prevent model weight theft.

Companies and the US government should cooperate to make these effective; Anthropic notes it has consistently advocated all three as essential to any pacing. Executed well, they could slow China's progress enough to widen America's lead significantly over the next 3–5 years — the window when AI becomes geopolitically most important. The essay closes this thread by arguing these measures do not hinder cooperation with China but increase democracies' leverage and make a future agreement more likely.
