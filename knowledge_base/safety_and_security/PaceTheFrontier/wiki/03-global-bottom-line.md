# Global pacing and bottom line

**In one sentence:** Frontier safety requires worldwide pacing alongside democratic pacing, pursued through verifiable layered agreements with China while protecting the US-allied lead, because deliberate pacing buys time to solve interpretability, security, and alignment before AI benefits can be safely realized.

## Key points

- In parallel with pacing within democracies, aim for worldwide pacing of the frontier, with cooperation with China as the central hard requirement given its advanced AI capabilities.
- Any global pacing agreement must either have ironclad verifiability or be limited enough that defection would not be militarily existential, since unilateral restraint followed by defection could hand the defector geopolitical dominance.
- Level 1 — prohibit narrow, obviously dangerous uses such as AI for production of biological weapons: eminently feasible because bioterrorist attacks harm all sides including the US and its adversaries.
- Level 2 — both sides test models before release for acute risks in cybersecurity, biology, and alignment, possibly via a global standards body: the body itself is likely feasible but giving it teeth and verifying no secret untested military models is the hard part.
- Level 3 — a "speed limit" on recursive self-improvement (RSI), slowing improvement from "extremely fast" to "only somewhat fast": analogous to SALT treaties, giving up little strategic advantage while greatly improving safety, difficult but just on the edge of possible.
- Level 4 — full pacing or "pause" substantially limiting overall AI development: worth floating but unlikely soon because evading monitoring could radically shift global power, creating enormous defection incentives and very high verification demands.
- Even without formal agreements, shifting informal norms — sharing information on RSI and model misalignment — can convince all parties that recklessness is not in their interest, and any cooperation achieved extends the time available for pacing within democracies.

## Global pacing: goal and constraint

Pacing within democracies should be paralleled by worldwide pacing of the frontier, though global pacing will be much harder to achieve.

It requires cooperation with China, the autocratic country with by far the most advanced AI capabilities.

No naivety is warranted: geopolitical stakes are so high that there will likely be stark limits on what can be achieved, especially at first.

The core defection risk:

- If democracies greatly restrain AI capabilities believing China will reciprocate, and China defects, AI could be so powerful that defection leads to Chinese geopolitical dominance.
- Therefore any agreement must satisfy one of two conditions:
  - have ironclad verifiability, or
  - be limited enough that defection would not be militarily existential.
- Both the US and China likely share these concerns and anxieties.
- Near-term global pacing decisions should be approached in a way that protects the lead of the US and its allies.

## Four levels of possible agreement

Several levels of agreement exist, in order of increasing difficulty — some eminently feasible, some viewed with deep skepticism though still worth trying.

### Level 1: Prohibit narrow dangerous uses

- Agreement prohibiting certain narrow and obviously dangerous uses of AI, such as using AI for the production of biological weapons or allowing users to do so.
- Assessment: probably possible, because bioterrorist attacks are bad for everyone, including both the US and US adversaries.

### Level 2: Pre-release testing for acute risks

- Both sides agree to test their models before release for acute risks in areas such as cybersecurity, biology, and alignment.
- Mechanism: could be done through a global standards body.
- Assessment: creating such a body is likely feasible, but giving it real teeth will be a challenge.
- Central verification problem: ensuring neither side holds secret models which they do not test but may deploy in secret, for example for military applications.

### Level 3: Speed limit on recursive self-improvement

- Some kind of "speed limit" on the rate of recursive self-improvement (RSI).
- Rationale: as models build future models, the rate of improvement may become staggeringly fast; slowing from "extremely fast" to "only somewhat fast" gives up relatively little strategic advantage while potentially greatly improving safety.
- Analogy: the SALT treaties — capping the number of missiles limited the potential for destruction while preserving each country's deterrent.
- Assessment: difficult but just on the edge of being possible.

### Level 4: Full pacing or pause

- Participating governments agree to substantially limit the overall rate of AI development.
- Assessment: worth floating, but unlikely to happen any time soon.
- Reason: defecting by evading monitoring could radically shift the balance of global power, so incentives to defect are enormous and the confidence required in verification is very high.

## Strategy across levels

- Aim for the higher levels while treating the lower levels as much more likely and realistic.
- Any cooperation achieved with China extends the amount of time available for pacing the frontier within the democratic nations.

## Informal norms without formal agreements

- Even absent formal agreements, simply changing informal norms may have some value.
- Sharing information about recursive self-improvement and about the misalignment of models can help convince everyone that it is not in their interest to be reckless.

## Bottom line

- Continued belief that AI can enormously improve the quality of human life; the desire to achieve these benefits is undimmed.
- Benefits arrive only if the technology is built the right way, and — so long as the gained time is used well — it is worth taking unusually deliberate care to get it right.
- Progress will still be relatively fast, and the time should be used to:
  - advance the science of interpretability,
  - improve operational security and rigor at the frontier AI companies,
  - build models whose alignment inspires much more confidence.
- The proposed measures to advance the frontier at a safe pace will not be easy, but they are owed to humanity to try.
