# Sebastian Raschka on Kimi K3, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 and the Open-Weight AI Explosion — In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Think of an AI chatbot like a dish at a restaurant. A "closed" or "proprietary" model — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — is a dish you can only order at that restaurant: you eat what's served, on their schedule, at their price, and you never see the recipe. An "open-weight" model is different: the company publishes the actual recipe and hands you all the ingredients (the "weights" — the millions of learned numbers that make the AI work), so you can cook it yourself, in your own kitchen, on your own computer, and even tinker with it. This podcast episode is a conversation about a sudden explosion of new, free recipes — models with names like DeepSeek, GLM, Qwen, and Kimi — all released within a few weeks of each other, each claiming to cook almost as well as the expensive restaurant dishes.

The guest, Sebastian Raschka, is an AI researcher and the author of a couple of well-known books that teach people to build these systems "from scratch." The host, Hugo Bowne-Anderson, invited him on the very day a brand-new recipe — "Kimi K3" — was published, so much of the conversation is Raschka thinking out loud, in real time, about what's actually new inside it versus what's just repackaged from earlier recipes. It's an informal, spoken conversation, not a lecture or a scientific paper — closer to two chefs comparing notes on a new competitor's dish than a certified recipe review.

Threaded through the specific "what changed in this new model" talk is a bigger, more useful question: as these free recipes keep multiplying and improving, what's actually changed under the hood, what's just better packaging, and how should an ordinary person or developer decide whether to cook at home (run a model on their own machine) or keep ordering out (pay for a subscription service)?

## Why does it matter?

For years, the assumption was that the best AI lived behind a paywall, on someone else's servers, and that "free, downloadable" AI models lagged 6-9 months behind. This episode is about that gap closing fast — sometimes down to weeks. If that trend keeps going, it changes real decisions people make: whether it's worth paying for a subscription AI service at all, whether privacy-sensitive work (medical questions, confidential business data) can be handled on a personal computer instead of being sent to a company's servers, and whether "the newest model just came out" is actually a reason to switch tools.

It also matters for anyone using an AI coding assistant. A recurring worry in the conversation is that these AI "brains" are increasingly trained and tested using one specific companion tool (called a "harness" — more on that below), and may quietly perform worse when plugged into a different one. That means the tool you use to talk to an AI coding assistant isn't a neutral wrapper — it can be the difference between the assistant working well and working badly, even with the exact same underlying model.

## How does it work?

Here is a plain-language walkthrough of the main mechanisms discussed, in the order that makes them easiest to grasp:

1. **A "team of specialists" instead of one generalist (mixture of experts).** Instead of one giant AI brain that tries to know everything, many of these models are built like a large firm with many specialist employees ("experts"), where a receptionist (a small "router" component) reads each question and forwards it to just a few relevant specialists rather than calling an all-hands meeting every time. This keeps the model fast and cheap to run even though its total "headcount" (parameter count) is enormous — only a handful of specialists get called in per question.

2. **Compressing before you store or send it (latent/compressed attention and "latent" experts).** Imagine mailing someone a photo: instead of shipping the full, giant image, you compress it into a small file, mail that, and the recipient decompresses it back into something close to the original on arrival. Several new models do exactly this internally — both for the "notes" the model keeps about earlier words in a conversation (compressing them before storing them in its working memory) and for the specialists themselves (using smaller, compressed versions of specialists instead of full-size ones). The point isn't to make the model smarter, but to make it cheaper and faster to run without losing much quality.

3. **Swapping the engine, not redesigning the car (hybrid/linear attention).** The classic mechanism AI models use to figure out which earlier words in a sentence matter — called "attention" — gets more expensive the longer the conversation gets, roughly like re-reading the entire conversation from scratch for every new word. Newer models increasingly replace this in most (not all) of their internal layers with a cheaper, more streamlined version that scales better with long conversations — Raschka compares it to swapping a car's gasoline engine for an electric motor: same car, same four wheels, just a more efficient engine under the hood.

4. **A brain needs a body to act in the world (the "harness").** An AI model on its own can only take in text and spit out text — it can't click a button, run code, or browse a file system. The surrounding software that gives it hands and eyes — letting it use tools, browse the web, edit files, or run a terminal command — is called the "harness." The host's analogy from Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles fits well: the model is like Krang, a disembodied brain, while the harness is the robotic body-suit Krang needs to actually act and move around in the world. The same "brain" plugged into two different "bodies" (say, two different coding-assistant tools) can behave quite differently, because each harness manages memory, tools, and instructions in its own way.

5. **Choosing how hard to think (effort levels).** Beyond picking which model or specialist to use, many models can also dial their own effort up or down for a given question — similar to a person deciding whether to do quick mental math in their head or sit down with a calculator and show every step. Companies train models to have "light," "medium," and "high" effort settings (sometimes crossed with subject area, like math versus writing), then merge all those variants into one model you can switch between with a setting. Right now, a human (or a fixed setting) has to choose the effort level in advance; nobody has yet built a system where the software itself notices "this looks like an easy question" or "this is a hard one" and adjusts automatically, mid-conversation.

6. **Practice makes a specialist, but only in their own gym (harness overfitting).** Because many models are trained to solve tasks primarily inside their own official harness, they can become a bit like an athlete who's only ever trained in one specific gym: excellent there, but a little off their game in an unfamiliar one — perhaps reaching for the wrong tool, or looping through extra unnecessary steps. This is a suspected pattern, not a proven fact — the conversation treats it as a plausible explanation for something the guest has personally noticed (a model behaving less efficiently outside its "home" tool), while acknowledging other explanations (like a less efficient tool, independent of the model) are equally possible.

## Where can this be used?

- **Deciding between a subscription AI service and running a model at home.** If your subscription is cheap relative to how much you use it, cooking at home (self-hosting) mostly buys you privacy and the hobbyist satisfaction of running your own hardware — not better answers. It becomes more compelling once you either hit usage limits on a subscription, need to keep sensitive data (medical, financial, confidential business information) off someone else's servers, or simply want a backup option in case prices rise or access changes later.
- **Understanding why your AI coding assistant "feels different" depending on the tool.** If an assistant seems to use the wrong tool, loop excessively, or burn far more effort than expected in one coding tool versus another, part of the explanation may be that the underlying model was mostly trained and tested inside a specific companion tool — the "gym" it knows best — so pairing model and tool thoughtfully (and not assuming any tool is a neutral wrapper) matters.
- **Deciding whether to chase every new model release.** Because so much of what makes a new release feel smarter is actually product polish (nicer image handling, smoother mid-task interruption, computer-control features) rather than a deeper leap in how the model "thinks," it's often reasonable to skip a given release and wait, rather than feeling obligated to switch the moment a new name appears.
- **Sizing expectations for self-hosting a large model.** Genuinely large models generally need more computing muscle than one ordinary computer has, and renting that muscle from cloud providers is often unavailable or expensive; a practical middle path is trying a new model through a hosted pass-through service first, and only investing in local hardware once you're sure it's worth keeping.

## Conclusions & takeaways

- **The gap between "free, downloadable" AI and "paid, closed" AI is shrinking fast**, but a lot of the apparent improvement is product packaging (how the tool is presented and used) rather than a fundamentally smarter model underneath.
- **New model architectures are mostly incremental tweaks bolted onto a stable core**, not full reinventions — compressing information before storing it, calling in fewer specialists per question, and swapping in cheaper "attention" mechanisms in most layers.
- **The tool you use to talk to a model is not neutral** — a model's behavior can shift meaningfully depending on which companion software (harness) is running it, especially since models are increasingly trained mostly inside one specific harness.
- **Running AI on your own computer today is mainly a privacy and hobby choice**, not a way to get better answers than a subscription service — though that could change if subscription costs or limits become more restrictive.
- **Honest limitation:** this whole conversation is one experienced researcher's same-day, informal reading of a technical report he skimmed hours earlier — not a peer-reviewed study. Several specific numbers (parameter counts, training-data sizes, token-budget details) are given from memory, explicitly flagged by the guest himself as approximate and possibly misremembered, and should be treated as "roughly this order of magnitude," not as precise, verified facts.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | Plain meaning |
|------|---------------|
| Open-weight model | An AI model whose underlying "recipe" (the trained numbers) is published for anyone to download and run themselves, unlike ChatGPT or Claude, which you can only access through the provider's own service. |
| Weights / parameters | The huge set of learned numbers inside a model that determine how it responds — roughly, the model's "knowledge" encoded as adjustable dials. |
| Mixture of experts (MoE) | A model design where many smaller specialist sub-networks exist, but only a few are consulted for any given question, like routing a call to the right department instead of asking everyone. |
| Latent / compressed representation | A smaller, squeezed-down version of some information (like a compressed photo) that captures most of what matters while taking up much less space and being cheaper to process. |
| Attention (in a model) | The mechanism a model uses to decide which earlier words or pieces of information matter most when producing its next word. |
| Harness | The surrounding software (a coding assistant app, a chat app, a command-line tool) that gives an AI model the ability to actually do things — use tools, browse files, run code — beyond just producing text. |
| Effort level / reasoning effort | A setting that controls how much extra "thinking" (extra computation and generated reasoning) a model does before answering — like choosing between quick mental math and working it out with a calculator. |
| Token | A chunk of text (often a word or part of a word) that a model reads or generates one piece at a time; "how many tokens" roughly tracks how much a model had to "say" or "think" to answer. |
| Fine-tuning | Additional training done after a model's main training, to specialize or adjust its behavior for a particular purpose (e.g., following instructions well, or working inside a specific tool). |
| RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) | A training method where a model is rewarded or penalized based on whether its answer can be automatically checked as correct (like a graded math problem), encouraging it to get better at tasks with clear right answers. |
| Distillation | Compressing what several larger or more specialized models learned into a single, smaller model that mimics their combined behavior. |
| Pretraining vs. post-training | Pretraining is the first, massive phase where a model learns general language patterns from huge amounts of text; post-training is everything that comes after, shaping the model toward being helpful, safe, and good at specific tasks. |
