# Critical Analysis: Sebastian Raschka on Kimi K3, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 and the Open-Weight AI Explosion

## Claims vs. evidence

**"Kimi K3 = Kimi Linear scaled up + latent MoE + residual attention."** *Suggestive-to-strong.* This is the one claim with real evidentiary weight in the episode: Raschka spent the afternoon of the recording speed-reading the actual Kimi K3 technical report and sketching its architecture diagram, and the description (93 vs. 27 layers, Kimi Delta Attention, latent-MoE compression analogous to Nemotron 3 Ultra) is a same-day read of a primary source, not a recalled impression. But even here the specific cost figures he attaches to residual attention ("4 or 5% training cost and 2 or 3% inference cost") are his recollection of the *separate* Kimi Linear standalone paper, not something he re-verified against the K3 report itself — a borrowed number, not a measured one.

**"Most 'the new model feels smarter' gains are product-layer, not training-layer."** *Weak, and explicitly self-rated as such.* Raschka states outright: "I can't know how much the models improve really versus... whether they just have better [tools]... or whether it's almost like polishing versus training" [19:12]-[19:57]. This is offered as an instinct ("my instinct is... it is almost more like on a product level"), not a claim he backs with any comparison, benchmark, or ablation. It is a genuinely interesting hypothesis, but the transcript itself never elevates it above "instinct."

**"Models may be overfitting to their native harness."** *Unsupported.* This is co-constructed live by Hugo and Raschka in real time — Hugo proposes the RLVR-in-one-harness mechanism, Raschka responds "Ah... I see" (visibly working it out on the spot) and later calls it "a good question" rather than a known answer [23:44]-[26:03]. The one supporting anecdote (Claude Code using 2-3x the tokens of other harnesses for the same task) is immediately undercut by Raschka's own caveat that this "is not necessarily over-fitting... it's also just like how efficient the harness compacts information" [26:03]-[26:49]. No experiment was run to distinguish the two explanations.

**"Pre-training datasets are now ~30 trillion tokens."** *Weak.* Raschka's own framing: "I don't recall from the Ki[mi] report just too many numbers... or maybe I'm conflating it with another recent model I looked at, but I think 30 trillion is currently like the common size" [65:28]. This is presented conversationally as a rounded, half-remembered figure, explicitly flagged as possibly cross-contaminated with a different model's number — the opposite of a citable statistic.

## Genuinely new vs. repackaged

The one piece of actually new information is Raschka's real-time read of the Kimi K3 technical report, published only hours before the recording — the architecture diagram, the latent-MoE/residual-attention breakdown, and the effort-specialist description ("based on the report I just skimmed today" [44:38]) did not exist as public commentary before this conversation.

Almost everything else is a restatement of trends already documented elsewhere, and the transcript is honest about this. Hybrid/linear attention (Gated DeltaNet in Qwen3-Next, Kimi Delta Attention, Mamba-2 layers in Nemotron) is presented as the dominant trend, but all three are named, pre-existing implementations, not new mechanisms coined here. DeepSeek V4's "highway connections" are explicitly said to have been "not invented... by DeepSeek version four, but... popularized" [58:29] — the transcript itself denies novelty. Multi-head latent attention is traced back to DeepSeek V3 [16:54], and effort-level selection via system prompt is explicitly called out as pre-existing GPT-OSS behavior: "this is not new... even back the GPT-OSS model from OpenAI had that" [44:38]. Kimi Linear itself (the "research prototype" that K3 scales up) and Nemotron 3 Ultra's LatentMoE are both named prior-art reference points, not contributions of this episode.

## Weaknesses and blind spots

Raschka flags several of his own gaps directly: he states he does not benchmark models himself ("I honestly don't really... benchmark things myself. I just use them... you kind of get a feeling" [27:36]), and about the looped-transformer architecture he mentions, he says plainly "I don't think they did a nice ablation study in the paper or technical report... so I don't know how much that actually improved" [60:02] — a gap in the source material he is citing, not just in his own knowledge.

What he does *not* flag as a gap is more interesting: the harness-overfitting discussion never considers a third explanation beyond "RLVR baked into weights" and "harness context-compaction differences" — e.g., that a native harness might simply expose different tools/prompts entirely, making "overfitting" and "just a different task" hard to distinguish even in principle. Similarly, the "what is 1+1" efficiency probe is presented as a meaningful signal of model-generation progress, but it is a single anecdotal prompt tried informally on whichever models he happens to set up, not a described sample of models or a controlled comparison — he never says how many models, or how systematically, this was applied.

Numeric precision degrades wherever recall is under same-day time pressure: model-size figures for GLM-5.2 (~2.7T) and Kimi K3 (~2.8T) are given with the explicit caveat "just my brain is also from looking at numbers today" [28:24]-[29:10], and the wiki notes the numbers are internally inconsistent — Raschka calls Kimi K3 "four times as big" in the same breath as citing figures that are actually roughly comparable. The "nine specialists" figure (3 effort levels × 3 domains) is Raschka's own multiplication from a report he elsewhere admits can be ambiguous about exactly this kind of combinatorial claim: "is it combined?... a lot of that is speculation also sometimes when you read these papers" [62:24]-[63:10] — an admission that undercuts confidence in the very number the summary reports as a "finding."

## Applicability

This source is useful as a fast, technically literate orientation to "what changed in open-weight LLMs in the last three months," and as a source of testable mental models — the product-vs-training distinction, the timeline/building-blocks learning approach, the Pareto-frontier framing for model/harness selection. It is not reliable as a citation for any specific number — model sizes, token budgets, cost percentages, specialist counts — without checking the underlying technical reports directly; nearly every number in the episode carries an explicit or implicit "recalled, not verified" qualifier.

**Relevance to my work:**
- The harness-model coupling/overfitting question is directly relevant to choosing coding-agent tooling for Fleet's coder workers (e.g., whether a model performs differently inside its native CLI vs. a general harness) — but per the transcript, this is currently a hunch with one confounded anecdote, not a tested effect, so it should inform experiments rather than tooling defaults.
- The effort-level/routing discussion (harness should pick model size and reasoning effort dynamically, which Raschka says "no one is doing... as far as I know" [46:57]) maps directly onto Fleet's own orchestrator design decisions (`--coder`/`--model` selection per task) — this is an open engineering problem, not a solved pattern to copy.
- The local-vs-hosted tradeoff framing (privacy as the main reason to self-host, not raw capability) is relevant to any Elisity/Athena data-lake work involving sensitive network or security telemetry that shouldn't transit third-party inference APIs.
- The "architecture as timeline, one component at a time" framing is a reasonable way to stay current on LLM architecture without chasing every release — consistent with treating the field as a marathon rather than reading every technical report same-day, which is the discipline Raschka admits he himself broke for this episode.

## What this changes

If the trends described hold up, self-hosting mid-size open models becomes more viable as both hardware (DGX Spark, Mac M4/M5 unified-memory throughput) and models improve in parallel — though Raschka's own numbers put this at "usable, not fast" (20-30 tok/s, maxing out 80-90GB of a 120GB box) today, not yet a capability advantage. The genuinely unresolved open question, by Raschka's own account, is harness-level automatic effort/model routing — no implementation of this exists yet that either he or Hugo has seen. A practitioner should track architecture family lineage (Kimi Linear → Kimi K3, DeepSeek V3's MLA → DeepSeek V4's highway connections, Qwen3-Next's Gated DeltaNet → other hybrid-attention variants) rather than every individual release, since Raschka's own account of how these models get built is incremental composition onto prior families, not ground-up redesign.

## Verdict

This is an unusually well-informed practitioner thinking out loud, not a verified technical source — the strongest evidence in the episode (the Kimi K3 architecture read) sits alongside claims that are explicitly hedged as same-day guesses, half-remembered numbers, and mutual speculation with the host. Take the architectural framing and mental models seriously; treat every specific number (parameter counts, token budgets, specialist counts, cost percentages) as a lead to verify against the primary technical report, not a citable fact. The single strongest reason to keep tracking this kind of source rather than dismissing it: Raschka's same-day reads of primary technical reports are a rare, fast signal for where architecture is heading, even though the numbers attached to them need independent verification. **Watch.**
