# FormBharo: Designing and Evaluating a Voice Agent for Conversational Form Filling in Rural India

**Paper:** [FormBharo: Designing and Evaluating a Voice Agent for Conversational Form Filling in Rural India (Dalmia et al., 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06027)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a rural mother who needs free maternal-health care but cannot read the enrollment form, so today a busy health worker must walk her through it question by question, like a guide with a clipboard. FormBharo is an automated phone caller that plays that guide's role: it asks each question aloud in Hindi, listens to her spoken answers, writes them into the right boxes, politely repeats a question when it mishears, and skips ahead when an answer changes what comes next. The big lesson is like testing a relay team rather than individual sprinters: the speech recognizer that looks best on paper can still fumble the baton on a noisy phone line, so the authors built a full obstacle-course benchmark with real recorded voices and found that strict end-to-end testing — not single-part scores — is what picks the team that actually gets mothers enrolled.

## TL;DR

FormBharo ("fill the form" in Hindi) is a hybrid voice agent that completes a 12-field maternal and child health enrollment form over a phone call under tight latency and cost budgets by using LLMs only where they add value — an EXTRACT LLM that turns transcripts into structured field values plus a short acknowledgement, and a REPLY LLM that phrases the next question naturally — while keeping validation, retries, skip/end failure handling, branching, and next-question selection fully rule-based around a form state that acts as the single source of truth. It is being piloted in rural Maharashtra with ARMMAN to enroll low-income, Hindi-speaking mothers in antenatal and postnatal care, claimed as the first conversational voice agent piloted for this population and form-filling use. The authors openly release FormVoiceAgentBench, a Hindi benchmark grounded in the pilot form that pairs 380 human-recorded audio clips with 3,760 multi-turn conversation tests across 960 simulated calls (240 call scripts times four acoustic conditions), scoring transcription, per-turn extraction, reply generation, and end-to-end form completion under real acoustic variation. Across 5 STT models and 11 LLMs, the headline results are that real-speech transcripts cut form completion by up to ~41 percentage points versus reference transcripts, that component-level leaders reorder once chained (GPT-5.5 leads turn-level extraction on clean text at 99.79% but not end-to-end), that the rule-based layer recovers many extraction errors so smaller cheaper models match or beat frontier models, and that no model wins on accuracy, cost, and latency at once — so the deployed configuration (Scribe v2 for STT, Gemini 3.5 Flash for EXTRACT, GPT-5.4-mini for REPLY) is chosen by sequential Pareto filtering plus weighted-sum scalarization.

## Problem & Motivation

In India almost every social benefit begins with a form a citizen must complete to enroll, yet the people who need these benefits most are disproportionately low-income and low-literacy — more than half the women in the lowest wealth quintile cannot read at all — so enrollment in practice falls to frontline health workers signing up beneficiaries one conversation at a time. That human channel does not scale: documentation already consumes much of frontline workers' day across dozens of overlapping reporting systems with the same data re-entered across apps and paper registers, which caps enrollment at worker capacity rather than need, and since these records are the administrator's source of truth for allocating resources, enrollment errors and delays slow the whole system's response. Voice is the natural way in, with strong precedent in Kilkari's outbound prerecorded-call service reaching more than 10 million subscribers at ~1.2 million calls per day and evidence that a carefully designed speech interface beats touch-tone on task completion for both low-literate and literate users, and LLM-powered voice agents now make real-time spoken interaction increasingly practical with the target experience that a mother can simply call a number and enroll by speaking naturally, with nothing to read, type, or install.

The difficulty is that the cooperative-speaker-in-a-quiet-room assumption breaks immediately in this setting, because form-filling calls compound background noise, telephone-channel distortion, underrepresented regional accents, unusual speaking rates, mispronunciations, disfluencies, Hindi-English code-mixing, structured values such as phone numbers and dates that are spoken many ways but resolve to one value, caller hesitation and mid-answer self-correction, and branching where one mis-captured field sends the agent down the wrong path. FormBharo is framed directly against these constraints: a spoken form-filler for low-literacy users in low- and middle-income countries that must work over real phone audio within deployment latency and cost budgets, where transcription errors are not a footnote but the dominant failure mode that the architecture and the evaluation methodology must both survive.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Hybrid LLM-plus-rule-based call pipeline** — The turn flow chains Voice Activity Detection, STT transcription, an EXTRACT LLM that reads the transcript plus conversation history and extracts every field answered in that turn (returning option indices for categorical fields) while generating a short acknowledgement in parallel to mask latency, a deterministic rule-based layer that validates each value against field-specific guards such as minimum length, 10 digits for phone numbers, or past-only dates, manages per-field retry limits by passing validation errors plus history to REPLY for re-asks, handles skip flags when callers decline or do not know optional fields, writes validated values into the form state, evaluates branch activation, and selects the next question (or ends the call), and a REPLY LLM plus TTS that phrases the selected question naturally, with graceful interruption handling that terminates the in-flight turn when the caller starts speaking. The stated design principle is to use LLMs only for interpreting inputs, structuring answers, phrasing questions, and completion decisions, with everything else rule-based.

2. **FormVoiceAgentBench: noisy-audio form-completion benchmark** — The authors define a methodology and release a Hindi benchmark grounded in the pilot's 12-field enrollment form (name, district, clinic name, pregnancy/child branches, clinic-linked and WhatsApp numbers, Aadhaar last 4 digits, each with skip-or-end failure semantics and conditional fields), evaluated at two levels: unit tests scoring each component in isolation and integration tests chaining them to measure end-to-end form completion. Five simulated user profiles with fixed personal details each carry a value, a GPT-5.5-generated natural spoken transcript (with phonetically hard names and districts from outside the pilot state and multi-verbalization numbers, plus decline variants for optional fields), and recordings by five native Hindi-speaking women aged 18–35 from Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra under four acoustic conditions per utterance (ideal, background chatter, random close/far microphone distance, random fast/slow pace, supervisor-checked), stitched into 48 form paths per user for 240 call scripts and 960 calls, deduplicated into 1,880 EXTRACT and 1,880 REPLY unit tests (920 reply plus 960 end-call decisions) with no real-caller PII.

3. **Calibrated unit-plus-integration scoring with LLM judges** — Closed-ended fields and skip flags are scored by exact match while open-ended names, clinic names, and acknowledgements use a calibrated binary LLM judge (gpt-5.4-mini at temperature 0) with tolerant transliteration rubrics, and each REPLY is graded independently on five dimensions by separate judges (correct next question, Hindi adherence, single-question conciseness, no acknowledgement, no value echo), calibrated by iterative prompt revision to full human agreement on 50 tests and confirmed on a held-out 50. Integration tests swap expected values for actual upstream outputs — EXTRACT's real output through validation and flow control for REPLY inputs, and every STT-by-EXTRACT and STT-by-EXTRACT-by-REPLY combination end-to-end — so the gap to reference-transcript runs directly quantifies transcription-error impact.

4. **Sequential Pareto-based weighted-sum model selection** — Because each downstream stage consumes the previous stage's outputs, STT is selected first, then EXTRACT on the chosen STT's transcripts, then REPLY with both fixed, optimizing task accuracy against p95 latency and per-turn cost by discarding deployment-constraint violators, keeping Pareto-optimal candidates, and ranking the rest with min–max-normalized weighted-sum scalarization (accuracy, latency, and cost with latency and cost reversed so higher is better), sweeping the accuracy weight from 0.5 to 0.9 at fixed low latency weight to reflect that the hard latency gate already did most of the latency work.

## Key Findings

Standard word error rate misleads for this task, so STT models are ranked by LLM-WER: Nova-3 has the best raw WER yet the second-worst LLM-WER, Chirp 3 has the best LLM-WER but costs at least twice any other model and is eliminated alongside least-accurate GPT-4o-transcribe, leaving Scribe v2, Saaras v3, and Nova-3 for integration, with Scribe v2 selected for the strongest downstream medians (97.29% extraction, 91.84% completion) and the smallest drops versus reference transcripts. On clean reference transcripts frontier extraction saturates with GPT-5.5 at 99.79% ahead of Gemini 3.5 Flash at 99.36% and two Claude models at 99.15%, and median per-turn drops on real transcripts are modest at 0.42 to 3.14 points, but robustness varies sharply with the four most accurate models losing at most 4.63 points while weaker ones collapse (GLM-5.1 by 35 points on Scribe v2), and the leaderboard reorders on real speech to 98.94% for Gemini 3.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4.6 on Scribe v2, Claude Opus 4.8 on Saaras v3 at 96.22%, and Gemini 3.5 Flash on Nova-3 at 96.38%.

End-to-end form completion tells a different story than per-turn extraction in both directions: the rule-based layer normalizes whole error classes, as when Gemini 3 Flash converts 95.96% extraction into 100% completion on reference transcripts by fixing numeric-as-string type mismatches, so the best per-turn model (GPT-5.5) is not the best end-to-end model (Gemini 3 Flash and 3.5 Flash tied at 100% on reference) — yet completion degrades more than extraction once speech errors enter, with median completion drops of 7.67 points on Saaras v3, 7.38 on Scribe v2, and 13.56 on Nova-3 against extraction medians of only 0.42–3.14, peaking at a ~41-point collapse for GLM-5.1 on Scribe v2 as uncorrected errors accumulate across turns. That two-sided gap is the paper's central empirical punchline: errors both propagate and cancel across the pipeline, so component accuracy does not predict form completion and the configuration emerges only through end-to-end evaluation.

Selection under constraints then favors the efficient middle over the frontier extremes: for EXTRACT on Scribe v2 transcripts the p95-under-5-seconds and above-90%-completion gates exclude even the completion leader Gemini Pro, all six survivors sit on the Pareto frontier, and at the deployment weighting Gemini 3.5 Flash wins for accuracy weights 0.50–0.61 before Claude Sonnet 4.6 takes over at 0.62–0.90, with Gemini 3.5 Flash deployed because it trails Sonnet 4.6 by only 0.51 points while responding faster at less than half the per-turn cost (equal weights would instead crown Mistral Medium 3.5). REPLY, the narrowest role, shows error propagation of 1.06–3.40 points with a 2.23 median, the p95-under-4-seconds and above-95%-accuracy gates exclude Sonnet 4.6 on latency and Gemini 3.5 Flash on accuracy, Pareto filtering leaves GPT-5.4-mini and Gemini 3 Flash, and GPT-5.4-mini ranks highest throughout the sweep and is selected at 96.70% on Scribe v2 transcripts with 3,356 ms p95 and $0.0008 per turn.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The authors are explicit that the benchmark is still a controlled proxy for the field: answers are scripted rather than spontaneous, each call holds a single acoustic condition throughout instead of mixing noise, distance, and pace within a call, only five simulated users and five annotators stand in for a diverse caller population, all data is Hindi-only, TTS quality is not evaluated, judge calibration rests on a small 50-plus-50 sample the authors plan to expand, and every configuration ran once on nondeterministic hosted APIs so confidence intervals reflect variation across test items rather than repeated runs. The natural next steps flow directly from those limits: broaden the speaker pool and linguistic coverage beyond Hindi with spontaneous, disfluent, code-mixed speech and within-call acoustic shifts, evaluate the TTS side and full duplex interruption behavior that live deployment will stress, harden and scale judge calibration, and repeat runs toward variance-aware selection. On the system side the findings argue for investing in the hybrid layer rather than only larger models — richer validation, normalization, and retry/branching logic is what lets smaller cheaper models meet deployment constraints — and for keeping end-to-end, cost- and latency-aware Pareto evaluation as the decision procedure, since any future model swap will again reorder once chained through real speech.
