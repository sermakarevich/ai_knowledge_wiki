# JAL-Turn: Joint Acoustic-Linguistic Modeling for Real-Time and Robust Turn-Taking Detection in Full-Duplex Spoken Dialog

**Paper:** [JAL-Turn: Joint Acoustic-Linguistic Modeling for Real-Time and Robust Turn-Taking Detection in Full-Duplex Spoken Dialog](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26515) — arXiv, March 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine talking to a voice assistant that keeps cutting you off every time you pause to think — that is the turn-taking problem JAL-Turn tackles. Humans hand over the floor using a blend of cues, like the words just said ("my phone number is...") plus the sound of the voice (trailing off, flattening pitch), and they do it within a few hundred milliseconds. JAL-Turn listens with two ears at once: one ear for what was said and one ear for how it sounded, fusing both to decide whether you are truly done or just catching your breath. Because one of those ears is shared with the speech recognizer, the decision arrives in tens of milliseconds with no extra waiting, and the system learns from thousands of hours of real conversations labeled automatically rather than by expensive human annotators.

## TL;DR

JAL-Turn is a lightweight speech-only turn-taking detector that jointly models high-level linguistic cues from a frozen SenseVoice encoder and fine-grained acoustic cues from a frozen CPC encoder, fused through stacked cross-attention and processed by an ALiBi Transformer with attention-based temporal pooling and a sigmoid hold/shift head. Its SenseVoice encoder is shared with ASR so turn-taking predictions are computed synchronously with recognition in a single forward pass, keeping end-to-end latency at 12–38 ms. A scalable data pipeline derives labels automatically from stereo conversational audio via 50 Hz frame-level VAD with a 2-second future-window score requiring agreement across linear, square-root, and exponential weightings, anchoring samples at VAD falling edges with up to 10 seconds of backward context, which converts 1,128 hours of in-house dialogue into ~2,299 hours of training segments at ~85% labeling accuracy. Evaluated on Mandarin Easy-Turn, multilingual STurn-v3 (23 languages), and an in-house Japanese customer-service corpus, JAL-Turn matches or beats the SLM-based EasyTurn on complete turns (96.67% vs 96.33%) at 12 ms versus 263 ms latency and outperforms LLM-based detectors (up to +15 points accuracy at a fraction of the latency), with ablations confirming the SenseVoice stream as the primary driver and CPC, cross-attention, and attention pooling each contributing meaningfully.

## Problem & Motivation

Voice AI agents in customer service, personal assistance, and human–AI collaboration must listen continuously and respond with extremely low latency, which makes them prone to interrupting users mid-utterance. The classical fix — wait for a fixed or adaptive silence duration — is unreliable because silence alone does not signal handover: users constantly produce within-utterance pauses, hesitations, thinking pauses, and self-repairs. Human turn transitions are instead rapid, often on the order of 100–500 ms across languages, which means listeners proactively anticipate completions using a combination of lexical, prosodic, and multimodal cues rather than merely reacting to quiet. Earlier data-driven models built on acoustic or linguistic features used relatively simple architectures under real-time constraints and could not capture these fine-grained discriminative cues, while recent LLM/SLM full-duplex integrations improve quality at a steep price: they need large manually annotated dialogue corpora that are expensive and hard to scale, multi-function backbones such as ASR add latency, they prioritize semantics while discarding fine-grained acoustic cues, and they degrade in complex real-world scenarios. JAL-Turn is motivated by this gap between accuracy and real-time deployability, aiming to jointly exploit what was said and how it sounded in a lightweight model that runs synchronously with recognition and learns from automatically labeled real-world conversation at scale.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Dual frozen encoders for complementary cues.** JAL-Turn processes the input waveform through two parallel frozen encoders: a SenseVoice encoder (512-dim frame features) that emphasizes high-level semantic and linguistic elements, and a contrastive predictive coding (CPC) encoder (256-dim frame features) that emphasizes low-level acoustic regularities learned via self-supervised contrastive learning. Both encoders are kept frozen to preserve pre-trained knowledge, and their outputs are linearly projected into a shared latent space, so the model jointly exploits rich linguistic context and fine-grained prosodic detail instead of forcing one backbone to do both jobs.

2. **Encoder sharing for zero-overhead parallel inference.** The SenseVoice encoder is shared between ASR and JAL-Turn, so turn-taking predictions are computed synchronously with ASR in parallel from a single forward pass of the shared encoder, with no extra stages before or after ASR decoding. This architectural choice is what makes the system deployable: turn-state estimation rides along with recognition the system already performs rather than adding a separate sequential pipeline.

3. **Cross-attention acoustic–linguistic fusion.** Fusion uses two stacked cross-attention layers in which SenseVoice features act as queries and CPC features serve as keys and values (with layer normalization, 256-dim model width, 4 heads, residual connections, and position-wise feed-forward networks). Rather than merely concatenating the two streams, the model explicitly learns how acoustic evidence should modulate linguistic hypotheses and vice versa, and ablations confirm this interaction modeling outperforms co-presenting the features.

4. **ALiBi Transformer with attention-based temporal pooling.** The fused representation feeds a causal self-attention Transformer using Attention with Linear Biases (ALiBi), which replaces absolute positional embeddings with a distance-dependent bias on attention logits, aiding length extrapolation and encoding a recency bias well matched to the local temporal patterns governing turn-taking. An attention-based temporal pooling layer then computes normalized weights over time steps and forms a weighted utterance representation that focuses on the most informative segments before a lightweight linear head emits a shift logit with sigmoid activation, thresholded at 0.5 and trained with binary cross-entropy (shift = 1, hold = 0).

5. **Scalable automatic labeling and context pipeline.** Instead of manual turn annotation, the pipeline extracts frame-level stereo VAD at 50 Hz per channel and labels each frame with a weighted VAD score over a 2-second future window, keeping a Hold/Shift label only when linear, square-root, and exponential weighting schemes all agree — a consensus design that suppresses backchannels, since instantaneous VAD comparisons are easily confounded by brief listener responses while future VAD patterns reveal genuine transitions. Training samples are anchored at each VAD falling edge after a speech segment, with context extended backward to the previous long silence (≥ 2 seconds) and normalized to a fixed 10-second window via left-padding or truncation so each sample carries a complete preceding speech segment. Applied to 1,128 hours of in-house stereo conversation this yields ~2,299 hours of trainable segments at ~85% labeling accuracy by manual inspection, complemented by a 95-hour single-utterance set yielding 749 hours of near-100%-accurate but conversationally narrow supervision, with the model trained on a mixture of both.

## Key Findings

On the Mandarin Easy-Turn benchmark, JAL-Turn achieves the best complete-turn accuracy (96.67%) at just 12 ms end-to-end latency, markedly beating Paraformer+TEN (86.67% at 204 ms) and STurn-v2 (78.67% at 27 ms), while matching or slightly edging the SLM-based EasyTurn on complete turns (96.67% vs 96.33%) at roughly one-twentieth the latency (12 ms vs 263 ms). It stays within 4 and 6 points of EasyTurn on incomplete and wait states but trails on backchannels (80% vs 91%), a gap the authors attribute to the intrinsically context-dependent nature of backchannels as short, semantically light responses whose role is better determined with explicit lexical cues — and despite that gap the paper concludes JAL-Turn offers a substantially more favorable quality–latency trade-off under strict real-time constraints. On multilingual STurn-v3 and the in-house Japanese corpus, JAL-Turn reaches 93.27% accuracy (0.934 F1) and 92.03% accuracy (0.925 F1) respectively at 36–38 ms latency, strongly outperforming STurn-v2 and matching or beating STurn-v3 with large gains on the real-world in-house data. Against LLM-based detectors on the in-house benchmark it wins decisively on both quality and speed: 92.03%/0.925 at 38 ms versus 76.91%/0.817 at 595 ms for Gemini-2.5-Flash, 78.70%/0.782 at 124 ms for Qwen3-0.6B, and 85.52%/0.874 at over a second for GPT-5.1, improving accuracy by 6.5–15 absolute points while cutting latency by multiples. Ablations confirm each component earns its place: dropping the SenseVoice encoder collapses accuracy from 92.03% to 72.01%, showing linguistically enriched representations are the primary driver; removing CPC costs nearly 8 points (to 84.18%), confirming complementary acoustic robustness; eliminating cross-attention while keeping both encoders drops accuracy to 88.59%, proving explicit interaction beats feature co-presence; and replacing attention pooling degrades accuracy to 90.23% while raising latency from 38 ms to 48 ms, showing the lightweight pooling improves both representation quality and the accuracy–latency trade-off. The model trains end-to-end in 10 epochs on a single H100 GPU with AdamW and cosine annealing, and the paper reports consistent gains in accuracy and robustness with real-time performance on both public multilingual benchmarks and the in-house Japanese customer-service dataset of 500 human-labeled business samples spanning gender, age, and scenario.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The clearest weakness is backchannel handling, where JAL-Turn lags the SLM-based EasyTurn by 11 points, so a natural next step is to inject explicit lexical or semantic cues for short acknowledgment-like segments — for example a lightweight text-side signal from the shared ASR hypothesis — without surrendering the latency advantage. Since the automatic labels sit at ~85% accuracy on conversational data, improving the VAD future-window scheme (adaptive windows, learned weighting, or confidence-weighted losses) could raise the ceiling imposed by label noise, and the partially inconsistent latency figures reported across tables and running text deserve reconciliation for clean deployment claims. Broader validation should extend beyond Mandarin and Japanese customer service to more languages, noisier acoustic conditions, overlapping speech, and multi-party dialogue, and the recency-biased ALiBi design invites study of longer conversational context versus the current 10-second window. Finally, the encoder-sharing idea generalizes: co-training or distilling turn-taking alongside related full-duplex tasks such as backchannel prediction, interruption handling, and endpointing could yield a single real-time conversational front-end.
