# MSI-Bench: Evaluating Multi-Speaker Voice Interaction for Collaborative AI Agents

**Paper:** [MSI-Bench: Evaluating Multi-Speaker Voice Interaction for Collaborative AI Agents (Xiong et al., 2026)](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24812v1) — arXiv, 21 Sep 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a smart speaker sitting on a busy kitchen counter while Mom, Dad, and the kids all talk over each other — one person sets a rule, another tries to bend it, and a third whispers a secret the speaker must not repeat. Most voice-AI tests quiz the assistant as if only one person ever talks to it, like a private phone call, but MSI-Bench tests the far messier family-dinner reality where the assistant must remember who said what, whose permission counts, and when to stay silent. The authors built over a thousand short bilingual audio plays across homes, hospitals, schools, and workplaces, each ending with a tricky request that hinges on speaker identity, and graded a dozen leading voice models on whether they acted on the right person's facts with the right tools. The headline is sobering: even the best systems fail a third to nearly half the time, open models mostly mishear who spoke, and frontier models hear correctly yet still obey the wrong person or blurt out secrets.

## TL;DR

MSI-Bench reframes voice-agent evaluation from single-user assistance to shared multi-speaker collaboration, testing whether a model grounds its next answer, refusal, clarification, or tool call to the correct speaker, scope, authority, privacy boundary, and constraint priority. The benchmark contains 1,152 test cases evenly split between English and Mandarin (576 each), each a short multi-party multi-turn audio scene with participant context, an available-function catalog with decoys and gold tool calls, and atomic rubrics, organized into three capability families — multi-speaker memory, multi-speaker instruction following, and multi-speaker reasoning — with six patterns total: background speech retrieval, scope tracking, selective disclosure, speaker authority constraint, sequential constraint integration with request ownership, and constraint prioritization. Cases are authored natively per language (not translated) across eight behavior-setting domains inspired by Barker's ecological framework, generated through a four-stage pipeline (inputs, planning, generation, synthesis) with structural, schema, and semantic gates plus hand review (1,152 passed of 1,420 candidates), and rendered as 24-kHz mono PCM16 WAV with cloned voices, distance/listener-relative acoustics, and background beds. Evaluation covers 12 models under 15 configurations with capability metrics (APR with 95% Wilson intervals, ARS, tool execution) plus bystander-speech restraint metrics (BIR, PRR), LLM judging validated against humans, and ablations for transcript-vs-audio, speaker count, and overlay SNR. The strongest configuration passes all rubrics on only 66.8% of English and 54.5% of Mandarin cases (strongest open-weight: 34.0% and 19.3%), with multi-speaker memory weakest; transcript ablations and SNR/human studies locate distinct perceptual versus reasoning versus restraint bottlenecks.

## Problem & Motivation

Current voice assistants assume a one-to-one world in which every utterance is directed at the assistant and all instructions, preferences, and permissions belong to the same person, but real deployments in meetings, households, collaborative work, and shared channels such as Slack threads or Teams meetings violate that assumption on every turn. In a shared conversation the correct behavior depends jointly on who provided a piece of information, to whom it applies, who has authority to modify it, what may be disclosed to whom, and which of several speakers' constraints takes priority, as the paper's motivating vignette makes vivid: minutes later a daughter asks about an arriving package, and an agent that answers fluently has failed because it preserved neither the instruction's speaker, nor the person it protects, nor the disclosure boundary. Prior full-duplex and turn-taking benchmarks evaluate when and how an assistant manages the floor (interruption handling, backchannels, endpointing), and multi-speaker attribution benchmarks test speaker-attributed understanding or question answering, but neither tests whether attribution selects the correct next-assistant behavior. MSI-Bench therefore uses scripted, prerecorded multi-speaker audio ending in an assistant-directed handoff, avoiding the variability of a live simulator while enabling reproducible comparison across full-duplex and turn-based models, and scores each response with atomic rubrics that check grounding to the correct speaker, scope, authority, privacy boundary, and constraint priority.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Speaker-scoped interaction taxonomy with three capability families** — The paper defines multi-speaker memory (background speech retrieval: decisive facts occur only in overlapping background speech and must be recovered with source preserved or else admitted as missed; scope tracking: global constraints applying to the whole group versus local constraints applying to named individuals must be bound to holder without globalizing or localizing), multi-speaker instruction following (selective disclosure as audience design with per-listener knowledge states; speaker authority constraint as authorization tracking where an unauthorized participant requests an action controlled by another speaker through ownership, account, role, or permission), and multi-speaker reasoning (sequential constraint integration over interleaved requests and corrections; request ownership preserved through compressed handoffs without merging threads; constraint prioritization satisfying inviolable hard requirements such as deadlines, safety rules, medical needs, or physical impossibility before retaining compatible soft preferences).

2. **MSI-Bench structure with normalized outputs and atomic rubrics** — Each case stages a short multi-party multi-turn audio scene with participant identities, relationships, roles, and responsibilities so the answer depends on the full scene rather than the final wording, and requires an answer with optional tool calls scored by disjoint case-specific atomic criteria stating minimum correctness conditions plus a tool validator, with closed-domain arguments (enumerations, bounded numerics, canonical times/dates, stable Si speaker references by order of first appearance) and decoy functions to penalize leakage through the tool track.

3. **Four-stage data-generation pipeline with gated synthesis** — Inputs fix speaker count, language, topic, interaction pattern, scene, and acoustic assets; a planner LLM instantiates the cast (name, role, gender, age bucket, permission set) and storyline with primary goal and background bed under a structural gate; a lines-generation LLM expands the dialogue to an assistant-directed handoff while a tool-and-rubric LLM derives reference answers, function schemas, and gold calls under a schema type-check gate and a semantic verifier gating answer leakage and uniqueness; and Boson Higgs TTS 3 synthesis with voices cloned from human-validated Common Voice 17.0 clips, inline prosody tags, three listener-relative distances via gain, low-pass, and reverberation, ASR/energy gating against Higgs-audio-v3-stt, and mixing over Freesound beds with Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B timestamps and ESC-50/UrbanSound8K bursts cropped to loudest RMS windows, yielding 96 cloned voices, 8,849 base dialogue clips (23.15 h), 10,408 rendered artifacts, and 74 background assets.

4. **Perception-versus-reasoning-versus-restraint evaluation protocol** — Beyond APR/ARS/tool metrics, the protocol adds bystander-speech probes measuring restraint (whether the model speaks when no one addressed it), a nine-configuration speaker-labeled-transcript ablation isolating audio front-end failures from reasoning failures under constrained decoding controls, a balanced three- versus two-speaker contrast, a seven-level overlay-SNR remix ablation of background retrieval with hired human baselines and four-way behavior decomposition (capture, admit-miss, foreground-decoy, fabrication), and a Prolific human study validating the DeepSeek V4 Pro judge at 82.6% agreement.

## Key Findings

No system is reliable: across 12 models and 15 configurations the strongest per-split configuration passes all rubrics on only 66.8% of English and 54.5% of Mandarin cases, with the strongest open-weight configuration at 34.0% and 19.3%, and multi-speaker memory the weakest family, illustrated throughout the example cases by foreground-decoy grabs (ten-minute wing ETA mistaken for a 21:00 cutoff, pavilion_12 fabricated for pavilion_7), secret leaks (espresso-machine gift and Hangzhou itinerary disclosed), authority bypasses, swapped scope bindings (armchair and no-shellfish attached to the wrong speakers, peanut exclusion widened to the whole order), and flattened attribution (three owners' charging, stop, and podcast requests all bound to one speaker). Failure analysis separates two bottlenecks: open-weight models fail chiefly at perceiving who said what, with Gemma 4-12B and MiMo-Audio-7B gaining 21–44 APR points on transcripts and Gemma reaching 84.4–85.4% APR at hosted level, while hosted Gemini configurations gain at most 10.4 points and still fail 10–22% on clean speaker-labeled text, leaving residual reasoning failures including silence-as-consent execution on 28.6% of audio and 33.3% of transcript cases where approval was never given. Speaker count is not the primary bottleneck, with three- versus two-speaker relative ARS indistinguishable from zero within balanced cells (pooled −0.5 ARS, +1.5 APR points). Background recovery collapses with audibility while honesty diverges: Gemini 3.1 Pro capture falls from 62.5% at +8 dB to 15.6% at −8 dB versus humans at 98.5% to 15.5%, and as misses rise, GPT Realtime 2.1 admits 42–56% of misses at every level whereas Gemini 3.1 Pro admission falls from 44.4% to 6.2% and GPT Audio 1.5 from 23.3% to 10.5%, increasingly reporting the louder foreground fact (6.9% to 18.4%) rather than admitting the gap. The judge is statistically indistinguishable from an additional annotator at 82.6% agreement (Cohen's κ 0.63, 95% CI 0.57–0.69, Gwet's AC1 0.67, marginally stricter at 60.2% versus 61.9% human pass) and exceeds inter-annotator agreement (κ 0.56, 79.3%).

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The authors frame progress as requiring three largely independent axes — speaker-grounded audio perception, speaker-scoped decision making, and knowing when not to speak — and acknowledge that the current benchmark uses synthesized scripted speech that should be expanded with more languages, accents, spontaneous speech, and human recordings. They note the two bystander-speech metrics rest on negative cases only in which every injected utterance is task-irrelevant, so they measure restraint but not selective engagement, and propose adding pattern-specific positive cases where an interjection genuinely changes the task. The disclosure notes Claude was used for code completion, debugging, and refactoring with author review. Natural extensions from the analyses are targeted training on transcript-lifted perceptual gaps for open models, reasoning-focused fixes for frontier authorization, disclosure, and prioritization errors that persist on clean text, and calibration toward admitting missed background speech rather than fabricating louder foreground facts.
