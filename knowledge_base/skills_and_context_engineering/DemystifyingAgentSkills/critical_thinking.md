# Critical Analysis: Demystifying Agent Skills: Why They Work-Until They Don't

## Claims vs. evidence

**Claim 1 — Skills beat Workflow Memory built from the same trajectories (+6.06 pp).** Evidence is *strong* for this specific comparison: 528 matched triples, bootstrap CI [+0.76, +11.36] that excludes zero, and a controlled design that holds source experience fixed while varying representation. This is the paper's best-supported result precisely because it isolates the manipulated variable cleanly.

**Claim 2 — Skills work mainly through procedural anchoring (65.7%) rather than knowledge injection (4.5%).** Evidence is *suggestive but LLM-mediated*: the mechanism label itself is assigned by an LLM judge per triple, not independently human-validated at the mechanism-label level (only the taxonomy-construction stage — 238 labels, 12 modes — received human validation; the 1,584 arm-level mechanism/mode assignments over 528 triples did not). The category definitions are well-specified and the judge is evidence-quote-constrained, which mitigates but doesn't eliminate this gap.

**Claim 3 — Retrieval precision collapses with pool size (29.6%→3.3%), but downstream success stays flat (36.4%→39.3%).** Evidence is *strong* — this is a fairly mechanical measurement (precision/recall against native ground-truth annotations, not LLM judgment) run across a clean 5–100 pool-size sweep with three distractor regimes. This is arguably the paper's most robust and least LLM-dependent finding.

**Claim 4 — Skill vs Raw (as opposed to Skill vs Workflow Memory) shows a real effect (+2.84 pp).** Evidence is *weak*: the bootstrap CI for this comparison spans zero, and the paper is honest about this, correctly emphasizing Skill-vs-Workflow-Memory as the load-bearing comparison instead. Worth flagging because a less careful reader of secondary coverage could conflate "skills beat raw" with a robust finding when the paper itself does not claim that with the same confidence.

## Genuinely new vs. repackaged

The paper's genuinely new contribution is **methodological, not conceptual**: matched-triple contrastive analysis plus a validated mechanism taxonomy applied specifically to the skill-vs-workflow-memory-vs-raw question. The underlying idea that procedural memory helps agents is not new — the paper's own related-work section traces this through episodic-memory agents (Park et al. 2023; Shinn et al. 2023), structured external memory (Packer et al. 2024), and workflow-memory systems (Wang et al. 2024c; Fang et al. 2026) that summarize trajectories into action-level procedures. What's new is refusing to stop at aggregate task success and instead attributing gains/failures to specific pipeline stages (representation, retrieval, invocation) via a human-validated taxonomy — directly extending the critique the paper levels at SkillsBench (Li et al. 2026) and SWE-Skills-Bench (Han et al. 2026) for reporting only final task success.

Within the KB, this same "differential/contrastive attribution" idea appears independently in [[research/AgentSkillsCanBeHarmful/summary|Agent Skills Can Be Harmful]], published on essentially the same premise (pair a skill-guided run against a no-skill/reference run to attribute failure specifically to the skill) — suggesting contrastive attribution is becoming a convergent methodology in this space rather than a one-off invention of this paper.

## Weaknesses and blind spots

- **Benchmark scope is narrow and self-selected for procedural fragility.** Terminal-Bench, Terminal-Bench-Pro, and SkillsBench are all terminal/tool-use, multi-step execution tasks — exactly the setting where procedural anchoring should shine. The paper acknowledges (Limitations) that it doesn't cover long-horizon web interaction or open-ended collaboration, but doesn't quantify how much the headline numbers might shrink or invert in those settings.
- **Model generation drift is baked into RQ4.** Substituting GPT-5.4 for GPT-5.3-Codex mid-study (acknowledged) means RQ4 cannot be compared numerically to RQ1–RQ3, which somewhat limits the unified "lifecycle" narrative — the retrieval collapse and the representation-effect results technically come from partially non-overlapping model configurations, not one seamless pipeline.
- **The 3% open-coding sample is a genuine, acknowledged limitation** (240/8,135 records) — rare failure modes, especially ones concentrated in specific benchmark/setting combinations, could be entirely absent from the 12-mode taxonomy, silently folded into an adjacent mode instead.
- **The paper is silent on skill *authoring* cost/quality variance.** Every skill here is auto-generated by a fixed skill-creator prompt from a fixed trajectory pool — it says nothing about how much of the +6.06 pp gain depends on skill-creator prompt quality versus being an inherent property of the skill representation itself. A worse skill-creator prompt might erode or reverse the advantage.
- **Appendix B prompt bodies are missing from the extracted text** (a PDF-extraction artifact in this KB copy, not a paper defect) — this KB entry cannot reproduce the exact skill-creator or taxonomy-merge prompts verbatim, which limits how far a reader can replicate the taxonomy pipeline from this entry alone; the original PDF would need to be consulted directly for exact prompt wording.

## Applicability

This transfers well to any system that: (1) has agents perform multi-step, verifiable, tool-using tasks (coding, terminal ops, structured workflows); (2) already collects both successful and failed execution traces; (3) can afford to distill traces into a standardized procedural artifact rather than dumping raw logs into context. It is less clearly applicable to open-ended, conversational, or judgment-heavy tasks where "procedural anchoring" has less purchase (there's no fixed checklist to anchor to), or to domains where ground-truth verification is expensive or ambiguous (the paper leans heavily on oracle-status verifiers).

**Relevance to my work** (AI/ML engineering, agentic systems, Elisity data platform):
- If any Elisity or personal agentic-coding workflow is accumulating "how we solved this before" notes, this paper is direct evidence that *distilling* them into a compact SKILL.md-style artifact should outperform stuffing raw logs into context — worth testing with a small matched-triple experiment rather than assuming it.
- The retrieval-precision-collapse finding is a concrete warning for any skill/playbook library that grows past ~20-30 entries: expect retrieval to degrade sharply, especially among semantically similar playbooks, and design for "partial credit from a related skill" rather than assuming exact-match retrieval is required for the system to still work.
- The SC3 invocation-failure mode (skill followed too literally or misapplied) is a design signal: skills/playbooks should probably ship explicit applicability boundaries ("use this when X, do NOT use when Y") rather than pure step lists, to reduce the very failure mode this paper documents growing from ~1% to 10% of cases.
- The taxonomy-construction pattern (open-code a small sample → LLM-batch-merge into canonical categories → human-validate) is a reusable recipe for building any internal failure-mode taxonomy (e.g. for triaging fleet task failures) cheaply but with a credible reliability check.

## What this changes

If the claims hold broadly: teams building self-improving coding agents should prioritize a skill-distillation step over simply appending raw execution logs as memory, and should treat skill-library retrieval — not skill authoring — as the binding constraint once a library grows past a handful of entries. It also reframes "skill evaluation" away from single pass/fail leaderboards toward mechanism-level, trajectory-diffing analysis, which is a higher bar for anyone claiming a new skill/memory system "works." Second-order effect: skill-marketplace and skill-sharing platforms should expect retrieval quality, not skill quantity, to be their actual scaling bottleneck.

## Verdict

The paper's core comparisons (skill vs workflow memory; retrieval-precision collapse) are well-designed, honestly caveated where evidence is weaker (skill vs raw), and independently human-validated at the taxonomy-construction level, though the arm-level mechanism/mode labels applied at scale remain LLM-judged rather than human-checked. The narrow benchmark scope (terminal/tool-use tasks) and the acknowledged 3%-sample taxonomy induction mean the findings should be read as strong evidence for *this* class of agentic tasks, not a universal law of skill use. **trial** — worth running your own small matched-triple experiment against this paper's design before betting a production skill/memory architecture on its numbers, particularly the retrieval-precision-collapse finding, which is the most actionable and least LLM-dependent result.
