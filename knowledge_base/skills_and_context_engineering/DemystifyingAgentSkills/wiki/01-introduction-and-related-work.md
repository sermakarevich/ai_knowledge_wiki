# Introduction and Related Work

**In one sentence:** Existing skill evaluation only measures aggregate task success, so this work reframes the question from "whether skills work" to "when skills help, why they work, and where they fail" by contrasting matched executions with and without skills and attributing gains and failures to concrete mechanisms along the skill-use pipeline.

## Key points

- Skills are compact, standardized descriptions of what to do, what to check, and what pitfalls to avoid, and promise three advantages over raw traces or workflow memories: compressing noisy experience into shorter context, stabilizing format, and transferring knowledge across related tasks.
- Skills improve over Workflow Memory by 6.06 points in matched comparisons, with procedural anchoring accounting for 65.7% of skill cases versus 4.5% for explicit knowledge injection.
- The study normalizes 8,135 trial records, open-codes 240 sampled trajectories, and consolidates 238 valid unique labels into a taxonomy of three high-level categories and twelve skill-use modes.
- Retrieval is a separate bottleneck: as skill pools grow from 5 to 100, actual-use precision falls from 29.6% to 3.3%.
- Selecting the correct skill does not guarantee task success, and invoking a related non-ground-truth skill can still provide useful procedural support; confusable distractors impair offline identification yet downstream success remains stable.
- Skills primarily reduce execution-layer failures (environment-setup errors, output-format mismatches, service-lifecycle failures, shell-command corruption), whereas workflow memory preserves useful procedural evidence but also irrelevant exploration, failed branches, and verbose process noise.
- The work organizes itself along four research questions spanning representation, annotation, cross-framework transfer, and retrieval difficulty.
- Skill failure has a lifecycle structure: it happens when distilled guidance is noisy, over-specific, mismatched to the task context, or followed without adaptation.

## Motivation and the Gap in Existing Evaluation

The paper starts from the observation that LLM agents are increasingly expected to improve through experience rather than solve each task from scratch. Recent agent systems therefore store and reuse traces of prior execution — environment setup sequences, tool-use patterns, debugging routines, and verification steps discovered in earlier runs. This is especially appealing for tool-using agents (the introduction cites Yao et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2024), where repeated failures often arise not from a lack of high-level reasoning, but from rediscovering the same procedural details again and again.

Among the many proposed memory forms, skills (Anthropic, 2025) emerge as the most convincing abstraction. A skill is not a record of past execution, but a compact description of what to do, what to check, and what pitfalls to avoid. Compared to storing raw execution traces or direct workflow memories, the authors argue that skills carry three specific advantages: they can compress noisy experience into a shorter context, standardize procedural knowledge into a stable format, and potentially transfer that knowledge across related tasks.

The paper then identifies the gap it sets out to close: existing work studies skills only through aggregate task success. If a skill-augmented agent solves more tasks, the skill is declared useful. This does establish that skills matter, but it reveals almost nothing about why they matter — what fundamentally changes in an agent's behavior before and after a skill is loaded, which parts of execution are stabilized by skill guidance, and why the same skill can help one task while harming another. The authors argue this has left the field with a largely empirical view of skill design and improvement: skills are written, retrieved, and revised through heuristic iteration, prompt tuning, and benchmark-specific trial and error, rather than through a principled understanding of what makes a skill genuinely valuable.

To move beyond this, the authors reformulate the question from "whether skills work" to "when skills help, why they work, and where they fail." Their key contribution is a taxonomy of skill utility and failure, paired with a research methodology for making skill effects observable rather than treating the skill pipeline as a black box. At a high level, the method compares matched executions with and without skill access and analyzes where the two runs diverge across the skill-use pipeline: how prior experience is represented, how it is transferred across agent frameworks, how it is retrieved, and how it is invoked. This contrastive view lets the authors attribute gains and failures to concrete mechanisms instead of aggregate outcomes alone, and provides a more rigorous foundation for studying skill use and, ultimately, for automating the generation of reliably useful skills.

## Research Questions and Experimental Pipeline

The paper argues that skill utility cannot be understood from end-to-end success alone; it must be analyzed along the stages of the skill-use pipeline. The study is organized around four research questions: (1) whether representing the same prior experience as a standardized skill differs from injecting it as direct procedural memory; (2) whether skill gains come from the underlying experience itself or from explicit success/failure annotations; (3) whether distilled procedural guidance remains useful when transferred across agent frameworks; and (4) how skill-pool size and confusability affect retrieval and downstream execution.

The experimental apparatus behind these questions is sketched in the introduction's Figure 1. The top pipeline (skill versus procedural memory) executes each task in a fixed Docker environment, collects successful and failed trajectories, and forms a fixed-budget composition grid; the same trace pool is then distilled either into Workflow Memory or into a reusable SKILL.md, and both are evaluated on matched tasks under the same protocol. The bottom pipeline (skill retrieval) pairs each task with a candidate pool containing its ground-truth skill plus k − 1 real distractors (random, similar, or dissimilar). Matched pools are evaluated independently in three procedures: (A) embedding-based ranking without task execution; (B) explicit agent selection without Docker execution or verification; and (C) full-pool real execution with skill-use parsing after task verification. Crucially, outputs from (A) and (B) are not passed to (C), keeping the three retrieval procedures cleanly separated. The results previewed in the introduction show that procedural anchoring (65.7%) dominates over explicit knowledge injection (4.5%), that skills improve over Workflow Memory by 6.06 points in matched comparisons, and that as skill pools grow from 5 to 100 candidates, actual-use precision drops from 29.6% to 3.3% while downstream success remains relatively stable. The paper also sketches the second failure boundary: a useful skill may exist in the pool, yet the agent fails because the skill is retrieved for the wrong procedural context, invoked only superficially, or insufficient to overcome execution bottlenecks such as timeouts, numerical precision, missing dependencies, and brittle implementation requirements. The overall conclusion is a lifecycle view of skill use: skills work when prior experience is distilled into reusable procedural anchors and then retrieved and invoked in compatible contexts; they fail when the distilled guidance is noisy, over-specific, mismatched to the current task, or followed without adaptation. The authors frame this as the central takeaway of the chunk: skill-based self-improvement is not primarily a problem of accumulating more memories, but of building agents that can generate, retrieve, and apply procedural abstractions reliably.

## Related Work Positioning

The paper positions itself against two related-work literatures.

The first is memory reuse in LLM agents. Early systems stored and retrieved episodic experiences, reflections, or interaction histories to support future planning and decision-making (Park et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023). This line of work expanded to structured external memory (Packer et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025), retrieval-augmented experience reuse (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2026), procedural-memory management (Belikova et al., 2026), and task-agnostic memory graphs that compress episodic observations into knowledge-centric structures (Yang et al., 2026a). Recent surveys frame agent memory as a write-manage-read loop and identify open problems in consolidation, retrieval, contradiction handling, and evaluation (Zhang et al., 2024; Du, 2026). A related but distinct line of research shifts from remembering what happened to reusing how to act: workflow memories summarize past trajectories into action-level procedures for future tasks (Wang et al., 2024c; Fang et al., 2026), and skill-based systems in turn make procedural knowledge more explicit by storing reusable procedures as first-class artifacts — executable routines, scripts, skill directories, or hierarchical skill knowledge bases (Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026). Even more recent work studies skill activation and selection during execution (Xia et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026), as well as automatic skill discovery, revision, and evolution through experience (Mi et al., 2026; Alzubi et al., 2026; Lei et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026; He et al., 2026).

The introduction criticizes this body of work for still evaluating skills almost exclusively through aggregate task success. This obscures how skills differ from broader procedural memory, and why they help or fail in specific executions. The paper addresses this gap by comparing skills directly against procedural memory on the same prior-evidence pool and by attributing observed effects to specific stages of the skill pipeline — representation, retrieval, invocation, transfer, and abstraction. This is the core methodological contrast: where prior work treats a skill as an opaque input to the task, this work analyzes the skill's effect at each stage where it interacts with the agent's execution.

The second related-work thread is benchmarks for evaluating agent behavior. General tool-use and computer-use benchmarks evaluate multi-step decision-making, tool invocation, and environment interaction (Wang et al., 2024b; Xu et al., 2023; Mialon et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Koh et al., 2024; Drouin et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Rawles et al., 2025; Trivedi et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025). Software and terminal benchmarks (Jimenez et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a) are especially relevant here because they require long-horizon execution over files, tests, and environment state. The paper highlights Terminal-Bench (Merrill et al., 2026) in particular: its realistic command-line tasks, isolated environments, and test-based verification make it well suited for testing whether procedural guidance actually improves execution.

More recently, benchmark work has begun to test agent skills explicitly. SkillsBench (Li et al., 2026) measures structured skills across diverse tasks, and SWE-Skills-Bench (Han et al., 2026) studies the marginal utility of skill documents in real-world software-engineering settings. The introduction criticizes both as still primarily reporting final task success. In contrast, the current work extends the analysis to skill-based memory by connecting success and failure modes to specific memory decisions: which skill is retrieved, how it is interpreted, and whether its procedural abstraction matches the current task. This is the precise extension claim against the two skills benchmarks named by name: same task-level outcomes, but additionally tied to concrete retrieval and interpretation decisions.

Putting both threads together, the paper's positioning is that it contributes neither a new skill format nor a new benchmark, but a new unit of analysis. Memory-reuse papers establish that agents should reuse prior experience; agent benchmarks establish settings in which this can be measured; both leave the mechanism of how a skill helps or fails in a specific execution under-explained. The paper's claim is that mechanism-level analysis along pipeline stages — representation, annotation, transfer, retrieval, invocation — is what is needed to move from empirical skill design toward principled, automatable generation of reliably useful skills.
