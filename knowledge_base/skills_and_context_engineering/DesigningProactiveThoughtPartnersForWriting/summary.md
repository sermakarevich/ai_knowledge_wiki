# Designing Proactive Thought Partners for Writing
**Paper:** [Designing Proactive Thought Partners for Writing (Zhang, Davis, Chen, Hsu, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01588v1)
## Human Readable TL;DR
Think of a writing coach who only taps you on the shoulder when you told them it is okay to do so. Before you start, you tell the coach what kind of help you want and when to step in, for example to suggest examples when you pause. While you write, the coach stays quiet in the corner and fades away if you keep typing. If you are open to help, the coach shows a short question that sparks ideas or helps you check your own direction, and only writes text for you if you ask for it.
## TL;DR
The paper proposes proactive thought partners -- customizable AI partners that offer higher-level cognitive support during writing without waiting for explicit prompts. It builds a Next.js Markdown probe where writers configure each partner by role plus proactivity (event triggers and contextual heuristics), with a gemini-2.5-flash-lite decision engine that activates at most 2 partners per trigger. A 1-week exploratory deployment with 16 participants (minimum 4 substantive sessions) found prospective planning in setup, frequent ignoring to preserve flow, and use of opened suggestions for ideation and self-monitoring. Writers preferred lightweight peripheral visuals and non-directive acknowledgement-plus-question framing, leading to 4 design implication groups around customization, timing, engagement, and representation.
---
## Problem & Motivation
Writing is a shifting cognitive process that moves between planning ideas, turning ideas into text, and reviewing, with needs that differ by person and by moment. Existing tools split into two camps -- proactive-but-shallow help such as autocomplete and next-phrase suggestions for local text production, and rich-but-reactive help such as chatbots and menu tools for ideation, reflection, and revision that require explicit prompting. Higher-level work such as ideation, reflection, and revision depends on writer goals and changing context, so waiting for the writer to stop, prompt, and restart breaks flow and misses moments when help is most useful. The paper argues the missing quadrant is proactive plus cognitive support, mapped in Figure 1 on initiative (reactive vs proactive) by level of support (textual vs cognitive).
## Main Original Ideas
1. **Proactive thought partners concept** -- AI partners that take initiative to offer customizable higher-level cognitive support during writing without explicit prompting, placed in the proactive-plus-cognitive quadrant.
2. **Role plus proactivity customization (triggers vs heuristics)** -- writers define partner role (what kind of help) and timing (when to intervene), separating broad event triggers that mark candidate moments from contextual heuristics that specify precise conditions for intervening.
3. **Activation pipeline with max 2 partners and 15s keystroke window** -- rule-based triggers for Long Pause (5s inactivity), Sentence End (plus 1s idle), and Text Selection (plus 5s idle) send session goal, current text, cursor position, trigger event, 15s prior keystroke logs, and enabled partners to an LLM decision engine that selects at most 2 matching partners in under 1 second.
4. **Acknowledgement plus question suggestion format** -- each suggestion pairs a brief acknowledgement of what the writer just did or may try next with a thought-provoking question, designed to signal understanding while preserving ownership.
5. **Graduated engagement (ignore / inspire / execute)** -- writers can ignore by continuing to write while the tag fades after 15s, inspire by clicking to expand a suggestion card with optional follow-up chat, or execute via Help Me Write which inserts or revises text with accept/revert control.
## Key Findings
- Study scale: N=16 participants over 7 days with minimum 4 substantive sessions (at least 20 minutes active writing or at least 300 words plus a self-contained piece); 66 sessions total, mean 4.13 per person, 390.34 words and 17.09 minutes per session, across personal (19), academic (19), professional (9), creative (8), technical (8), and journalistic (3) writing; SUS mean 82.81 rated Excellent; daily satisfaction mean 8.16/10 rising over sessions (b = 0.33, p = .036).
- **Prospective planning in setup:** participants created 54 partners (mean 3.38 per person; 35 single-session and 19 multi-session) by goal-driven design from an imagined finished product or difficulty-driven design from anticipated blockers; 43 of 54 (79.63%) targeted cognitive support -- information seeking 14, argument development 12, critical reflection 10, ideation support 7 -- versus 11 (20.37%) for textual assistance.
- **Broad triggers with specific heuristics:** 31 of 54 partners (57.41%) enabled all three triggers (pause 74.07%, sentence end 74.07%, selection 81.48%), while heuristics encoded draft-state cues (64.81%), writing-activity cues (70.37%), and anticipated-need cues (66.67%).
- **Ignoring to preserve flow:** of 1,100 interventions, 58.27% ignored (641), 19.55% inspiring (215), 22.18% executing (244); pause tags most ignored (64.39%), sentence ends less so (55.93%), selections least (35.17%) but most executed (54.48%); writers described ignoring as low-cost continuation when they knew what to write next.
- **Ideation plus self-monitoring uses:** opened suggestions sparked new ideas (unexpected links described as eye openers that became the writer's own idea) and regulatory self-checks (noticing veering off track or one-sided arguments); execution acceptance was 84.43% (206/244), mainly to preview directions, phrase clear intent, or finish co-developed ideas, with hesitation for reflection partners and over-reliance concerns.
- **Lightweight non-directive preference:** helpfulness 8.11/10 and timeliness 7.85/10; writers valued push over pull when timing fit momentary need rather than event type alone, plus peripheral fading visuals (right-side small tags) and acknowledgement-plus-question framing that preserved control, except unknown-unknown cases needing direct answers.
## Suggestions & Future Directions
1. **Design customization as prospective planning:** help writers anticipate goals, difficulties, and assistance needs and translate them into specialized partners for information seeking, argument development, critical reflection, and ideation; support iterative testing and revision based on usage traces such as repeated ignoring or rare activation.
2. **Design timing as contextual alignment:** treat interaction events as candidate opportunities rather than sufficient evidence of need; infer opportunities from draft-state, writing-activity, and anticipated-need cues and reassess relevance close to delivery time.
3. **Design engagement as graduated commitment:** make ignoring a first-class response with no explicit dismissal, provide low-commitment inspire use (inspect, question, adapt without changing the draft), and reserve execution for settled intentions rather than the default for reflective nudges.
4. **Design representation as lightweight and non-directive:** use peripheral small floating tags that recede when not engaged, pair brief acknowledgements of inferred intent with question-based suggestions, and extend grounding to sentence-level and document-level views such as outlines and gap highlights.
5. **Privacy by design:** authors warn continuous monitoring of draft text, cursor position, events, and keystroke logs creates privacy risks; future systems need data minimization, local or ephemeral processing, retention limits, disclosure, pause and delete controls, and must not treat inferred states as definitive nor reuse traces for training, profiling, or identity inference without separate explicit consent.
6. **Limits and future work stated by authors:** probe explores a design space rather than testing causal effects on quality, productivity, learning, or long-term agency and ownership; sample of English-proficient experienced AI users with confidential work excluded limits generalizability, so recruit more diverse writers and high-stakes contexts; system reflects one model, prompt, and orchestration choice, so future models and benchmarks should better distinguish similar-event states and select appropriate support.
