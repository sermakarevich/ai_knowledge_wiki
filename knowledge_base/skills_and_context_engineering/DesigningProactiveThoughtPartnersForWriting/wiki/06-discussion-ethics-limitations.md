# Discussion, Ethics, Limitations, Conclusion
**In one sentence:** The authors translate findings into four design implications -- treat customization as prospective planning with iterative refinement, treat timing as contextual alignment rather than event reaction, treat engagement as graduated commitment from ignoring to inspiring to executing, and treat representation as lightweight peripheral non-directive framing -- while warning that continuous observation creates privacy risks, that a short probe with experienced English-speaking writers and a specific LLM (Large Language Model) implementation limits generalizability and causal claims, and concluding that effective proactivity depends on shapeable support, deferrable engagement, and subordinate presentation, not just when to intervene.
## Key points
- 7.1 customization is prospective planning: writers externalize anticipated goals, difficulties, and needed cognitive work before writing, so systems should help translate those expectations into specialized partners for information seeking, argument development, critical reflection, and ideation (DI1--DI2), and support iterative testing and revision based on usage traces such as repeated ignoring or rare activation.
- 7.2 timing is contextual alignment: pauses, sentence endings, and text selections are only candidate moments because the same event can mean block, reflection, completion, verification, or reconsideration, so systems should add an inference layer that combines draft-state cues, writing-activity cues, and anticipated-need cues and reassesses relevance close to delivery time (DI3--DI4).
- 7.3 engagement is graduated commitment: ignoring preserves flow and should require no explicit dismissal (DI5), opening suggestions for inspiration supports low-commitment inspection and dialogue without changing the draft (DI6), and direct execution should be a deliberate escalation reserved for settled intentions rather than the default for reflective suggestions (DI7).
- 7.4 representation is lightweight and non-directive: peripheral placement, small floating tags, and fading presence keep suggestions as receding bids for attention (DI8), while brief acknowledgments of inferred intent plus question-based framing make system interpretation visible without prescribing a response (DI9), extendable to sentence-level and document-level grounding such as outlines and gap highlights.
- Ethical considerations authors raise: timely support requires continuous monitoring of draft text, cursor position, behavioral events, and keystroke logs that can expose sensitive content and identity-revealing habits, so future systems need privacy-by-design -- data minimization, local or ephemeral processing, retention limits, disclosure, pause and delete controls -- and must not treat inferred cognitive states as definitive nor reuse traces for training, profiling, or identity inference without separate explicit consent.
- Limitations authors acknowledge: the probe explores a design space rather than testing causal effects on quality, productivity, learning, or long-term agency and ownership; the sample of English-proficient daily writers with prior AI (Artificial Intelligence) experience plus exclusion of confidential work limits generalizability; and current LLM limits, plus dependence on one model, prompt, and orchestration choice, mean contextual inference and suggestion quality may not transfer.
- Conclusion takeaway: a one-week deployment shows writers use configuration to plan ahead, engage through graduated commitment, and experience good timing as alignment with momentary intention, so future proactive systems for complex cognitive work must let writers shape support, defer or deepen engagement, and receive help through non-intrusive representations.

## 7.1 Designing Customization as Prospective Planning
Configuration externalized a situated model of forthcoming work: goals to achieve, difficulties anticipated, and kinds of cognitive support that might become useful. This extends prior work on customizable writing feedback after writing into before and during writing.
- DI1: support prospective planning by helping writers anticipate goals, difficulties, and assistance needs for an upcoming task and translate them into partner configurations.
- DI2: support specialized partners for distinct cognitive work rather than a general-purpose assistant, making it easy to assemble expertise around information seeking, argument development, critical reflection, and ideation.
- Because planning is imperfect, configuration should be iterative rather than one-time setup: preview likely interventions on a sample passage, simulate role and timing behavior across situations, and revise based on use.
- Example mechanism: surface patterns such as a partner being repeatedly ignored, rarely activated, or frequently followed up, then suggest refinements to role or scope while preserving writer authority over desired support.
## 7.2 Designing Timing as Contextual Alignment
Observable events should initiate assessment, not directly trigger action. Participants configured long pauses, sentence ends, and selections, yet the same event mapped to different states: a pause could mean block, focused reflection, or readiness; a sentence ending could mean completion, extension, verification, or reconsideration.
- DI3: treat interaction events as candidate opportunities for intervention rather than sufficient evidence of need.
- DI4: infer opportunities from multiple contextual cues covering what is in the draft, what the writer is doing, and what support may be needed next, aligning assistance with momentary intention before choosing whether and which partner intervenes.
- This complements interruption management literature and complicates systems that tie action directly to predefined events.
- Implementation view: use triggers to start contextual assessment, combine recent behavior with local and document-level content plus writer-defined goals, decide whether and which partner should act, and reassess near delivery time because an inferred need may expire after the writer moves on.
- Relevant theoretical grounding includes the recursive interplay of planning, translating (converting ideas into text), and reviewing in writing.
## 7.3 Designing Engagement as Graduated Commitment
Three levels were observed: ignoring, inspiring through inspection and dialogue, and executing through insertion or revision. This speaks to agency and ownership concerns around AI-generated text entering drafts.
- Ignoring was most common and functional: continuing to write naturally bypasses unneeded help. DI5 makes ignoring a first-class response requiring no explicit dismissal and no flow disruption.
- Inspiring: participants opened suggestions as prompts for idea generation and self-monitoring. DI6 provides low-commitment use as material for thinking -- inspect, question, elaborate, or adapt -- without immediately incorporating AI text into the draft.
- Executing: direct text changes fit moments of settled intention, such as a clear revision target or an idea developed through dialogue. Participants often did not want execution for reflection-oriented suggestions where the suggestion itself was enough. DI7 reserves generation for those moments and separates initiation of support from authority to modify the document.
- Future interfaces should support smooth escalation across levels so proactive AI takes initiative without assuming control over whether or how contributions enter the draft.
## 7.4 Designing Representation as Lightweight and Non-directive
Visual and rhetorical form made initiative feel optional and assessable. Peripheral placement, small floating tags, and fading presence kept suggestions available without competing with the main task. Acknowledgments built trust by showing local understanding; questioning preserved choice.
- DI8: represent suggestions as lightweight peripheral bids for attention that remain noticeable without occupying primary writing space and recede when not engaged.
- DI9: make contextual interpretation visible while preserving writer judgment -- brief acknowledgments communicate what the system believes the writer is doing, while question-based suggestions invite reflection without prescribing a response.
- Interruption cost depends on presentation as well as timing. Future grounding can operate at multiple document levels: local interventions reference the motivating sentence or passage, while argument-flow or section-level partners show broader structure through outlines, highlighted gaps, or cross-section references.
## Ethical considerations
Proactive support needs continuous observation of evolving text and interaction traces -- current draft, cursor position, behavioral events, recent keystroke logs -- which may expose sensitive content and distinctive habits that reveal identity. Telling participants to avoid personal, confidential, or highly sensitive input shifts responsibility to users and is unrealistic for everyday writing.
- Adopt privacy-by-design: data minimization, local or ephemeral processing of behavioral traces, strict retention limits, clear disclosure of what is monitored, and granular controls for pausing monitoring or deleting collected data.
- Avoid treating inferred cognitive states as definitive.
- Do not reuse writing traces for model training, profiling, or identity inference without separate, explicit consent.
## Limitations
- Probe scope: the study explores an emerging design space rather than evaluating a finished system, so findings do not establish causal effects on writing quality, productivity, learning, or long-term agency. Longer deployments and larger comparative quantitative studies against reactive assistants and conventional tools are needed.
- Ownership effects are preliminary: although writers controlled ignore, check, or execute choices, proactive interventions may still shape what writers notice, how they frame problems, and which directions they pursue, with greater consequences if reliance grows. Future work should study perceived authorship, confidence, skill development, and dependence over time.
- Sample: English-proficient daily writers across genres, all with prior AI writing tool experience, could compare proactivity with chatbots and autocomplete but may differ in expectations and comfort from less experienced or different-language writers; excluding confidential workplace content likely omitted high-stakes writing. Future work should recruit more demographically and linguistically diverse writers and cover professional high-stakes contexts.
- System specificity: current LLMs may not reliably infer momentary intentions or anticipated needs; draft-state, writing-activity, and anticipated-need cues are a starting point for future models and benchmarks that distinguish similar-event states and select appropriate support. Suggestion quality and timing also reflect one LLM, prompt, context representation, and orchestration choice, so results mix the broader paradigm with this implementation.
## Conclusion
The authors introduced proactive thought partners: AI writing assistants that proactively provide customizable higher-level cognitive support during writing. A one-week technology probe found writers used customization for prospective planning, engaged through graduated commitment, and experienced good timing as contextual alignment. Effective proactivity requires more than intervention timing: writers need ways to shape support types, ignore or deepen engagement as needed, and receive help through non-intrusive visual and rhetorical forms. The authors offer the design and insights as guidance for future proactive AI for complex cognitive work.
