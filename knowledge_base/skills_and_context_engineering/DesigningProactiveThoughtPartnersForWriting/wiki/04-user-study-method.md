# User Study: One-Week Probe Deployment
**In one sentence:** This was an exploratory technology-probe study, not an efficacy evaluation, with 16 participants completing onboarding plus a 7-day diary with a minimum of 4 substantive sessions plus an exit interview to answer RQ1-3 on partner creation, engagement, and experience.

## Key points
- The study used an exploratory technology-probe framing in the tradition of Hutchinson 2003, following recent examples such as Kaur, Seymour, and Lu, to study appropriation in real writing practice rather than to evaluate efficacy.
- RQ1 asked how writers create proactive thought partners, RQ2 asked how they engage with proactive interventions, and RQ3 asked how they experience proactive support including benefits, challenges, ownership, and agency.
- N=16 participants were recruited from an organizational mailing list of more than 10,000 recipients at a large global software company, with ages 26-53 and M=37.47, 11 identifying as female and 5 as male, and 5 native plus 11 non-native English speakers.
- Participants were everyday writers with prior AI-for-writing experience, including 10 daily and 6 weekly AI users in the prior three months, using tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Smart Compose for drafting, correspondence, and creative writing.
- Onboarding was a 60-minute remote session with a tutorial, four preconfigured partners -- Ideation Partner, Evidence Partner, Rebuttal Partner, and Logical Fallacy Partner -- plus a 300-word warm-up essay on positive and negative aspects of AI in daily life requiring creation of two custom partners.
- The diary phase lasted 7 days with a minimum of 4 substantive sessions, where substantive meant at least 20 minutes of active writing or at least 300 words plus a self-contained piece of writing, with an explicit instruction not to enter PII or confidential information.
- Analysis triangulated partner configurations, interaction and keystroke logs, binary thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback, structured diary entries with 10-point satisfaction, timeliness, and helpfulness scales, UMUX-LITE scores, and semi-structured interview transcripts coded by two researchers using thematic analysis.

## Study framing and RQs
The deployment was framed as an exploratory technology probe, not as a controlled efficacy evaluation. The goal was to observe how participants appropriate proactive thought partners in their own writing practices, what configuration and engagement strategies emerge, and what benefits and challenges they perceive. This follows the technology-probe tradition described by Hutchinson and colleagues, with recent related deployments by Kaur, Seymour, and Lu cited as models. Three research questions organized the study: RQ1 on partner creation -- how writers configure proactive thought partners; RQ2 on engagement -- how writers respond to proactive interventions during writing; and RQ3 on experience -- how writers perceive benefits, challenges, ownership, and agency when working with proactive support.

## Participants
Sixteen participants were recruited through an organizational mailing list with more than 10,000 recipients at a large global software company. Ages ranged from 26 to 53 years, with M=37.47 and SD=8.57, where one participant preferred not to disclose age; 11 identified as female and 5 as male. Because tasks required open-ended writing in English, proficiency in reading and writing English was required, with 5 native speakers and 11 non-native speakers. Recruitment did not stratify by professional writing expertise and instead sought everyday writers with regular writing practices, so backgrounds were diverse and included content strategy, communications, software engineering, program management, pricing, and operations roles. Participants were purposively sampled for prior AI-for-writing experience: during the three months before the study, 10 reported daily AI use and 6 reported weekly AI use. Reported tools included ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Smart Compose in Gmail, applied to technical and professional documentation such as daily reports, policy documents, and research papers, routine correspondence, and personal or creative writing such as fiction, journaling, and scripts, with AI used to brainstorm outlines, draft content, and edit for tone, clarity, and conciseness.

## Three-phase procedure
The study consisted of three phases: an onboarding session, a week-long diary study, and a concluding semi-structured interview. Before onboarding, participants provided informed consent and completed a demographics questionnaire; after the study each participant was compensated $140.

Phase 1 -- Onboarding, 60 minutes remote: participants received a short tutorial on the probe core features and were introduced to four preconfigured partners built around the warm-up task and informed by prior work on AI-assisted argumentative writing such as VISAR -- an Ideation Partner, an Evidence Partner, a Rebuttal Partner, and a Logical Fallacy Partner, with full configurations in supplementary materials. Participants then completed a warm-up writing task of approximately 300 words on the positive and negative aspects of AI in daily life, during which they created two custom thought partners and optionally enabled or updated preconfigured partners, then completed the essay with enabled partners to observe how proactive suggestions were triggered and to practice key interaction features. The session ended with instructions for the diary study.

Phase 2 -- Diary study, 7 days starting the next day: genre, topic, and purpose were unconstrained to capture appropriation in participants own practices, but participants were asked to complete at least four substantive writing sessions to ensure sufficient exposure. A substantive session required at least 20 minutes of active writing or production of at least 300 words, plus completion of a self-contained piece of writing rather than randomly connected sentences or artificial text written only for the study. To mitigate privacy risks from logging, participants were explicitly instructed not to enter personally identifiable information, confidential workplace information, or other highly sensitive data. During deployment the probe collected interaction logs including partner configurations, keystroke logs, and interactions with proactive suggestions, plus optional binary feedback per intervention through thumbs-up or thumbs-down icons. After each session participants submitted a structured diary entry with three 10-point scales for satisfaction, perceived timeliness, and perceived helpfulness, plus four open-ended prompts on partners configured and why, proactive support received and feedback on it, challenges encountered, and any other observations.

Phase 3 -- Semi-structured interview, 60 minutes: participants first completed the short-form Usability Metric for User Experience, UMUX-LITE. The interview then covered general week-long experience, comparison of the probe proactive features with reactive AI chatbots such as ChatGPT or Gemini and with proactive next-phrase suggestions such as Smart Compose in Gmail, and -- using diary entries, binary feedback logs, and system logs as elicitation artifacts -- strategies for configuring partners, specific helpful or intrusive suggestion instances, rationales for engagement choices, perceived benefits and challenges, and perceived ownership and agency. The discussion then moved beyond current prototype constraints to elicit visions of ideal future proactive writing assistants; the full protocol is in supplementary materials.

## Analysis approach
Analysis combined qualitative and quantitative sources: partner configurations, interaction logs, diary entries, and semi-structured interview transcripts. Interaction logs were used to characterize partner creation behaviors and engagement with proactive interventions, while diary entries and interviews provided insight into rationales, experiences, and perceptions. Two researchers conducted thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke: they reviewed interview transcripts and diary entries to identify recurring concepts, met to compare, discuss, and consolidate codes into a codebook, then collaboratively grouped related codes into higher-level themes addressing the research questions through multiple rounds of discussion. Throughout, interaction logs and quantitative signals were used alongside qualitative reflections to triangulate system behavior, immediate per-intervention feedback, and longer-form session reflections.
