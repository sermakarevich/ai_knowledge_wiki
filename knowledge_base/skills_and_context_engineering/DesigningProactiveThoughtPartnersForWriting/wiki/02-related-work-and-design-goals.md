# Related Work and Design Goals
**In one sentence:** The paper situates proactive thought partners across intelligent writing tools, proactive assistants, and customizable agents, then sets three design goals to guide a customizable probe for writing.
## Key points
- Flower and Hayes (1981) frame writing as recursive planning, translating ideas into text, and reviewing, which the paper uses to scope cognitive support beyond text production.
- Local-text tools such as autocomplete and grammar checkers give narrow proactive help on the immediate text, while LLM-based tools support broader ideation, reflection, and revision but usually stay reactive and require explicit prompts.
- Horvitz (1999) mixed-initiative interaction frames proactive assistance, and timing work ties interventions to observable events such as typing, pauses, edits, or saves, where the same event can mean different writing states.
- Yin et al. (2026) provide Wizard-of-Oz evidence that simulated proactive AI can boost creativity as inspiration rather than direct content provision, but leave open how to build, time, and customize such support in a functional system.
- Prior customization work including Benharrak et al. (2024), Lehmann et al. (2026), and Zheng et al. (2025) lets users shape AI role and behavior, and this paper adds proactivity itself as a new customization dimension.
- DG1 requires configurable role plus proactivity, so writers can define what kind of cognitive support they receive and when it should enter the writing process.
- DG2 requires flexible ignore, inspire, and execute engagement, so writers can dismiss, take inspiration from, or directly use proactive suggestions without breaking flow.
- DG3 requires a lightweight deployable editor probe, so the system can be used in everyday writing and studied outside the lab.

## 2.1 Intelligent writing tools
Flower and Hayes describe writing as planning, translating, and reviewing. Earlier intelligent tools focused mainly on producing, correcting, or refining text. Examples include autocomplete and predictive text for word- or phrase-level continuations, plus spelling, grammar, and style checkers for local errors and surface expression. These reduce production and revision effort but offer narrow assistance tied to the immediate text.
LLM-based systems expanded support to brainstorming ideas, generating outlines, drafting passages, rewriting text, providing feedback, and exploring alternatives across many writing contexts. This work shows AI can support higher-level activities such as ideation, reflection, and revision. Most of that richer support remains reactive because writers must leave the flow to prompt, select text, click buttons, or otherwise invoke the system. The paper identifies a gap between proactive but narrow text continuation or correction tools and cognitively broader but reactive LLM tools, and names the target space in that gap "proactive thought partners."
## 2.2 Proactive assistants and timing
Proactive assistants take initiative when assistance may be useful, building on mixed-initiative interaction where both user and system can lead toward a shared task (Horvitz, 1999). Recent work explores proactivity in programming, meetings, design, surveys, tool manipulation, recommendation, storytelling games, web search, problem-solving, and collaboration. The paper follows recent proactive AI work in defining proactivity as actively inferring user needs and offering suggestions without explicit prompting.
Timing is central to initiative. Existing writing assistants often cue action directly from interaction events, such as surfacing continuations while typing or bundling autonomous tasks with pauses, edits, or saves. The paper argues events alone are insufficient for higher-level support because one event can reflect different states: a pause may mean concentration, being stuck, or reviewing text. It therefore separates broad event triggers, which mark opportunities to check whether support may be relevant, from contextual heuristics, which specify more specific conditions for whether and which support should actually appear. Yin et al. (2026) is the closest prior writing study, using a human to simulate proactive suggestions during story writing and finding creativity benefits, but it does not resolve how to implement contextual interpretation, timing, or customization in a working system.
## 2.3 Customizable agents
HCI research on customizable agents studies how users align LLM systems with goals, preferences, and contexts across writing, emotional support, social chat, research ideation, and brainstorming. Customization lets users shape AI behavior, role, and responses, and can also serve as a probe method: what users choose to configure reveals latent needs and expectations. Writing is especially customization-sensitive because useful support depends on goals, genre, audience, voice, and process stage.
Prior writing examples include writer-defined personas for on-demand feedback, multiple user-defined agents and tasks for collaborative editing, layered interfaces for rhetorical strategy, and just-in-time objectives inferred from screenshots or page text to generate expert responses or situated tools. These help writers get diverse perspectives, delegate tasks, and align AI with communicative intent. Their limit is on-demand invocation: writers still manually trigger the agent. The contribution here is to make proactivity customizable, letting writers shape both the type of cognitive support and the moments when it enters the process.
## Design goals DG1-DG3
DG1 -- configurable role plus proactivity: writers define the partner's cognitive role and the timing conditions under which it should intervene, making both support type and entry moment explicit objects of configuration.
DG2 -- ignore, inspire, and execute engagement: writers can ignore suggestions and keep writing, use them as inspiration for their own thinking, or execute them directly in the draft, preserving agency and flow.
DG3 -- lightweight deployable editor: the probe is built as a familiar, low-friction writing environment suitable for everyday use and diary-study deployment, so configuration and experience can be studied in practice rather than only in the lab.
