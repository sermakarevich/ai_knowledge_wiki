# Findings: Customization, Engagement, Experience
**In one sentence:** participants prospectively planned partners, mostly ignored tags to protect flow but used opened suggestions for ideation and self-monitoring, and judged support by timing fit and lightweight non-directive framing.
## Key points
- Deployment scale and usability were strong: 16 participants completed 66 sessions (mean 4.13 per person, 390.34 words and 17.09 minutes per session) across personal (19), academic (19), professional (9), creative (8), technical (8), and journalistic (3) writing, with SUS mean 82.81 rated Excellent and daily satisfaction mean 8.16/10 rising over sessions (b = 0.33, p = .036).
- RQ1 showed prospective planning: participants created 54 partners (mean 3.38 per person; 35 single-session and 19 multi-session) by goal-driven design from an imagined finished product (P04) or difficulty-driven design from anticipated blockers (P13).
- RQ1 roles favored higher-level thinking over text polish: 43 of 54 partners (79.63%) targeted cognitive support -- information seeking 14, argument development 12, critical reflection 10, ideation support 7 -- versus 11 (20.37%) for textual assistance.
- RQ1 timing setup split labor between triggers and heuristics: 31 of 54 partners (57.41%) enabled all three triggers (pause 74.07%, sentence end 74.07%, selection 81.48%), while heuristics encoded specific relevance cues for draft state (64.81%), writing activity (70.37%), and anticipated need (66.67%).
- RQ2 showed graduated engagement with 1,100 interventions: 58.27% ignored (641), 19.55% inspiring (215), and 22.18% executing (244); pause interventions were most ignored (64.39%), sentence ends less so (55.93%), and selections least (35.17%) but most executed (54.48%).
- RQ2 uses were generative and regulatory, but execution was selective: opened suggestions sparked new ideas (P12 "eye opener", P01 database to memory leak became "my idea") and self-monitoring (P13 "veering off track", P04 "one side of the coin"), while executing reached 84.43% acceptance (206/244) mainly to preview directions (P03), phrase clear intent (P04, P11, P12), or finish co-developed ideas (P11), with hesitation for reflection partners and over-reliance concerns (P05, P06, P10).
- RQ3 experience valued push over pull when timing fit intentions and form stayed light: helpfulness 8.11/10 and timeliness 7.85/10, with P08 "don't know what question to ask" and P10 "push mechanism", good timing judged by momentary need rather than event type (P16, P12, P06), plus peripheral fading visuals (P10, P12, P13) and acknowledgment plus question framing that preserved control (P12 "same wavelength", P13 "not telling me what to do", P15 "everything under control"), except P08 unknown-unknowns cases needing direct answers.

## 6.1 Usage and usability
Across 16 participants, the study collected 66 writing sessions excluding onboarding, averaging 4.13 sessions per participant (SD = 0.50). Each session averaged 390.34 words (SD = 151.41) and 17.09 minutes (SD = 7.47). Sessions covered personal writing (N = 19), academic writing (N = 19), professional writing (N = 9), creative writing (N = 8), technical writing (N = 8), and journalistic writing (N = 3).

Usability was high. SUS scores from UMUX-LITE averaged 82.81 (SD = 11.97), in the Excellent category and about the 90th-95th percentile. Daily satisfaction averaged 8.16/10 (SD = 1.76). A linear mixed-effects model with participant as random effect showed satisfaction increased with session index (b = 0.33, SE = 0.16, p = .036), indicating growing satisfaction with experience.

## 6.2 RQ1 partner creation
Participants created 54 partners, mean 3.38 per participant (SD = 1.96). Of these, 35 (64.81%) were enabled in only one session and 19 (35.19%) across multiple sessions.

KF1: Creating partners by anticipating writing needs. Participants reasoned prospectively before writing. Goal-driven design started from the imagined finished piece and inferred required expertise, as P04 said: "I try to envision what the end product would look like and then, if I had a human writer, I imagine what that person's expertise should be." Difficulty-driven design started from known personal blockers, as P13 said: "I knew myself well enough to know what potential blockers I would have towards reaching that goal... where I anticipated myself struggling."

KF2: Partner roles clustered around distinct forms of cognitive support. Of 54 partners, 43 (79.63%) were for higher-level cognitive support and 11 (20.37%) for local textual assistance such as language and style. Breakdown: information seeking 14 (25.93%), argument development and reasoning 12 (22.22%), critical reflection and perspective-taking 10 (18.52%), and ideation and creative development 7 (12.96%). Examples included Research Partner, India Partner, Internal Monologue Partner, Ethical Partner, Synthesis Partner, and Corporate Speak Partner.

KF3: Partner proactivity is configured through broad event triggers and context-specific heuristics. Triggers were broad: 31 of 54 (57.41%) enabled all three triggers; pauses for 40 (74.07%), sentence endings for 40 (74.07%), and text selections for 44 (81.48%). Heuristics were more specific: draft-state cues about text as it stands, such as too-casual language or one-sided argument, in 35/54 (64.81%); writing-activity cues about current action, such as introducing a concept or moving toward conclusion, in 38/54 (70.37%); anticipated-need cues about next support needed, such as an example or stronger argument, in 36/54 (66.67%). The pattern suggests triggers specified when to look, while heuristics specified what to look for in the writer's state and goals.

RQ1 Takeaway: participants engaged in prospective planning, translating anticipated goals and difficulties into specialized roles and relevance conditions, with broad triggers as candidate moments and heuristics as specific expectations.

## 6.3 RQ2 engagement
The probe delivered 1,100 proactive interventions. Participants opened 41.73% (459/1,100). Engagement varied by trigger: Pause (660 total) -- Ignoring 425 (64.39%), Inspiring 155 (23.48%), Executing 80 (12.12%); Sentence (295 total) -- Ignoring 165 (55.93%), Inspiring 45 (15.25%), Executing 85 (28.81%); Selection (145 total) -- Ignoring 51 (35.17%), Inspiring 15 (10.34%), Executing 79 (54.48%); Total -- Ignoring 641 (58.27%), Inspiring 215 (19.55%), Executing 244 (22.18%). Executing required opening first and was coded only as Executing.

KF4: Suggestions supported both idea generation and self-monitoring. Generative use introduced unexpected connections: P12 called a link between Buddhist and Abrahamic concepts "an eye opener I hadn't thought about it", and P01 said a database to memory-leak link "became like a kind of my idea". Regulatory use helped alignment: P13 valued prompts when "veering off track", P06 valued "have you thought about it" prompts that prevented missing points, and P04 valued noticing "leaning on just one side of the coin".

KF5: Ignoring preserved writing flow and occurred more often after pauses. Ignoring without clicking was most common at 58.27% (641/1,100). Participants described it as low-cost continuation when they knew what to write next. P13 said: "There are times I did not click on the little bubble thing and I was like, 'No, I'm good.'" P05 said: "if I have time I will read it, or if I think I need it I will read it, and then it just helps in a more organic way." Pause-triggered tags were most ignored (64.39%), reflecting ambiguity: a pause can mean need but also focused concentration (P01).

KF6: Executing was selective and depended on readiness to externalize text. Executing was 22.18% (244/1,100), with 84.43% (206/244) of generated text accepted. Selection led to executing most (54.48%) versus pause (20.61%) and sentence end (28.81%). Three situations prompted execution: curiosity-driven preview before deciding (P03); translating clear intent into fluent phrasing when wording was hard (P04, P11, P12); and finalizing after short back-and-forth once ideas settled (P11). Role mattered: ideation partners allowed execution as shortcut (P06, P11), while reflection or bias-rebuttal partners prompted hesitation (P06, P10: "we just want one nudge and move on"). Participants warned that always offering execution could cause over-reliance and narrow exploration (P03, P05, P11); P05 preferred to "guide it to write deeper" and said always offering the button would "limit the creativity... the human part of it".

RQ2 Takeaway: engagement was graduated -- ignoring protected flow especially after pauses, opened suggestions aided ideation and self-monitoring, and executing fit moments with clear or developed direction especially after selection, less so for reflection-oriented nudges.

## 6.4 RQ3 experience
Overall impressions were positive. Daily ratings averaged 8.11/10 for helpfulness (SD = 1.87) and 7.85/10 for timeliness (SD = 1.86).

KF7: Proactivity shifted support from pulling to pushing. Chatbots required leaving composition, formulating prompts, and re-entering, which was hard when needs were vague. P08 said: "sometimes it's like I don't even know what question to ask." P15 called chatbot help fragmented spots ("here's a spot, there's a spot") versus proactive support as continuous "flow" and "waves". P10 named the difference: "it's a push mechanism rather than a pull mechanism... I don't need to again click and put the information in a different thing and ask."

KF8: Good timing depended on contextual alignment with writers' momentary intentions. Participants judged timing by fit to current intent, not by event type alone, and by their own heuristics. A pause could mean stuck and needing help or developing an idea; P16 described good timing as "this time I need a help", P12 pointed to "brain fade or writer's block mid-sentence", and P06 distinguished sentence ends as textual boundaries versus "I'm done with that, now I want to move on". Poor timing meant mismatch or missed chances, as P13 said: "Oh, come on. That was a good time for one to like pop in."

KF9: Peripheral and fading visual representations made proactive support feel non-intrusive. Right-side panel placement, small floating tags, and auto-dismissal kept support outside the main workspace. P10 noted suggestions "come on the right tab and not on the main writing screen... they're very small". P16 said: "it's in the set panel right so it's not blocked my main... if it's good then I look at that if not I just ignore." P13 valued "it's just a little icon and it's not opening this whole box". P12 said fading was "perfect because when I didn't want them, it kind of disappeared."

KF10: Acknowledgments built trust, while questions preserved sense of control. Opening acknowledgments signaled the partner understood context. P12 called it a "same wavelength" signal that gave "confidence that what it's going to propose next will be relevant". P15 said it helped check whether the partner understood intent. When direction was already clear, it could feel unnecessary (P07: "I know what I write; I just want to know what goes next"). Question framing was preferred over directives because it left choice: P13 said "it's a partner. it's not telling me what to do; it's just offering me suggestions", and P15 said questions gave "the impression that I have still everything under control and I can decide." The exception was unknown-unknowns: P08 said "You can tell me that I missed something, but you actually have to tell me what I missed because the reason why I didn't think about it is because I don't know about it", leading to follow-ups for examples or options.

RQ3 Takeaway: proactive support reduced help-seeking effort and fit writing flow, but only when aligned to momentary intent and easy to ignore; peripheral fading visuals reduced disruption, acknowledgments signaled understanding, and question framing preserved control.
