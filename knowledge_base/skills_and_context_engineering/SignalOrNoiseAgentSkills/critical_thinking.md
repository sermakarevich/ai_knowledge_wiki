# Critical Analysis: Signal or Noise? A Benchmark Study of Agent Skills in Web Development

## Claims vs. evidence

**Claim 1: Target Skill injection is net-harmful on average, across all four models.** Evidence is **strong** for the aggregate pattern — the effect is directionally consistent across four architecturally distinct models (closed frontier, open-weight, general vs. coding-specialized), with CIs excluding zero on three of four models for ∆Pass@2 and on all four for ∆Pass@1, and it is corroborated by a parallel drop in Task Completion Depth (not just a pass-rate artifact). This is a well-triangulated result.

**Claim 2: The harm splits into length distraction vs content misalignment mechanisms.** Evidence is **suggestive but not conclusive**. The C2 length-matched control is the paper's best methodological contribution, but the paper itself flags that only 109 unique C2 runs cover 117 pairs (some C2 prompts are shared across same-project pairs), so the decomposition has less independent statistical power than the headline framing implies. The mechanism labels ("length-distracted," "content-misled") are inferred from which term's CI excludes zero, not from a direct causal probe of attention or internal state — plausible, but indirect.

**Claim 3: Cross-model per-pair effects are near-uncorrelated (|r| ≤ 0.12).** Evidence is **strong** — this is a straightforward correlation computed over the full 117-pair set with a concrete worked counterexample (lowdb × database-optimizer, a 55 pp cross-model swing). It's hard to argue with the raw numbers, though the correlation itself inherits the pair-level noise discussed below.

**Claim 4: Anti-pattern rules are the one reliably positive, cost-effective SKILL.md slice.** Evidence is **suggestive, scope-limited**. This claim rests on just 5 (Skill, project) pairs selected specifically because they were positive-tail cases — the paper is explicit that the C3 gross effect (+5.1 pp) is opposite in sign to the panel average and "must not be read as evidence that Skills help on average." The finding is real within that scope but should not be generalized to "anti-pattern rules always help"; it's evidence about the composition of *already-helpful* Skills, not a universal prescription.

## Genuinely new vs. repackaged

The core novelty is methodological, not architectural: the workspace-aware injection protocol (only SKILL.md in-prompt, auxiliary files mounted to the filesystem) is what makes a clean byte-matched control tractable for real multi-file Skills — prior Skill benchmarks (SkillsBench, SWE-Skills-Bench) neither isolate WebDev nor use a length-matched control, so they cannot distinguish "the Skill's ideas are bad" from "any extra 5,000 characters would have hurt." The chain-position analysis (easy vs. hard task degradation) and the leave-one-out slice attribution are also new relative to prior Skill benchmarks. The underlying finding that long injected context can hurt performance is not new to LLM research generally (context-length/distraction effects are well documented elsewhere); this paper's contribution is applying that lens specifically and rigorously to Agent Skills in coding agents.

## Weaknesses and blind spots

- **Pair-level noise vs. headline precision.** The authors disclose that seed spread across Sonnet's three replicates (4.4 pp for C0, 3.6 pp for C1) is comparable in magnitude to the headline effect sizes (−1.3 to −4.2 pp). Individual pair-level numbers used throughout the results (e.g. the 55 pp lowdb swing, the −15 pp/+18 pp Appendix traces) should be read as illustrative rather than statistically precise, even though the paper leans on them narratively.
- **C2 measurement under-power.** Only 109 unique length-matched runs back 117 pairs due to shared C2 prompts on same-project pairs; the authors flag a cluster bootstrap and full per-pair deduplication as open follow-ups, meaning the length/content split — the paper's central mechanism — rests on a somewhat thinner sample than the pair count suggests.
- **Conservative routing leaves off-target deployment untested.** C1 is only evaluated on the 117 "core" (Skill, project) pairs that two expert annotators judged genuinely relevant. Real-world Skill deployment is not always this well-curated (auto-attached Skills, broad marketplace defaults); the paper has no data on what happens when a Skill is injected off-target, which is arguably the more common failure mode in practice.
- **Skill-set provenance is narrow.** All 31 Skills come from high-visibility public repositories (Anthropic, Mindrally, Osmani, Vercel Labs) — well-written, well-maintained examples by the standards of the ecosystem. Enterprise-internal Skills, auto-generated Skills, or Skills from a fine-tuned skill-router might show a different (possibly worse, possibly better) pattern; the paper is silent on this.
- **Functional-correctness-only metrics.** Web-Bench scores via deterministic Playwright tests. A Skill that improves code readability, accessibility, or design quality without changing whether the automated test passes would register as a pure loss in this benchmark — the paper acknowledges this gap explicitly but it means the "Skills mostly hurt" headline is scoped to functional pass rate, not overall code quality.
- **Pre-deployment, not online A/B.** The benchmark uses Web-Bench project tasks as a proxy for real work; there is no live user traffic, human developer intervention, or product-specific acceptance criteria in the loop.

**Relevance to my work:**
- For any Elisity or personal agentic-coding setup that auto-attaches framework/style Skills to every session, this is a direct argument to A/B the Skill against a length-matched no-op control before trusting it — don't assume a Skill helps just because it "sounds right."
- The chain-position finding (Skills hurt easy tasks, are neutral-to-positive on hard ones) suggests a cheap heuristic worth trying in fleet/coder-agent workflows: withhold Skills/playbooks on the first pass and inject them only after an initial failure, rather than by default.
- The anti-patterns-over-examples finding is directly actionable for authoring internal Skills or CLAUDE.md-style instruction files: prefer concise "don't do X" rules over long example code blocks, which are the most expensive and least reliably helpful slice.

## What this changes

If the claims hold broadly, the common practice of attaching a framework/style Skill at the start of every coding-agent session should shift to a conditional, evaluated practice: Skills become something you A/B-test per (model, project) before deploying, not a default productivity add-on. Marketplace-style Skill rankings become substantially less useful without per-model breakdowns. Teams authoring their own Skills get a concrete prioritization signal — invest in anti-pattern rules, be skeptical of example code, and consider trimming Skills to reduce both token cost and distraction risk. If the claims only partially hold (e.g. if the length/content split doesn't replicate outside WebDev), the core routing-decision framing and the chain-position heuristic likely still survive, since those rest on the more robustly-supported claims 1 and 3.

## Verdict

The benchmark's central methodological contribution — pairing a byte-matched irrelevant-Skill control with a leave-one-out slice ablation — is a real advance over prior Skill benchmarks and the headline finding (net-negative average effect, decorrelated across models) is well-supported by consistent, corroborated evidence across four models. The mechanism-level claims (length vs. content split, anti-pattern superiority) are more scope-limited than the paper's framing suggests, given disclosed seed-spread and C2 sample-size caveats. **Trial** — adopt the paper's evaluation methodology (length-matched control, per-model traces, chain-position breakdown) for any Skill/playbook deployment decision, but treat individual reported pair-level numbers as illustrative rather than as settled facts to build automation on directly. The single strongest reason: the core claim that Skill utility is a property of the (Skill, project, model) triple rather than an intrinsic property of the Skill is corroborated by a genuinely rigorous control design, and it directly contradicts widespread current practice of treating Skills as universally beneficial defaults.
