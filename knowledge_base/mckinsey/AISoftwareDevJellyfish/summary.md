# AI in software development: interview with Jellyfish CEO Andrew Lau

**Article:** [AI in software development: interview with Jellyfish CEO Andrew Lau](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-technology/our-insights/measuring-ai-in-software-development-interview-with-jellyfish-ceo-andrew-lau)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a factory assembly line where one worker suddenly works twice as fast but the next station cannot keep up, so finished goods still leave the factory at the same pace. That is what is happening with artificial intelligence coding assistants: writing code got much faster, but reviewing, testing, and releasing did not, so most companies see only modest gains. The Jellyfish chief executive argues the big payoff goes to firms that get nearly everyone using the tools, measure speed across the whole line rather than at one station, and retrain people so their main job becomes describing precisely what should be built instead of typing it out.

## TL;DR

McKinsey Senior Partner Martin Harrysson and Partner Prakhar Dixit interview Jellyfish chief executive and cofounder Andrew Lau on how artificial intelligence is reshaping software development and how its impact should be measured. Lau predicts the full development life cycle will be redefined within three years in a shift larger than agile or cloud, and grounds that claim in Jellyfish telemetry across 600-plus organizations. The core contribution is a three-layer measurement model covering adoption, throughput and process efficiency, and business outcomes, joined to evidence that deep organization-wide adoption delivers nonlinear gains and to a forecast that review, agentic coordination, and testing are the next tooling frontier.

---

## Problem & Motivation

Companies are investing heavily in artificial intelligence coding tools on the promise of large productivity gains, yet most observe only incremental improvement and lack a credible way to tell whether the tools are actually effective. Shallow, pocketed usage produces roughly a quarter more output while the rest of the delivery pipeline stays fixed, so coding speedups stall in review queues, test suites, and governance checks. Without a measurement approach that spans the whole product development life cycle and connects tool usage to business results, leaders cannot decide where to invest, what to change, or whether the transformation is working at all.

---

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Three-layer impact measurement.** Adoption comes first since unused tools change nothing, followed by throughput and process efficiency captured through flow metrics such as pull-request rate and cycle time, and finally genuine business outcomes such as road map progress, defect rates, and customer impact.
2. **Systems-thinking bottleneck dynamics.** The life cycle is treated as a single flow, so accelerating coding while review or compliance lags leaves total throughput unchanged and merely relocates the constraint to requirements, testing, or governance.
3. **Depth-driven nonlinear returns.** Broad but shallow use yields at least 25 percent gains across most firms, while 80 to 100 percent developer adoption more than doubles output, making adoption depth rather than tool choice the decisive variable.
4. **Three sequential transformation walls.** Organizations clear enablement and training first, then matching tools and models to each codebase, and finally the hardest wall of redefining roles, processes, and incentives, with younger firms bypassing that wall through natural hiring cycles.
5. **Spec-writing as the core human task.** As generative tools collapse implementation cost, describing what to build becomes harder than building it, so defining intent in the specification replaces code-writing as the scarce human capability.

## Key Findings

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Baseline adoption payoff | 600-plus tracked organizations, over 60 percent see at least 25 percent productivity improvement; judged progress but not a breakthrough |
| Depth payoff | Joint Jellyfish–OpenAI research: 80–100 percent developer adoption associated with gains above 110 percent |
| First tooling wave | Progress concentrated in editor-based assistants for individual developers writing code |
| Next tooling wave | Code review and agentic systems coordinating multi-stage life-cycle work; testing as the hardest frontier |
| Testing blocker | Full quality automation requires defining truth, which many legacy systems lack, forcing a redefinition of how correctness is expressed |
| Regulatory open question | Whether an agent can count as an independent validator is unresolved; controls must move earlier in the process meanwhile |
| Role inversion | Clarity, systems thinking, and guiding artificial intelligence toward the right outcomes replace raw implementation speed as the rewarded capabilities |

- Faster coding without pipeline-wide change creates downstream friction rather than faster delivery.
- Older and larger firms absorb the role-redesign friction that newer firms avoid through hiring-cycle evolution.
- The durable organizational needs are diagnostics, enablement, role redefinition, and change management.

---

## Suggestions & Future Directions

1. Instrument the entire pipeline with telemetry so emerging bottlenecks in review, testing, and governance become visible as coding accelerates.
2. Drive adoption to genuine organization-wide depth through training and cultural nudges before judging tool effectiveness, since shallow trials understate the payoff.
3. Match models and tools to each codebase and team instead of standardizing on a single assistant, treating fit as a distinct transformation phase.
4. Rebuild roles, processes, and incentives around specification quality and flow outcomes rather than individual coding output.
5. Develop formal ways to express intent and correctness so testing can be automated even for legacy systems lacking specifications.
6. Resolve, with regulators and practitioners, what constitutes an independent validator when agents participate in assurance, and embed controls earlier until that question is settled.
