# Adoption data and the three phases

**In one sentence:** Across 600-plus tracked organizations more than 60 percent see at least a 25 percent productivity improvement from AI, but Lau argues true impact only arrives with deep organization-wide adoption — 80 to 100 percent adoption delivered gains above 110 percent in joint research with OpenAI — through three phases of enablement, tool fit, and role and process redesign.

## Key points

- Across the 600-plus organizations Jellyfish tracks, more than 60 percent see at least a 25 percent productivity improvement from AI, a figure Lau calls progress but not a breakthrough.
- The 60-percent figure is sourced to Jellyfish's 2025 State of Engineering Management report, specifically Gail Axelrod's July 8, 2025 write-up on engineering in the age of AI.
- True impact comes only when adoption is deep and organization-wide: joint Jellyfish–OpenAI research found companies with 80 to 100 percent developer adoption saw gains of more than 110 percent.
- The OpenAI finding is sourced to Nicholas Arcolano's August 13, 2025 piece on Jellyfish teaming up with OpenAI to measure AI coding-tool impact.
- Transformation runs through three walls in order: enablement, training, and sometimes cultural nudges first; then finding the right tools and models that fit each codebase and team; hardest of all, redefining roles, processes, and incentives.
- Newer companies escape the hardest phase because they are not bound by legacy structures and can evolve roles naturally, with team ratios and responsibilities changing over hiring cycles rather than through sudden reorganizations, while older and larger companies absorb that friction directly.

---

## The 60-percent baseline

Lau's "reality bears this out" passage grounds the transformation thesis in Jellyfish telemetry: across 600-plus organizations, more than 60 percent see at least a 25 percent productivity improvement. The deliberate deflation — "that is progress, but not a breakthrough" — reframes shallow gains as the expected outcome of shallow adoption and motivates the depth argument that follows.

## The depth effect: 80 to 100 percent adoption, over 110 percent gains

The contrast number is the interview's most striking statistic: companies with 80 to 100 percent developer adoption saw gains of more than 110 percent in the joint research with OpenAI. The implied shape is nonlinear — middling adoption buys a quarter of extra output while near-total adoption more than doubles it — which is why Lau insists impact must be deep and organization-wide rather than concentrated in enthusiastic pockets.

## Three walls in order

Lau sequences transformation as three successive barriers: first adoption through enablement, training, and cultural nudges; second fit, meaning the right tools and models matched to each codebase and team; third and hardest, the redefinition of roles, processes, and incentives. The ordering is a practical diagnostic — an organization stuck at low gains can locate itself at the first wall it has not cleared.

## Why incumbents suffer most

The asymmetry Lau draws is structural, not motivational: newer companies without legacy structures evolve roles naturally through hiring cycles, while older and larger ones must push through explicit role, process, and incentive redesign. Team ratios and responsibilities changing over time through hiring is presented as the low-friction path incumbents cannot easily take.
