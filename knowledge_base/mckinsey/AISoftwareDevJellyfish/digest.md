# Digest — AI in software development: interview with Jellyfish CEO Andrew Lau

## Journey and the transformation thesis

**In one sentence:** Engineer-turned-founder Andrew Lau, who cofounded Jellyfish in 2017, argues that artificial intelligence (AI) is the biggest transformation in software development since the invention of the internet and will completely redefine the software development life cycle within three years.

- Andrew Lau describes himself as an engineer by training who caught the entrepreneurial bug about 15 years ago and, after founding and working at a few companies, cofounded Jellyfish in 2017.
- Lau states plainly that with AI in software development the company is seeing another tailwind, and that he fundamentally believes the software development life cycle will be completely redefined within three years, which he calls exciting and scary for the industry.
- He frames the change as one of the biggest transformations since the invention of the internet, explicitly larger than the earlier agile and cloud transitions.
- The 20-year stable pattern he says is ending runs from requirement generation and issue tracking through integrated development environment (IDE) development, source control, continuous integration and continuous development (CI/CD), testing, deployment, and post-deployment observation.
- The shift is human as much as technical because roles, skills, and collaboration models will all evolve, redefining what people actually do rather than merely speeding up existing tasks.
- The interview was conducted by McKinsey Senior Partner Martin Harrysson of the Bay Area office and Partner Prakhar Dixit of the Seattle office, published December 10, 2025, and edited for length and clarity.

## Adoption data and the three phases

**In one sentence:** Across 600-plus tracked organizations more than 60 percent see at least a 25 percent productivity improvement from AI, but Lau argues true impact only arrives with deep organization-wide adoption — 80 to 100 percent adoption delivered gains above 110 percent in joint research with OpenAI — through three phases of enablement, tool fit, and role and process redesign.

- Across the 600-plus organizations Jellyfish tracks, more than 60 percent see at least a 25 percent productivity improvement from AI, a figure Lau calls progress but not a breakthrough.
- The 60-percent figure is sourced to Jellyfish's 2025 State of Engineering Management report, specifically Gail Axelrod's July 8, 2025 write-up on engineering in the age of AI.
- True impact comes only when adoption is deep and organization-wide: joint Jellyfish–OpenAI research found companies with 80 to 100 percent developer adoption saw gains of more than 110 percent.
- The OpenAI finding is sourced to Nicholas Arcolano's August 13, 2025 piece on Jellyfish teaming up with OpenAI to measure AI coding-tool impact.
- Transformation runs through three walls in order: enablement, training, and sometimes cultural nudges first; then finding the right tools and models that fit each codebase and team; hardest of all, redefining roles, processes, and incentives.
- Newer companies escape the hardest phase because they are not bound by legacy structures and can evolve roles naturally, with team ratios and responsibilities changing over hiring cycles rather than through sudden reorganizations, while older and larger companies absorb that friction directly.

## The three-layer measurement model

**In one sentence:** Lau measures AI transformation in three layers — adoption first, then throughput and process efficiency, then business outcomes — held together by systems thinking that treats the product development life cycle (PDLC) as one flow where a local speedup without pipeline telemetry merely moves the bottleneck.

- Layer one is adoption, on the logic that if people are not using the tools transformation cannot happen, tracked both quantitatively and qualitatively.
- Layer two is throughput and process efficiency, using metrics such as pull-request rate, cycle time, and latency, which Lau admits are not perfect but provide a systems-level view.
- Layer three is outcomes, meaning whether business objectives are met, exemplified by road map progress, defect rates, and customer impact.
- The governing metaphor is systems thinking: the PDLC is a flow, so accelerating one stage such as coding while review or compliance lags means the organization has not truly improved.
- Telemetry across the whole pipeline is essential precisely to reveal where bottlenecks appear so they can eventually be fixed, rather than assuming coding speed equals delivery speed.
- Faster coding actively shifts bottlenecks elsewhere — requirements definition, testing, and governance are named — so without holistic change, gains at one point create friction downstream.

## The next wave of AI tooling

**In one sentence:** With integrated development environment (IDE) coding assistance largely won, Lau places the next innovation wave in code review and agentic systems spanning the life cycle, with testing as the hardest frontier because full automation requires formalizing intent and redefining correctness — an open question for regulated industries.

- Most progress so far sits in IDE-based tools and experiences for individual developers writing code, which Lau treats as the completed first wave rather than the frontier.
- The next wave is code review plus agentic systems that coordinate work across stages of the PDLC, moving assistance from single-developer tasks to multi-stage coordination.
- Testing is singled out as a particularly interesting frontier because quality cannot be fully automated without defining truth, and many legacy systems lack that specification.
- The missing-specification problem is a blocker for full-scale AI transformation: organizations will need new ways to formalize intent, essentially redefining how correctness is expressed.
- Regulated industries sharpen the problem because they must prove independence and accuracy, raising the unresolved question of whether an agent can count as an independent validator — Lau's answer is that we are not there yet.
- Until validators exist, organizations must embed controls earlier in the process rather than relying on end-of-pipe checking.

## Human and AI roles and the closing prescription

**In one sentence:** Because generative tools make writing code easy, Lau argues the core human task becomes defining intent in the spec, rewarding clarity and systems thinking — and organizations that want this shift need diagnostics, enablement, role redefinition, and change management.

- Lau inverts the historic assumption directly: for decades coding was treated as the hard part, but describing what to build turns out to be harder.
- Generative tools making code-writing easy promote intent-definition — the spec — to the core human task in the development process.
- The shift changes who adds value by rewarding clarity, systems thinking, and the ability to guide AI toward the right outcomes rather than raw implementation speed.
- This reframes hiring and development: specification skill, precision of expression, and architectural judgment become the scarce capabilities instead of syntax fluency.
- The interview's closing prescription bundles four organizational needs — diagnostics, enablement, role redefinition, and change management — as what teams require to succeed. - Taken together, the role inversion explains why the third transformation wall is hardest: redefining roles, processes, and incentives is not adoption overhead but the actual substance of the change.

## The argument in five moves

1. The whole software development life cycle will be redefined within three years in a shift bigger than agile or cloud.
2. Shallow adoption buys modest gains (over 60 percent of firms see at least 25 percent), while deep adoption more than doubles output (over 110 percent at 80–100 percent adoption).
3. Transformation clears three walls in order — enablement, tool fit, then the hardest: roles, processes, and incentives.
4. Measurement must therefore track adoption, then pipeline throughput, then business outcomes, treating the life cycle as one flow.
5. With code-writing automated, humans own the spec and organizations must rebuild roles and controls around that inversion.
