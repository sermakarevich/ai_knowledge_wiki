# From technically possible to operationally viable

**In one sentence:** The article frames 2026 as the year the enterprise artificial intelligence (AI) conversation shifts from what AI can demo to what AI can run reliably at scale, making execution rather than experimentation the competitive differentiator.

## Key points

- The January 2026 framing piece declares that discussion inside organisations is shifting decisively from what is technically possible to what is operationally viable at scale.
- Leaders are moving beyond isolated pilots to confront governance, trust, and end-to-end integration, which reframes AI as an operating challenge rather than a technology showcase.
- The article's closing synthesis names execution as the clear 2026 differentiator, with advantage going to sustained investment in fundamentals over incremental experimentation.
- Organisations that win will hard-wire AI into redesigned processes, build confidence through robust governance and controls, and invest in human and technical foundations.
- The piece is a compiled outlook, not original research: seven McKinsey technical leaders contribute one perspective each, assembled by Rohit Rathod, a Senior Client Development Advisor in the London office.
- Its target reader is the UK enterprise leader deciding where 2026 AI budgets go, and its consistent message is to fund foundations and workflow redesign before new model spending.

---

## The shift in the question being asked

The opening paragraph sets the whole article's terms: "The focus of discussions around AI is shifting decisively within organisations — from what is technically possible, to what is operationally viable at scale." Possibility was the 2023–2025 question, answered by demos and pilots; viability is the 2026 question, answered by reliability, cost, and control. The sentence matters because every leader quote that follows is an instance of it: each describes one thing that must change for AI to survive contact with production operations.

## Beyond isolated pilots

The dek makes the same point at the level of practice: "Leaders are moving beyond isolated pilots to confront more complex challenges around governance, trust, and end-to-end integration." The three nouns are the article's table of contents in miniature — governance maps to the Sukharevsky and Walsh perspectives, trust to Kerr, and end-to-end integration to Patnaik and Sagar-Jones. Naming all three up front tells the reader that pilot success no longer counts as progress unless it survives these three confrontations.

## Execution as the differentiator

The closing synthesis converts the seven perspectives into a single verdict: "these perspectives point to a clear differentiator in 2026: execution. Competitive advantage will depend on a sustained investment in fundamentals rather than incremental experimentation." The contrast is deliberate — sustained versus incremental, fundamentals versus experimentation — and it is the article's instruction to budget-holders: stop funding one more pilot and start funding the foundations that let pilots graduate. The three winning moves named are hard-wiring AI into redesigned processes, building confidence through governance and controls, and investing in human and technical foundations.
