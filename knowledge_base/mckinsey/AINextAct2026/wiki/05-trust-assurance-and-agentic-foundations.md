# Trust, assurance, and agentic foundations

**In one sentence:** Dave Kerr and Rory Walsh argue that 2026 confidence in AI will be built through layered trust-and-assurance practices and serious investment in evaluations, observability, and agent identity management, because only provably reliable systems earn deployment in real operations.

## Key points

- Kerr, a Partner leading Agentic deployments, says the defining question shifts from "can it work?" to "can we trust it, consistently, in the real world?"
- Real confidence comes through trust and assurance, with wider deployment of autonomous systems gated on proof of reliability, consistent behavior, and strong preventive controls.
- He demotes the fashionable large language model (LLM)-as-judge technique to one tool inside a broader regime combining deterministic tests, statistical measures of accuracy, and robust safeguards.
- Walsh, a Partner in QuantumBlack Labs, predicts enterprises will tackle the foundational enablers for building, governing, and operating Agentic workflows under business, security, and regulatory requirements.
- Moving beyond proofs of concept and single deployments will require considerable investment, ending the era when agentic progress looked cheap.
- Walsh names three concrete foundation topics: at-scale evaluations, agentic observability across first- and third-party platforms, and Identity and Access Management for agents.

---

## From "can it work" to "can we trust it"

Kerr's pivot sentence reframes the entire field's success criterion: "The focus will shift from 'can it work?' to 'can we trust it, consistently, in the real world?'" Three qualifiers carry the argument — trust rather than capability, consistency rather than peak performance, the real world rather than the demo environment. Deployment follows proof: "More autonomous AI systems will start being used widely in real business operations once organisations can prove they are reliable, behave consistently, and have strong controls in place to prevent problems."

## LLM-as-judge belongs inside a broader regime

Kerr positions the popular evaluation shortcut carefully: "LLM-as-judge is a useful tool, but it should sit within a broader approach that combines deterministic tests, statistical measures of accuracy, and robust safeguards." The three layers cover different failure modes — deterministic tests catch known regressions exactly, statistical measures quantify accuracy distributions rather than anecdotes, and safeguards prevent harm when both miss something. A judge model alone can be gamed, drift, or share its subject's blind spots, which is why it must be one instrument among several.

## The foundational enablers enterprises must now build

Walsh describes the year's infrastructure agenda: "enterprises tackling the foundational enablers required to build, govern, and operate Agentic workflows in a way that meets business needs, security obligations and regulatory requirements." The build-govern-operate triad matters because most 2025 work stopped at build; governing and operating agent fleets under regulatory scrutiny is the unfamiliar, expensive part. His warning that "moving beyond proof of concepts and single deployments will require considerable investment" tells budget-holders the cheap-experiment phase is over.

## Three concrete foundation topics

Walsh's agenda is specific enough to plan against: "at-scale evaluations, agentic observability across 1st and 3rd party agentic platforms and Identity and Access Management for agents." At-scale evaluations mean testing agent behavior across volumes and edge cases, not single golden paths. Observability across first- and third-party platforms means tracing what agents did even when the agent runs on someone else's platform. Identity and Access Management for agents means every agent has an identity, permissions, and an audit trail — the enterprise security model extended to nonhuman workers.
