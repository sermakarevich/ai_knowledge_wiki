# AI's next act — Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before re-reading. One or more questions per digest section.

## 1. From technically possible to operationally viable

**Q1 (recall):** What is the article's framing shift for 2026, in the leaders' own terms, and what three challenges replace isolated pilots?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The discussion shifts from what is technically possible to what is operationally viable at scale. Leaders move beyond isolated pilots to confront governance, trust, and end-to-end integration.
</details>

**Q2 (why / breaks):** Why does the article claim execution beats experimentation as the 2026 differentiator — what breaks if a company keeps funding pilots?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Because advantage goes to sustained investment in fundamentals over incremental experimentation. A company stuck on pilots never hard-wires AI into redesigned processes or builds governance and human foundations, so nothing compounds into performance gains while rivals pull ahead.
</details>

## 2. Autonomy shrinks by design

**Q3 (recall):** State Patnaik's "chats to settles" distinction and the three coordination items he says hold the real productivity lift.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Enterprises move from "AI that chats" to "AI that settles", closing tickets and clearing exceptions end to end with audit trails. The productivity lift comes from automating coordination: handoffs, approvals, escalation — not just tasks.
</details>

**Q4 (why / breaks):** Why would granting agents maximum autonomy backfire, and what operating envelope does Patnaik prescribe instead?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Unbounded autonomy cannot be trusted, governed, or scaled because actions are unverifiable. The prescribed envelope is controlled environments with verifiable actions and hard guardrails, with business value correlating to workflow redesign rather than model spend.
</details>

## 3. Boards own outcomes as pilots convert to production

**Q5 (recall):** What ownership change does Smaje report, and what three ingredients must sit under production at scale?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Boards and C-suite teams step up to own end-to-end outcomes rather than delegating experiments and individual use cases. Production at scale needs evaluation, controls, and the "humanware" on top that makes impact material and repeatable.
</details>

**Q6 (transfer):** Your board asks why last year's successful chatbot pilot did not change any business metric. Using Smaje, how do you answer?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
A pilot proves possibility, not viability: without board-owned end-to-end outcomes, plus evaluation, controls, and humanware (roles, training, adoption), the result stays a one-off experiment that cannot repeat or compound across the organisation.
</details>

## 4. Fundamentals and the audit moment

**Q7 (recall):** List Sukharevsky's five fundamentals and state what fails programmes at the audit moment.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Modern IT architecture, high-quality data, capabilities, operating model, and change management. Programmes fall short not because models underperform but because enablers and economics were never in place.
</details>

**Q8 (why / breaks):** Why does the native-versus-traditional gap widen even when both sides buy the same models?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Because natives already possess the fundamentals, so each model generation converts into value immediately, while traditional firms stall on legacy architecture, fragmented data, and missing change capacity — shifting competitive dynamics toward secure, ethical scaling built on digital trust.
</details>

## 5. Trust, assurance, and agentic foundations

**Q9 (recall):** What question replaces "can it work?", and what three layers must surround LLM-as-judge?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
"Can we trust it, consistently, in the real world?" LLM-as-judge must sit within deterministic tests, statistical measures of accuracy, and robust safeguards.
</details>

**Q10 (recall):** Name Walsh's three foundation topics for operating Agentic workflows.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
At-scale evaluations, agentic observability across first- and third-party agentic platforms, and Identity and Access Management for agents.
</details>

## 6. The Agentic OS and competitive wake-up calls

**Q11 (recall):** Describe the Agentic OS in Sagar-Jones's three verbs plus its escalation rule.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
An operating layer that anticipates needs, responds to events, and coordinates across systems by deploying the right agents autonomously in the background of everyday work, escalating to humans only when necessary.
</details>

**Q12 (transfer):** A rival in your industry just announced a step-change in service speed from scaled agentic workflows. What does Champagne predict happens next, and what should your company do Monday morning?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Champagne predicts the visible advantage jolts everyone else from experimentation to production. Monday's move: treat it as a wake-up call — accelerate the pilot-to-production conversion with narrowed autonomy, layered assurance, and funded foundations rather than launching another experiment.
</details>
