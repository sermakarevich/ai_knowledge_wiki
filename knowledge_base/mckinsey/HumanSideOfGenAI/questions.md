# Retrieval practice — The human side of generative AI

## Workforce archetypes (Page 1)

**Q1.** What share of gen-AI-involved workers hold tech-heavy roles versus nontechnical jobs, and what does that imply for adoption strategy?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Only 12 percent are technical (data scientists, engineers, machine learning specialists); 88 percent are nontechnical (managers, educators, healthcare workers, administrators). Adoption strategy must therefore target ordinary workers across the enterprise, not just hire technical specialists.
</details>

**Q2.** Name the four worker archetypes and their approximate shares of the employee sample.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Creators (~2 percent) build models and interfaces; heavy users (8 percent) use gen AI for most core tasks; light users (~18 percent) use it for fewer than half their tasks; nonusers (~70 percent) are unaffected or unaware — though most are expected to become users.
</details>

**Q3.** Why does the expectation that most nonusers will become users change how leaders should read the 70-percent figure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because 70 percent is a snapshot of early diffusion, not a stable segment: if the majority converts to light or heavy use, job redesign and EVP fixes must be built for the whole workforce now rather than for a small pilot group.
</details>

## Retention and burnout (Page 2)

**Q4.** What percentage of gen AI creators and heavy users plan to quit in three to six months, and how does that compare with the overall workforce?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

51 percent versus 34 percent overall (down from 40 percent in 2021) — the vanguard's quit intent is roughly 1.5 times the workforce average.
</details>

**Q5.** Rank-order the top three stay factors and the top three attraction factors for this group, with percentages.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Stay: flexibility 40 percent, meaningful work 33 percent, health and well-being 31 percent. Attract: reliable and supportive people 50 percent, caring and inspiring leaders 45 percent, meaningful work 39 percent. Compensation ranks seventh as an attractor.
</details>

**Q6.** What breaks if leaders assume compensation is the retention lever for gen AI talent?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

They misprice the offer: people will not join for money and will not stay for it, so raises fail while the real drivers — flexibility, belonging, care, manageable load — go unfixed, and the 51 percent walk, taking the in-house upskilling strategy with them.
</details>

**Q7.** State the engaged-but-exhausted paradox with both numbers.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Among stayers, 72 percent feel engaged (above the 63 percent overall rate), yet 55 percent report clinical-level burnout versus 32 percent globally — engagement without recovery still destroys sustainable productivity.
</details>

## Skills (Page 3)

**Q8.** Even among technical creators, which skill type outranks technological skill, and by what margin?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Higher cognitive skills at 59 percent outrank technological skills at 55 percent — a 4-point margin among the most technical group surveyed.
</details>

**Q9.** Describe the opposite social-emotional trends for technical versus nontechnical talent.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Technical talent rates social-emotional skills higher with heavier use (25 → 27 → 37 percent, though creators drop to 20); nontechnical talent rates them lower with heavier use (62 → 54 → 43 → 28 percent for creators).
</details>

**Q10.** Why might nontechnical heavy users undervalue social-emotional skills, and what breaks if they are right versus wrong?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

They may be unaware how their jobs will shift toward managing people and gen AI use. If they are right, tool fluency suffices; if wrong — the authors' bet — they arrive unskilled at the exact capabilities (judgment, interaction, coaching) their redefined jobs demand.
</details>

## Employer disconnect (Page 4)

**Q11.** What shares of employers favor in-house versus external routes to gen AI talent, with sub-component percentages?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

57 percent in-house (upskilling 28, reskilling 23, redeploying 6) versus 30 percent external (hiring 19, contracting 11); 10 percent N/A and 3 percent don't know.
</details>

**Q12.** What is the internal contradiction in the build-in-house strategy?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Building on existing staff requires retaining the people who embody the skills, yet 51 percent of creators and heavy users plan to leave within months — "it's hard to reskill and upskill people when they are looking to leave."
</details>

**Q13.** Name the three leadership questions and the provocative hours benchmark attached to the second.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Reimagine jobs as human-centric; redefine flexibility around results; listen continuously. The provocation: meaningful weekly work might fit in 20 hours, calling the 40-hour week into question.
</details>

## The two portraits (Page 5)

**Q14.** Quantify the communications specialist's transformation and list where the freed time goes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Synthesis falls from 60 percent to 10 percent of her time (weeks of interviews become seconds of chatbot synthesis, with human review retained). Freed time goes to message strategy, reporter relationships, and helping the chief human resources officer with a book.
</details>

**Q15.** A skeptic says the manager example just adds a duty (coaching the AI) without removing any. How does the article answer?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It proposes a real reallocation: managers now spend nearly half their time on admin and a quarter on people, and gen AI absorbs the non-people share, so coaching the tool is funded by subtracted paperwork — netting more people time, not more total load.
</details>

**Q16.** Transfer: your team adopts a coding assistant and output doubles but code review queues explode. Apply the article's three questions.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Reimagine: assign drafting to the tool and judgment-heavy review design to people, possibly rotating a "review star." Redefine flexibility: measure merged quality and cycle time, not lines written or hours online. Listen: ask reviewers what friction the tool creates and iterate the workflow with them rather than mandating usage.
</details>
