# Who is in the gen AI mix: four workforce archetypes

**In one sentence:** McKinsey's Talent Trends Survey of 12,802 workers finds that generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI) talent is overwhelmingly nontechnical — only 12 percent hold tech-heavy roles while 88 percent are managers, educators, healthcare workers, and administrators using gen AI for rote tasks — so leaders must plan for four distinct usage archetypes rather than a narrow technical elite.

## Key points

- The survey covered 12,802 workers — 9,684 employees and 3,118 employers — across 16 industries in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States, fielded July 28 to August 15, 2023.
- Creators (about 2 percent of employees) build gen AI models and the tools and interfaces others use, and are predominantly software engineers, programmers, and machine learning scientists.
- Heavy users (8 percent) apply gen AI to most of their core tasks, and span roles from designers expediting 3D modeling to data scientists checking code semantics.
- Light users (about 18 percent) apply gen AI to less than half of their primary tasks, including middle managers drafting meeting notes and teachers designing classroom activities.
- Nonusers (about 70 percent of the sample) include nurse practitioners in direct patient care and retail associates in face-to-face roles, but the authors expect a majority to become light or heavy users as gen AI spreads.
- The 88-percent nontechnical share means enterprise gen AI strategy succeeds or fails on adoption by ordinary knowledge and service workers, not on hiring data scientists.
- The article's thesis is that leaders must first understand how gen AI changes employees' experience of work before matching talent to jobs, because productivity-focused talent strategies alone miss what workers want.

---

## About the research

### Sample and method

To track labor market trends in retention, engagement, and attraction, McKinsey surveyed 12,802 workers — 9,684 employees and 3,118 employers — across 16 industries in Canada (n = 3,183), the United Kingdom (n = 3,227), and the United States (n = 6,392). The survey ran from July 28, 2023, to August 15, 2023. A separate executive sample of more than 3,000 leaders was asked how they expect to close their organization's gen AI skills gaps over the next two years.

### The subpopulations that matter

Two subpopulations of interest represent 9.93 percent of the employee sample: gen AI creators at 1.75 percent (n = 169) and heavy users at 8.19 percent (n = 793). Self-identified light users add 18.18 percent (n = 1,761), for a total of 28.12 percent (n = 2,723) of workers who use or build gen AI; nonusers are 71.88 percent (n = 6,961).

## The four archetypes in detail

### Creators: the builders

"These employees help build the gen AI models for their organizations and develop the tools and interfaces most of us use to interact with these models." At roughly 2 percent of employees surveyed, they are predominantly software engineers, programmers, and machine learning scientists.

### Heavy users: gen AI inside core tasks

"These employees use gen AI to help them perform most of their core tasks or to enhance their work functions." At 8 percent of the sample, they range from designers using gen AI to expedite 3D modeling to data scientists using it to verify coding-language semantics.

### Light users: gen AI at the margins

Workers here use gen AI for less than 50 percent of their primary tasks — about 18 percent of the sample, including middle managers, educators, and communications professionals. Examples: "a manager might use gen AI to create meeting notes or to help delegate tasks, while a teacher may use it to innovate classroom activities" and "journalists and writers researching topics might use gen AI to give them a baseline of facts or to help write a first draft."

### Nonusers: large today, shrinking tomorrow

These workers are "either unaffected by or unaware of the impact of gen AI on their jobs" — for instance, nurse practitioners in direct patient care and retail associates whose roles are face-to-face. At about 70 percent of the survey they dominate today, but "our expectation is that a majority of nonusers will become light or heavy users as the scope and usage of gen AI changes."

## Why the 88-percent finding matters

Exhibit 1 shows 88 percent of gen-AI-involved workers in nontechnical roles (administrators, cashiers, customer care, educators, healthcare, human resources, managers, nurses, retail, sales, supervisors, warehouse) versus 12 percent technical, with a 2-percent gen-AI-adjacent technical slice (data analysts, data engineers, data scientists, machine learning specialists, researchers). The implication the article draws: "the pool of gen AI talent is likely broader than many leaders realize — and it's poised to grow rapidly."
