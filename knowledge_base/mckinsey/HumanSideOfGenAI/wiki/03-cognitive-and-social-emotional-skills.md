# Most wanted: cognitive and social-emotional skills over technological ones

**In one sentence:** Across technical and nontechnical workers alike, deeper gen AI use correlates with rating higher cognitive skills above technological skills — even technical creators rate higher cognitive skills at 59 percent versus 55 percent for technological skills — while social-emotional skills rise with usage for technical talent but fall for nontechnical talent, a blind spot the authors flag.

## Key points

- Moving from nonuse to light to heavy use, both technical and nontechnical workers consistently rate higher cognitive skills as more important than technological skills.
- Even technical gen AI creators rate higher cognitive skills (59 percent) above technological skills (55 percent), the article's most surprising skills finding.
- Technical talent reports social-emotional skills rising in importance with heavier gen AI use — except creators, who rate them as low as nonusers (20 percent versus 25 percent).
- Nontechnical talent shows the opposite trend: social-emotional importance falls as gen AI use deepens, from 62 percent for nonusers down to 28 percent for creators.
- The authors warn workers may be "unaware of how their jobs will change in relation to managing and interacting with other people."
- The mechanism is substitution: as gen AI absorbs repetitive tasks, critical thinking and decision making become the scarce, differentiating human contribution.
- The practical upshot is that coding and tool skills become baseline while higher cognitive and social-emotional skills differentiate performance in creative, collaborative work.

---

## The headline trend

### Higher cognitive beats technological, everywhere

"As gen AI interaction deepens (moving from nonuse to light use to heavy use), we see a consistent trend among both technical and nontechnical workers: they rate higher cognitive skills as more important than technological skills. Even among the technical workers who identify as gen AI creators, higher cognitive skills, at 59 percent, are rated as more important than technological skills, at 55 percent (Exhibit 4)."

### Definitions used

Higher cognitive skills "involve more complex thinking processes"; social-emotional skills "include effectively managing emotions, interpersonal relations, and personal responsibilities."

## The social-emotional split

### Two diverging trends

"First, most technical talent sees social–emotional skills rise in importance as this group increases its usage of gen AI, while nontechnical talent reports the opposite trend. Second, creators who identify as technical talent report lower importance for social–emotional skills at a similar level to nonusers."

### Exhibit 4 numbers

Technical workers' social-emotional ratings: nonusers 25 percent, light users 27 percent, heavy users 37 percent, creators 20 percent. Nontechnical workers' social-emotional ratings: nonusers 62 percent, light users 54 percent, heavy users 43 percent, creators 28 percent. Technical higher-cognitive ratings stay high across archetypes (nonusers 72, light 70, heavy 69, creators 59), while technical technological ratings climb steeply (nonusers 21, light 33, heavy 52, creators 55).

### The authors' interpretation

"Taken together, it appears that as workers become more heavily involved with gen AI, their focus shifts away from social–emotional skills, unless they are in technical positions. It may be that workers are unaware of how their jobs will change in relation to managing and interacting with other people, particularly regarding the importance of developing crucial social–emotional skills."
