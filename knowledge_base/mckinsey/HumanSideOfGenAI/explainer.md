# Explainer — The human side of generative AI: Creating a path to productivity

## What it is

In March 2024, a team of McKinsey researchers published an article asking a people-first question about generative Artificial Intelligence (the technology behind chatbots like ChatGPT that can draft text, images, and code from a plain-language request). Instead of asking how companies can squeeze more output from the technology, they asked what the workers who actually use it want from their jobs. To answer, they surveyed 12,802 employees and employers across Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States about how they use generative Artificial Intelligence, what keeps them in a job, and which skills they think matter most.

## Why it matters

Most companies plan to train their existing staff in generative Artificial Intelligence rather than hire new experts — 57 percent prefer this in-house route. But there is a catch: the very employees who know the technology best are the most likely to quit, with 51 percent of heavy users and builders saying they plan to leave within months. Losing them would sink the whole training strategy, and replacing burnt-out, disengaged workers costs companies millions of dollars a year. Understanding what these people actually value is therefore a business survival question, not a feel-good exercise.

## How it works

The argument unfolds in three steps. First, the researchers map the workforce into four groups — builders of the technology (about 2 percent), heavy users who rely on it for most core tasks (8 percent), light users who dabble (18 percent), and nonusers (70 percent, expected to shrink fast). The surprise is that 88 percent of the people involved with the technology are not technical specialists at all; they are managers, teachers, nurses, and administrators using it for routine chores. Second, the survey reveals that these sought-after workers stay for flexibility, meaningful work, and caring colleagues — pay ranks only seventh on their list — and that 55 percent of them suffer clinical-level burnout even though 72 percent feel engaged. Third, the article prescribes three leadership moves: redesign jobs so people do the creative, human work while the technology handles routine tasks; measure results instead of hours (perhaps even questioning the 40-hour week); and keep an ongoing conversation with employees instead of assuming what motivates them.

## Where it is used

The article illustrates the future with two everyday examples. A communications specialist who once spent weeks interviewing executives and condensing their remarks now feeds recordings into a chatbot and gets a draft synthesis in seconds, cutting that chore from 60 percent of her time to 10 percent and spending the savings on strategy and relationships. A middle manager who currently spends half his time on paperwork could redirect those hours to coaching his team — treating the Artificial Intelligence system itself as a junior team member that needs guidance. The underlying pattern applies anywhere knowledge workers juggle routine production tasks alongside judgment-heavy work: the machine drafts, the human decides and connects.

## Takeaways

- Generative Artificial Intelligence expertise lives mostly outside the IT department, so adoption plans must reach ordinary workers.
- Retention of Artificial Intelligence talent runs on flexibility and human connection, not salary.
- Burnout is the silent tax: high engagement coexists with 55-percent clinical burnout among the vanguard.
- Judgment, critical thinking, and people skills outrank tool skills as the capabilities to develop.
- Managers decide whether the technology elevates work or hollows it out, by redesigning jobs around results and keeping a live dialogue with their teams.

## Jargon decoder

- **Generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI):** Artificial Intelligence that creates new content — text, images, code — from a user's request, unlike older Artificial Intelligence that mostly classified or predicted.
- **Creators / heavy users / light users / nonusers:** the article's four groups, defined by whether people build the technology, use it for most core tasks, use it for fewer than half their tasks, or do not use it at all.
- **Employee value proposition (EVP):** the bundle of reasons someone stays in or joins a job — pay, flexibility, meaning, colleagues, well-being, and similar factors.
- **Higher cognitive skills:** complex thinking such as critical reasoning and decision making, contrasted with routine or basic mental tasks.
- **Social-emotional skills:** the ability to manage emotions, relationships, and responsibilities when working with other people.
- **Thriving stars:** the rare roughly 4 percent of employees who both perform at high levels and inspire better work from those around them.
- **Upskilling / reskilling / redeploying:** training people to do their current job better with new tools, training them for a different job, or moving them to where their skills fit best.
