# Digest — The human side of generative AI: Creating a path to productivity

## Page 1 — Who is in the gen AI mix: four workforce archetypes

**In one sentence:** McKinsey's Talent Trends Survey of 12,802 workers finds that generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI) talent is overwhelmingly nontechnical — only 12 percent hold tech-heavy roles while 88 percent are managers, educators, healthcare workers, and administrators using gen AI for rote tasks — so leaders must plan for four distinct usage archetypes rather than a narrow technical elite.

- The survey covered 12,802 workers — 9,684 employees and 3,118 employers — across 16 industries in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States, fielded July 28 to August 15, 2023.
- Creators (about 2 percent of employees) build gen AI models and the tools and interfaces others use, and are predominantly software engineers, programmers, and machine learning scientists.
- Heavy users (8 percent) apply gen AI to most of their core tasks, and span roles from designers expediting 3D modeling to data scientists checking code semantics.
- Light users (about 18 percent) apply gen AI to less than half of their primary tasks, including middle managers drafting meeting notes and teachers designing classroom activities.
- Nonusers (about 70 percent of the sample) include nurse practitioners in direct patient care and retail associates in face-to-face roles, but the authors expect a majority to become light or heavy users as gen AI spreads.
- The 88-percent nontechnical share means enterprise gen AI strategy succeeds or fails on adoption by ordinary knowledge and service workers, not on hiring data scientists.
- The article's thesis is that leaders must first understand how gen AI changes employees' experience of work before matching talent to jobs, because productivity-focused talent strategies alone miss what workers want.

## Page 2 — People over pay: what gen AI talent wants and why it may leave

**In one sentence:** Fifty-one percent of gen AI creators and heavy users plan to quit within three to six months — not for higher pay, which ranks only seventh as an attractor, but for workplace flexibility, meaningful work, caring leaders, and well-being — while 55 percent already report clinical-level burnout, far above the 32 percent global sample rate.

- The 51-percent quit intent among creators and heavy users dwarfs the broader workforce figure of 34 percent planning to leave (itself down from 40 percent in 2021).
- The article explicitly busts the myth that these workers leave for better compensation elsewhere: pay ranks seventh as a reason to take a job, versus third for the broader workforce.
- The top stay factors for stayers (n = 475) are workplace flexibility at 40 percent, meaningful work at 33 percent, health and well-being support at 31 percent, and safe workplace at 29 percent.
- The top attraction factors are relational: reliable and supportive people at 50 percent, caring and inspiring leaders at 45 percent, meaningful work at 39 percent, and inclusive community at 37 percent.
- Among stayers, 72 percent feel engaged at work (versus 63 percent overall) — yet 55 percent report clinical burnout versus 32 percent globally, an engaged-but-exhausted paradox.
- McKinsey analysis notes high disengagement and dissatisfaction can cost companies millions of dollars a year, making retention of this scarce group financially material.
- "People won't come just for the money, and they certainly won't stay for it" — the retention lever is flexibility plus belonging, care, and reliability in the work community.

## Page 3 — Most wanted: cognitive and social-emotional skills over technological ones

**In one sentence:** Across technical and nontechnical workers alike, deeper gen AI use correlates with rating higher cognitive skills above technological skills — even technical creators rate higher cognitive skills at 59 percent versus 55 percent for technological skills — while social-emotional skills rise with usage for technical talent but fall for nontechnical talent, a blind spot the authors flag.

- Moving from nonuse to light to heavy use, both technical and nontechnical workers consistently rate higher cognitive skills as more important than technological skills.
- Even technical gen AI creators rate higher cognitive skills (59 percent) above technological skills (55 percent), the article's most surprising skills finding.
- Technical talent reports social-emotional skills rising in importance with heavier gen AI use — except creators, who rate them as low as nonusers (20 percent versus 25 percent).
- Nontechnical talent shows the opposite trend: social-emotional importance falls as gen AI use deepens, from 62 percent for nonusers down to 28 percent for creators.
- The authors warn workers may be "unaware of how their jobs will change in relation to managing and interacting with other people."
- The mechanism is substitution: as gen AI absorbs repetitive tasks, critical thinking and decision making become the scarce, differentiating human contribution.
- The practical upshot is that coding and tool skills become baseline while higher cognitive and social-emotional skills differentiate performance in creative, collaborative work.

## Page 4 — The disconnect and how leaders close it: build in-house, humanize the job

**In one sentence:** Fifty-seven percent of employers plan to close gen AI skill gaps in-house through upskilling, reskilling, and redeployment versus 30 percent via hiring and contracting — yet the very people they must retain plan to leave — so leaders must answer three questions (reimagine jobs as human-centric, redefine flexibility around results, listen continuously) against a backdrop where gen AI may automate up to 30 percent of work activities by 2030.

- Employers favor in-house capability building at 57 percent (upskilling 28, reskilling 23, redeploying 6) over externally driven routes at 30 percent (hiring 19, contracting 11), per n = 3,112 responding employers.
- The strategy contains a contradiction: "it's hard to reskill and upskill people when they are looking to leave," with 51 percent of creators and heavy users eyeing the exit.
- McKinsey research puts the automation horizon at up to 30 percent of business activities across occupations by 2030, framing the urgency.
- "Thriving stars" — high performers who inspire others and spur collaboration, innovation, and better decisions — make up as little as 4 percent of organizations and must be placed where they multiply performance.
- Redefining flexibility means judging outcomes not hours: the article openly asks whether meaningful weekly work could fit in 20 hours and whether the 40-hour week remains the benchmark.
- Mapping gen AI workers onto EVP factors shows care for family gaining the most importance and compensation losing the most, with feeling valued by a manager, development access, and meaningful work rising.
- Only about 4 percent of respondents fear job displacement (7 percent among 18-to-24-year-olds) — an undercurrent of worry the authors recast as an opening for leaders to start an honest dialogue.

## Page 5 — Two portraits of gen-AI-enabled work: the specialist and the manager

**In one sentence:** A communications specialist cuts synthesis work from 60 percent of her time to 10 percent and reinvests in strategy and relationships, while a middle manager shifts from half-time administration toward coaching people plus coaching gen AI as "another member of the team" — together showing how humanized jobs convert efficiency gains into innovation, face time, and loyalty.

- The specialist's old workflow — sending questions, scheduling executive interviews over several weeks — collapses to feeding recorded interviews into a chatbot and getting synthesis "in seconds."
- Her synthesis share falls from 60 percent to 10 percent of time, with the freed bandwidth redirected to message strategy, reporter relationships, and helping the chief human resources officer with a book.
- The payoff is stated as a triple win: "increased productivity, more innovative thinking, and welcome face time with key constituencies — good for the employee, her team, and the organization."
- Middle managers today spend almost half their time on individual-contributor and administrative tasks and only about a quarter on people-related activities — the exact ratio gen AI can invert.
- The manager's role evolves into managing two things at once: people and the technology's use, with gen AI needing "more guidance and involvement from managers — at least initially and perhaps for much longer," like a new hire needing coaching.
- Managers become "a decisive factor in whether an organization allows gen AI to elevate people's work," creating jobs that are "less abstract and disconnected and more fulfilling and collaborative."
- The closing verdict ties it together: firms that "emphasize the importance of human skills over a simple race for increased output are likely to earn the loyalty of their workforces and higher performance over the long term."

## The argument in five moves

1. Gen AI talent is mostly nontechnical workers, so adoption strategy must target the whole workforce.
2. The most valuable gen AI workers are the most likely to leave, and pay will not keep them.
3. What they want is flexibility plus human connection, while burning out at alarming rates.
4. They need judgment and people skills more than tool skills, so employers must build those instead of only hiring engineers.
5. Leaders who redesign jobs around human strengths — and manage gen AI as a team member — convert efficiency into loyalty and lasting performance.
