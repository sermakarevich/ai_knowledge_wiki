# The human side of generative AI: creating a path to productivity

**Article:** [The human side of generative AI: creating a path to productivity](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/the-human-side-of-generative-ai-creating-a-path-to-productivity)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine your office gets a tireless intern who drafts every memo in seconds. The big discovery in this article is that the people who benefit most from that intern are not computer wizards but ordinary managers, teachers, and office staff — and half of the most skilled among them are thinking of quitting. They are not leaving for bigger paychecks; they stay where hours are flexible, the work feels meaningful, and colleagues genuinely care. The authors argue that companies should let the digital intern absorb the boring chores and reshape jobs around what humans do best: judging, creating, and connecting with each other.

---

## TL;DR

Drawing on McKinsey's Talent Trends Survey of 12,802 employees and employers in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States, the article shows that generative Artificial Intelligence talent is 88 percent nontechnical, that 51 percent of gen AI creators and heavy users intend to leave within months, and that retention hinges on flexibility and relational factors rather than compensation. It further finds that workers rate higher cognitive and social-emotional skills above technological skills as gen AI use deepens. The prescription is a people-centric talent strategy: reimagine jobs around human strengths, measure results instead of hours, and sustain an ongoing dialogue with employees.

---

## Problem & Motivation

Most employer strategies treat generative Artificial Intelligence as a productivity technology and plan to build the needed skills in-house, with 57 percent of surveyed executives favoring upskilling, reskilling, and redeployment over hiring. That plan collapses if leaders misunderstand who their gen AI talent is and what keeps them: the vanguard is largely nontechnical, highly flight-prone, and motivated by flexibility and belonging rather than pay, while burning out at 55 percent. Without a clear picture of these workers' experience, companies risk losing the very people their capability-building depends on and capturing efficiency without engagement. The article therefore reframes the challenge from deploying tools to humanizing work.

---

## Main Original Ideas

1. **The 88-percent nontechnical vanguard.** Only 12 percent of gen-AI-involved workers hold tech-heavy roles; the rest are managers, educators, healthcare workers, and administrators using the technology for routine tasks. Enterprise adoption therefore stands or falls on ordinary workers, and the expectation is that most of today's 70-percent nonuser share will convert to users.
2. **Retention runs on belonging, not pay.** For creators and heavy users, compensation ranks seventh among reasons to take a job, far below reliable colleagues, caring leaders, meaningful work, and flexibility. The blunt formulation is that people will not come for money and will not stay for it.
3. **The engaged-but-exhausted paradox.** Stayers report 72 percent engagement against 63 percent overall, yet 55 percent suffer clinical-level burnout versus 32 percent globally. High engagement without recovery still destroys sustainable productivity, so workload is a retention variable in its own right.
4. **Judgment outranks tool fluency.** Deeper gen AI use correlates with rating higher cognitive skills above technological skills — even technical creators do so by 59 to 55 percent — while social-emotional skills diverge by worker type. Coding becomes baseline; critical thinking and decision making become the differentiators.
5. **Manage the model as a team member.** The middle manager's role evolves into coaching both people and the technology's use, with the system needing sustained guidance like a new hire. Managers thus become the decisive factor in whether the technology elevates work or hollows it out.

## Key Findings

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Survey scale and fieldwork | 12,802 workers; 9,684 employees, 3,118 employers; 16 industries; Canada, UK, US; Jul 28–Aug 15, 2023 |
| Technical vs nontechnical gen AI talent | 12% technical, 88% nontechnical |
| Worker archetype shares | Creators ~2%, heavy users 8%, light users ~18%, nonusers ~70% |
| Creators + heavy users planning to quit in 3–6 months | 51% vs 34% overall workforce |
| Engagement vs burnout in vanguard stayers | 72% engaged vs 63% overall; 55% clinical burnout vs 32% globally |
| Top stay factors | Flexibility 40%, meaningful work 33%, well-being 31% |
| Top attraction factors | Reliable people 50%, caring leaders 45%, meaningful work 39%; pay 7th at 17% |
| Cognitive vs technological skills, technical creators | Higher cognitive 59% vs technological 55% |
| Employer build-vs-buy intent | 57% in-house (upskill 28, reskill 23, redeploy 6) vs 30% external (hire 19, contract 11) |
| Automation horizon | Up to 30% of work activities automatable by 2030 |
| Thriving stars in organizations | As little as 4% |
| Displacement worry | ~4% overall, 7% among 18-to-24-year-olds |

- Two worked examples show the mechanism: a communications specialist cuts synthesis from 60 to 10 percent of her time and reinvests in strategy and relationships, while a middle manager shifts from half-time administration toward coaching people and the system alike.
- Mapping gen AI workers onto employee-value-proposition factors shows care for family gaining the most importance and compensation losing the most relative to employer perceptions.
- The authors close by arguing that firms prioritizing human skills over a pure output race will earn workforce loyalty and higher long-run performance.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

1. Answer three leadership questions for every team: which tasks stay human, which go to the technology, and how humans govern its use; how flexibility is redefined around results rather than hours; and how continuous listening replaces assumption-led management.
2. Protect the hours that automation frees for creative and relational work instead of refilling them with higher output targets, explicitly testing arrangements such as condensed meaningful-work weeks.
3. Place the scarce roughly 4 percent of thriving stars where their collaboration and decision-making influence multiplies across teams.
4. Close the acknowledged evidence gaps: track whether quit intent converts to actual attrition, measure productivity effects longitudinally rather than attitudinally, and extend sampling beyond three Anglophone countries and the post-ChatGPT-launch window.
5. Investigate the social-emotional blind spot directly, testing whether nontechnical heavy users genuinely need less interpersonal capability or are underestimating how their redefined jobs will demand it.
