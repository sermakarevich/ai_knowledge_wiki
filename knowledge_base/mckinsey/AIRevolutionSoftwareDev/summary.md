# The AI revolution in software development

**Article:** [The AI revolution in software development](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-ai-revolution-in-software-development)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a bank where three engineers arrive in the morning to find that a hundred robot assistants worked all night building and testing software, leaving a neat pile of proposals to approve. That is the article's opening picture of where programming is headed: people stop hammering out every line of code and become supervisors who set goals, provide background facts, and check results each morning. The piece sorts this change into four stages and shows a real bank moving ten times faster at half the cost — but warns the payoff only comes to companies that reorganize how they work, not to those that simply buy new tools.

---

## TL;DR

This April 2026 McKinsey book excerpt by Charlotte Relyea and Martin Harrysson, Chapter 20 of Rewired's second edition, grades artificial intelligence (AI) in software as four levels of developer support from unaided coding to end-to-end application delivery by coordinated agent teams. Its method is framework plus showcase case: a taxonomy of Levels 1–4 anchored by a global bank's agent factory running daily human sprint reviews. The key result is that frontier agent delivery reaches roughly twentyfold leverage — ten times the speed at half the cost — while most firms still sit at Level 2 assistance.

---

## Problem & Motivation

Software demand perpetually outruns developer supply, and early AI coding tools delivered only shallow, task-level speedups while leaving team structures, review processes, and accountability untouched. The article addresses the gap between AI's visible promise and its thin realized gains by naming the operating model that captures the full prize: coordinated agent teams under daily human supervision rather than faster autocomplete. It matters because the winners, the piece argues, will rebuild the economics of software — smaller teams, lower unit costs, faster idea-to-impact cycles — while tool-only adopters fall behind.

---

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Four levels of developer support** — a maturity ladder from Level 1 solo coding through Level 2 pair-programmer acceleration and Level 3 plain-English workflow automation to Level 4 end-to-end agent delivery, locating most firms at Level 2 and Level 4 at the experimental frontier.
2. **The agent factory** — the enterprise operating model in which coordinated AI agent teams build software continuously, as demonstrated by a large regulated bank with overnight agent shifts and morning human reviews.
3. **Intent-and-boundaries supervision** — the durable human role of declaring goals and constraints, evaluating outputs, and reacting to agentic decisions while agents run compliance-spanning workflows from evidence provenance to legal and cyber checks.
4. **Context as the decisive input** — the maxim that production-grade output comes from good context rather than clever wording, operationalized through predefined agent activity sequences and standardized output templates.

## Key Findings

| Claim | Figure | Status |
|-------|--------|--------|
| Level 4 leverage | 20x (few practitioners, one department's output) | Frontier framing, experimental stage |
| Global systemically important bank (G-SIB) agent factory | 10x speed at half the cost | Showcase case, consistent with 20x |
| Financial services agent factory | 40–70% higher productivity | Single-organization report, via secondary attribution |
| LATAM Airlines | 50% gains with smaller teams | Single-organization report, via secondary attribution |
| Strongest development organizations | 16–30% productivity gains | Ceiling case, methodology unconfirmed |
| Assisted coding tasks | Up to 2x faster; docs ~half the time | Task-level best cases |

- The sprint review compresses from biweekly to daily, fed by AI-generated pull requests, test evidence, and risk flags, condensing a traditional month of progress into twelve hours.
- Agent workflows already span governance territory — evidence provenance, legal and cyber checks, counterfactual testing, and suggesting as well as making decisions.
- Value does not come from adoption alone; restructured roles, workflows, and risk controls separate showcase results from shallow tool-rollout gains.
- The hardest precondition, stressed by independent analysis, is a persistent shared layer of context across agent decisions, without which multi-agent systems stall at demo scale.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

1. Assess your organization against the four levels honestly, and close any gap between agent ambition and workflow maturity before scaling autonomy.
2. Invest first in machine-usable context — crisp requirements, acceptance criteria, repository knowledge — since output quality is bounded by context, not prompt craft.
3. Encode supervision as routine: predefined activity sequences, output templates, and daily evidence-and-risk-flag reviews with human sign-off.
4. Build continuous governance for security, privacy, intellectual property, and plausible-but-wrong outputs rather than relying on periodic audit.
5. Treat Level 4 as a frontier program requiring platform investment, and reproduce a fraction of the headline multiples in a local pilot before budgeting on them.
6. Open question: what shared-context infrastructure turns coordinated-agent demos into reliably scaling systems — the how the article leaves unspecified.
