# The agentic paradigm and the evolution of operating models

**In one sentence:** McKinsey frames Artificial Intelligence agents — both virtual and physical — as the largest organizational paradigm shift since the industrial and digital revolutions, uniting humans and agents side by side at near-zero marginal cost in what it calls the agentic organization.

## Key points

- The agentic organization is defined as the new paradigm in which humans and Artificial Intelligence agents, virtual and physical, work side by side at scale at near-zero marginal cost.
- Virtual agents are being deployed along a spectrum of rising complexity, from tools that augment existing activities to end-to-end workflow automation to entire Artificial Intelligence-first agentic systems.
- Physical agents are emerging in parallel as companies build "bodies" for Artificial Intelligence such as smart devices, drones, self-driving vehicles, and early humanoid robots that let Artificial Intelligence interface with the physical world.
- The reliably completable task length for Artificial Intelligence doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 and every four months since 2024, reaching about two hours at the time of writing, with a trajectory toward four unsupervised days of work by 2027.
- That trajectory implies an accelerated evolution from an intern-level employee needing constant supervision to a mid-tenure employee operating independently to possibly a senior executive shaping strategy.
- Organizational paradigms coexist today: 89 percent of organizations still live in the industrial age, 9 percent run agile or product-and-platform models from the digital age, and only 1 percent act as a decentralized network.
- The paradigm's promise depends on continued growth in Artificial Intelligence capabilities plus factors such as regulation, so leaders cannot wait for perfect clarity and must act now rather than be shaped by the era.

---

## The new paradigm

The article opens with its central claim: Artificial Intelligence is bringing the largest organizational paradigm shift since the industrial and digital revolutions, pointing readers to the sidebar on the evolution of operating models. The new paradigm unites humans and Artificial Intelligence agents — explicitly both virtual and physical — to work side by side at scale at near-zero marginal cost, and McKinsey names it "the agentic organization." The authors say they draw on early signals from pioneering companies plus insights from technology leaders and investors, and they position the article as a guide to where leaders can act now — refining operating models to create more value and rewiring for an Artificial Intelligence-first approach — instead of waiting to be shaped by the change.

## The capability spectrum

McKinsey's experience with early adopters indicates that Artificial Intelligence agents can unlock significant value, and deployment is described along a spectrum of increasing complexity. At the simple end are tools that augment existing activities; in the middle is end-to-end workflow automation; at the far end are entire Artificial Intelligence-first agentic systems. Physical Artificial Intelligence agents are emerging alongside the virtual ones as companies develop bodies for Artificial Intelligence — smart devices, drones, self-driving vehicles, and early humanoid robots — which allow Artificial Intelligence to interface with the physical world.

## The task-length doubling curve

The article grounds the urgency in a capability trend: the length of tasks that Artificial Intelligence can reliably complete doubled approximately every seven months since 2019 and every four months since 2024, reaching roughly two hours as of this writing. Extrapolated, Artificial Intelligence systems could potentially complete four days of work without supervision by 2027. McKinsey calls this a phenomenally accelerated evolution — from an intern-level employee requiring constant supervision, to a mid-tenure employee who can operate independently, to perhaps a senior executive who can shape and drive strategies. The caveat is explicit: the promise depends on continued capability growth and other factors such as regulation.

## Sidebar: the evolution of operating models

The sidebar traces three eras. In the industrial era, people moved into factories and operating models shifted to functional hierarchies, with products designed for mass replication by people and machines and major upgrades every three to ten years; new roles such as factory workers, engineers, and shift supervisors emerged, and by the 1970s, 39 percent of people in the United States worked in the industrial sector against just 4 percent in agriculture. As the digital era launched in the 1990s, industrial-age maxims began crumbling with the rise of computing: early Information Technology efforts mirrored industrial thinking by hard-coding business processes into monolithic systems for production and enterprise resource planning, then companies shifted to modular digital products and platforms updated monthly or even daily. Speed demanded agile operating models with small cross-functional teams and new roles such as software engineers, experience designers, and product managers, with speed and customer access becoming the keys to competitive advantage; today, 5.8 percent of the United States population works in technology jobs. Now, the Artificial Intelligence era is beginning the newest evolution, revolutionizing knowledge work the way previous eras revolutionized physical work, with the agentic organization bringing together humans, Artificial Intelligence agents, and machines in the workplace of the future.

## Coexisting paradigms

The article stresses that organizational paradigms coexist: 89 percent of organizations still live in the industrial age, while 9 percent have agile or product-and-platform operating models from the digital age, and only 1 percent act as a decentralized network. The conclusion is prescriptive rather than descriptive — the time has come for organizations to move as quickly as possible toward the new agentic paradigm to gain a significant competitive advantage or risk being left behind.
