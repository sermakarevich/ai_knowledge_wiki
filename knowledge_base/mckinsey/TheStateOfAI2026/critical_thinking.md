# Critical Analysis: The State of AI in 2026

## Claims vs. evidence

**"44% now scaling AI enterprise-wide, up from 38%"** — suggestive, not strong. This is a respondent's self-classification of their own org's "phase" (piloting vs. scaling), not an audited rollout inventory. No definition of "scaling" is given in the wiki pages themselves, and there's no cross-check against, say, actual headcount using the tools daily. A one-year, 6-point move on a self-reported categorical question is plausible but well within the range you'd expect from sampling noise plus social desirability (claiming "we're scaling" sounds better than "we're still piloting" nine years into an annual hype cycle).

**"Only 37% report EBIT impact, unchanged YoY"** — this is the most credible number in the report precisely because it is the one that doesn't flatter McKinsey's narrative, and because "unchanged YoY" is a harder claim to fabricate or spin than a favorable delta. But it's still respondent-estimated EBIT attribution, not financial-statement analysis — nobody is reconciling this against actual audited P&L. Treat the *direction* (flat despite rising adoption) as solid; treat the *precision* of "37%" as soft.

**"High performers are 3.3x more likely to plan business transformation"**, and the whole high-performer construct — weak/circular. "High performer" is defined as self-reporting ≥5% EBIT impact AND describing it as "significant" — both halves of the definition are self-reported perception, not verified against external data, and the group is only 6% of the sample (~103 people out of 1,719, before any subgroup cuts). Every subsequent "high performers are Nx more likely to..." stat (3.3x transformation intent, 2x leadership commitment, 2.7x agentic scaling) is comparing a self-selected group defined partly by how enthusiastically they answered EBIT/impact questions against everyone else — so of course they also answer enthusiastically on every other self-reported practice question. This is largely response-style correlation, not causal evidence that workflow redesign *causes* EBIT impact. Given McKinsey sells exactly the "workflow redesign, not tool adoption" transformation consulting this finding recommends, the throughline is also literally its own sales pitch — a direct conflict of interest that the report does not disclose or address.

**"32% decided against buying software due to coding agents"** — plausible as a directional signal (technology/healthcare/professional-services skew makes sense), but it's a single retrospective yes/no question with no size, dollar value, or verification of the counterfactual (would they really have bought it?). Treat as anecdotal-strength evidence of a real trend, not a measured one.

## Genuinely new vs. repackaged

New this year: the "tokenomics" framing (token consumption outpacing falling per-token prices, especially for agentic coding/reasoning), the coding-agent build-vs-buy shift, and the workforce-expectation correction (39% expect declines vs. only 14% who saw declines materialize, versus 32% who *predicted* declines last year). That correction is a rare instance of McKinsey checking its own prior-year forecast against reality and admitting it overshot — genuinely useful and appropriately caveated.

Repackaged: the individual-vs-enterprise "gap" (page 02) is structurally the same finding as prior editions of this series (McKinsey has flagged an adoption/impact gap for several years running) — the wiki even notes 37% is "essentially unchanged from 2025," meaning this exact metric has now been reported flat for at least two consecutive years. The 6%-high-performer segment is also explicitly flagged as "unchanged from 2025" — meaning the entire "look how different high performers are" section (04) is a rerun of last year's segment with fresh multiplier stats attached, not a new discovery. Given this is the ~9th-10th annual iteration of the same survey design, a repeating cast of characters (large-vs-small enterprise gap, individual-vs-enterprise gap, small high-performer cohort) each year suggests McKinsey may be optimizing the narrative structure for consulting-pipeline relevance more than for surfacing what's actually new.

## Weaknesses and blind spots

The methodology page (06) gives only: field dates, respondent count (1,719), country count (97), GDP-weighting, and revenue split (36% >$1B). It does not disclose: how respondents were recruited (opt-in panel vs. client list — McKinsey surveys have historically leaned on client and alumni networks, a plausible source of pro-AI selection bias), response rate, industry/functional-role breakdown, or whether question wording changed year over year (which matters directly for the "unchanged from 2025" and "overstated last year" comparisons — you can't validly compare two years' numbers if the question changed). There is no failure-case detail anywhere in the six pages: we're told 94% of respondents are *not* high performers, but nothing describes what specifically breaks for them — no failure-mode taxonomy, no root-cause analysis, just an implicit "they haven't redesigned workflows yet." That's an assertion dressed as a finding. Cost data is limited to self-reported IT-budget-share buckets (28% spend >10%) with no actual dollar figures, no ROI calculation, and no denominator disclosed for "10% of what budget." Workforce-reduction figures (14% actual vs. 32% predicted) are self-reported by employees/managers, who have a plausible incentive to underreport AI-attributed layoffs at their own organization (reputational and legal sensitivity), so the "overstated" framing could partly reflect underreporting of realized cuts rather than genuine overestimation a year ago — the report doesn't consider this alternative explanation.

## Applicability

The headline scaling numbers are heavily conditioned on company size: 54% of $1B+ revenue orgs report enterprise-wide scaling versus 33% of smaller orgs, and the large-vs-small agentic-AI gap widened sharply (40% vs. flat 22%). Only 36% of the sample works at $1B+ orgs, so most of the "scaling," "high performer," and "coding agent build-vs-buy" narrative is disproportionately describing a large-enterprise phenomenon, even though it's presented as a general industry trend. Findings are cross-industry averages; the pharma/insurance/banking skew toward increasing investment, and the technology/healthcare skew toward build-vs-buy decisions, both indicate the "average" org described in the summary doesn't exist — it depends heavily on sector and size.

**Relevance to my work** (AI/ML engineer at Elisity, a cybersecurity/data platform company, working with agentic systems):
- The "tokenomics" cost-monitoring discipline (page 03) is directly actionable — track token consumption growth separately from per-token price trends, especially for agentic coding/reasoning workloads, since that's exactly the workload profile likely at Elisity.
- The build-vs-buy shift toward in-house software via coding agents is a real, checkable signal to weigh when evaluating vendor tools vs. building internally — but note Elisity is smaller than the $1B+ cohort driving this trend, so the flat 22% adoption rate among smaller orgs is the more relevant comparator, not the 40%/32% large-enterprise figures.
- Treat the "high performer" framing skeptically — it's a marketing narrative (and a lead-gen funnel for McKinsey's transformation consulting) as much as an empirical finding; don't use "redesign workflows end-to-end" as a validated prescription, just as a hypothesis worth testing against Elisity's own outcomes.
- The workforce-expectation correction is a useful calibration data point: treat any AI-driven headcount forecasting (internal or vendor-supplied) with the same discount McKinsey itself now applies to its own prior-year survey.

## What this changes

If the claims hold: build-vs-buy calculus shifts further toward in-house builds for orgs with coding-agent maturity, which pressures software vendors (including security/data-platform vendors like Elisity) on packaged-feature pricing and differentiation — customers increasingly ask "why buy this when we could build it." IT budget allocation shifts from license spend to token/compute spend, making cost observability and tokenomics tooling a growing internal need rather than a nice-to-have. Workforce planning should discount aggressive AI-driven headcount-reduction forecasts, since the survey's own history shows a 2-year track record of predicted cuts running roughly double the realized cuts. Second-order effect: if the individual-productivity/enterprise-EBIT gap persists a third year, expect more skepticism (from CFOs, per McKinsey's own quote) toward AI spend increases, which could finally slow the "60% plan to increase investment" trend the report highlights as still-strong.

## Verdict

This is a large, longitudinally consistent survey with a genuinely useful self-correction (the workforce over-prediction finding) and one number worth trusting directionally (flat 37% EBIT impact despite rising adoption, because it cuts against the seller's interest). But the report is authored and published by a firm that sells the exact transformation services its central "redesign workflows, not tools" thesis recommends, its flagship "high performer" segment is a circularly self-reported 6% subgroup with no external verification, and the methodology section discloses too little (no response rate, no wording-stability check across years, no recruitment description) to fully trust the year-over-year deltas it leans on most heavily. Read it for the flat-EBIT and workforce-correction data points and the tokenomics framing; discount the high-performer multipliers as narrative dressed as evidence. Adoption call: **trial** — worth reading each edition for the few self-critical, low-incentive-to-spin data points, but don't cite the high-performer or scaling-rate figures as decision-grade evidence without independent corroboration.
