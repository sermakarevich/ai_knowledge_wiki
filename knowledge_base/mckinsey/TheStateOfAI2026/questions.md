# Retrieval Practice: The State of AI in 2026

Answer from memory before opening any answer. Run sessions with `ai show summary/quiz`.

### Q1. How much further ahead are large enterprises (>$1B revenue) than smaller organizations in scaling AI enterprise-wide, and in scaling agentic AI specifically, year over year?

> [!tip]- Answer
> 54% of large-enterprise respondents report scaling AI enterprise-wide versus 33% of smaller-org respondents. The gap is even sharper for agentic AI: large enterprises jumped from 27% to 40% scaling agents in at least one function, while smaller organizations stayed flat at 22%. See [[wiki/01-adoption-deepening|Adoption Deepening]].

### Q2. Why are coding agents starting to shift build-vs-buy decisions rather than just speeding up existing development work?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Agentic coding tools now let in-house teams build functionality that previously required buying packaged software, so 32% of respondents say their org skipped a purchase because they could build it themselves in-house (highest in tech, healthcare, professional services, energy/materials). This reflects a broader shift toward organizations taking ownership of their technology agenda rather than defaulting to external partnerships. See [[wiki/01-adoption-deepening|Adoption Deepening]].

### Q3. What is the central disconnect between individual-level and enterprise-level AI impact reported in the survey, and how large is each?

> [!tip]- Answer
> 80% of respondents say AI improved their individual productivity, and about half say it helped them build skills or make better decisions — but only 37% report AI has positively contributed to their organization's EBIT, essentially flat versus 2025. Only ~6% qualify as "AI high performers" (≥5% EBIT impact plus "significant" reported value), also unchanged from last year. See [[wiki/02-impact-concentrated|Impact Concentrated]].

### Q4. Per McKinsey's commentary, what needs to happen for organizations to convert widespread individual productivity gains into enterprise-level financial impact?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Individual AI fluency is already rising (on employees' own initiative or via company rollouts), but leaders need to build an operating model that lets them reimagine end-to-end workflows — not just let employees use AI tools within old processes — at speed and scale. Without workflow redesign, the productivity gains stay siloed at the individual level. See [[wiki/02-impact-concentrated|Impact Concentrated]].

### Q5. Explain the "tokenomics" paradox described for agentic/reasoning AI costs, and what two investment figures show organizations aren't pulling back despite it.

> [!tip]- Answer
> Per-token costs have fallen, but token consumption (especially for agentic software development and complex reasoning with frontier models) has grown even faster, so total AI spend keeps rising — about one in five respondents say costs are constraining AI use. Yet 28% already spend over 10% of their IT budget on AI, and 60% plan to increase AI investment next year. See [[wiki/03-cost-constraints|Cost Constraints]].

### Q6. Why does the report say horizontal chatbot spend is being treated differently from spend on agentic coding tools within the IT budget?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Chatbot spend is increasingly normalized as a routine cost of doing business, like office productivity or communications software, whereas spend on agentic coding tools is actively reshaping the budget by pulling software development in-house and displacing money that would otherwise go to purchasing packaged software. See [[wiki/03-cost-constraints|Cost Constraints]].

### Q7. What single behavioral difference most separates AI "high performers" from other organizations, and how has it changed year over year?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Nearly 74% of high performers report having fundamentally redesigned workflows because of AI, up sharply from 55% a year ago, versus only about 25% of other respondents. High performers are also 3.3x more likely to intend to fundamentally transform their business with AI within three years. See [[wiki/04-high-performers|High Performers]].

### Q8. A mid-size company has ~80% efficiency-focused AI usage like most peers but no EBIT impact yet. Based on the high-performer profile, what two or three concrete changes would most likely move it toward high-performer status, beyond spending more money?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Add growth/innovation objectives alongside efficiency goals (not budget alone — high performers aren't distinguished mainly by spend), redesign workflows end-to-end rather than layering AI onto existing processes, and institutionalize senior-leader commitment plus defined processes for measuring AI impact — high performers are twice as likely to have both. Broader risk management (guarding against technical vulnerabilities and unauthorized AI actions) is also a hallmark. See [[wiki/04-high-performers|High Performers]].

### Q9. How did last year's workforce-reduction expectations compare with what respondents report actually happened, and what does this year's survey now expect for the year ahead?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Only 14% of respondents report AI actually contributed to an overall workforce decline in the past year, versus 32% who had expected declines a year earlier — actual reductions were less than half of predicted ones, with the same overestimation pattern repeating function by function. Despite this, 39% now expect declines over the next year (up from 32%), while 43% expect little or no change. See [[wiki/05-workforce-reductions|Workforce Reductions]].

### Q10. What is a key methodological limitation of this survey's findings on financial impact (e.g., the 37% EBIT figure and the 6% high-performer share), and why does it warrant caution?

> [!tip]- Answer
> All figures — EBIT impact, high-performer status, cost constraints, workforce changes — are self-reported perceptions from 1,719 individual respondents surveyed online (May–June 2026), not audited financial statements or independently verified headcount data. Respondents' own estimate of "AI's contribution" to EBIT is a subjective attribution that can't be cross-checked against company financials, and the same self-report bias likely drove last year's workforce-reduction expectations to be roughly double what materialized — so the current year's forward-looking figures (39% expecting declines, 60% expecting more investment) should be read as sentiment, not verified forecasts. See [[wiki/06-outlook-and-methodology|Outlook and Methodology]].
