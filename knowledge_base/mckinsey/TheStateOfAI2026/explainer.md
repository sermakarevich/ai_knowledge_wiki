# The State of AI in 2026 — In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Imagine a big company as a kitchen. Over the last couple of years, everyone in the kitchen got a new gadget — a smart food processor (that's the AI). At first, only a few chefs used it here and there. Now, almost everyone in the kitchen uses it for at least one task, and in many kitchens the gadget has moved from "one chef's side project" to "the whole kitchen redesigned its recipes around it." That is the headline of this report: AI use inside companies is deepening, not just spreading thin.

This document summarizes McKinsey's (a large global consulting firm) yearly survey of about 1,700 executives and managers across 97 countries, asking one central question: is AI actually making companies more profitable, or is it just a cool tool people enjoy using? The answer, in short: individual workers genuinely feel more productive and capable, but so far that hasn't reliably turned into extra profit for most companies — except for a small group that changed *how they work*, not just *what tools they use*.

Think of it like buying everyone in the kitchen a food processor. Each chef chops vegetables faster. But if the kitchen still cooks the same old menu the same old way, the restaurant's profits don't necessarily go up — someone still has to redesign the menu and the workflow to actually turn "faster chopping" into "more meals served, cheaper, and tastier." That redesign step is what separates the companies pulling ahead from everyone else.

## Why does it matter?

Companies are pouring money into AI. More than a quarter of surveyed organizations now spend over 10% of their entire technology budget on AI, and 60% plan to spend even more next year. That's a serious, sustained bet — comparable to a household deciding AI subscriptions and tools now deserve a bigger slice of the monthly budget than electricity.

But here's the catch: only 37% of companies say AI has actually helped their bottom-line profit (measured as EBIT — see jargon table below), and that number hasn't budged from last year despite far more AI usage. In everyday terms: people are buying more gym memberships, going to the gym more often, and feeling more energetic — but the company's health insurance costs haven't dropped. Spending is up, satisfaction is up, but the "hard number" that boards and investors care about most is stuck.

If this gap never closes, AI risks becoming an expensive habit that employees love but that never shows up as extra profit — eventually inviting budget cuts. If the gap does close (as it has for a small "high performer" group), AI could become one of the biggest profit levers companies have ever had. Which way it goes depends less on buying better AI, and more on whether companies are willing to redesign how work actually gets done.

## How does it work?

**How the survey was done:** McKinsey ran an online questionnaire from May to June 2026, gathering 1,719 responses from executives, managers, and employees across 97 countries and many industries. Answers were weighted (adjusted) by each country's share of the world economy, so the results better reflect the global business landscape rather than just wherever they got the most responses.

**The core storyline, step by step:**

1. **Adoption keeps deepening.** 44% of companies now say AI is "scaling" — meaning it's used enterprise-wide rather than in one department — up from 38% last year. Nearly 90% of companies use AI regularly in at least one business function. Big companies (over $1 billion in revenue) are pulling far ahead of small ones, especially with "agentic AI" (AI that can take multi-step actions on its own, not just answer questions) and AI coding tools that write software.

2. **Individuals feel it — but the company's bottom line doesn't, yet.** 80% of workers say AI made them personally more productive, and about half say it helped them make better decisions or learn new skills. Yet only 37% of organizations report AI actually helping company-wide profit, unchanged from last year. It's a classic case of "everyone got faster individually, but the machine as a whole didn't speed up."

3. **A cost squeeze is starting to bite — but doesn't slow spending.** About one in five companies say the running costs of AI (including "tokens" — see jargon table) are limiting how much they use it. Yet paradoxically, spending keeps rising, because leaders believe the long-term payoff is worth the short-term cost pressure.

4. **A small group — "high performers" — cracked the code.** Only about 6% of companies qualify as "high performers": they report both a meaningful profit boost (5%+ of EBIT) from AI and describe its impact as "significant." What makes them different isn't a bigger budget — it's that they (a) aim for growth and innovation, not just cost-cutting, (b) actually redesigned their workflows around AI instead of bolting AI onto old processes, (c) have visible senior-leadership backing and ways to measure impact, and (d) proactively manage the new risks AI introduces (like AI taking unintended actions). Nearly three-quarters of them redesigned workflows, versus only a quarter of everyone else.

5. **Fears of mass layoffs were overblown — so far.** Last year, 32% of respondents expected AI to shrink their workforce. In reality, only 14% say it actually did. But expectations for the *next* year are rising again (39%), so the story isn't over — it's a case of "the layoffs didn't happen as fast as people feared, but people still expect them eventually."

## Where can this be used?

- **A mid-size company (not McKinsey's typical Fortune 500 client):** Don't just roll out a chatbot and call it done. Pick one painful, repetitive workflow (e.g., customer support ticket triage) and redesign the entire process around AI — who does what, what gets automated, how success is measured — rather than handing existing staff a new tool and hoping productivity magically shows up as profit.

- **A nonprofit:** Use the "high performer" lesson directly — before spending on AI tools, define what "success" looks like (cost saved, more people served, faster grant reporting) and get leadership to visibly back the initiative. Measurement and leadership buy-in mattered more than budget size in the survey.

- **A small tech team:** The survey shows smaller organizations lag on "agentic AI" and coding agents specifically. If your team already uses AI coding assistants, this is a place where even small teams can catch up to large enterprises fast, potentially reducing the need to buy expensive third-party software.

- **Any leader deciding whether to expand AI budgets:** Budget for the ongoing "running costs" (tokens, compute) as a real, growing line item, not a rounding error — costs are rising even as prices per unit fall, because usage is growing faster than prices are dropping.

## Conclusions & takeaways

- AI adoption is real, deep, and still growing — this is not hype fading out.
- Individual employees genuinely feel more productive and skilled because of AI.
- Company-wide profit impact has NOT meaningfully improved in a year, despite all that adoption — the "ROI" (return on investment) puzzle is still mostly unsolved.
- The difference between companies that see profit impact and those that don't is workflow redesign and leadership commitment — not just spending more money on AI.
- Fears of AI-driven mass layoffs were larger than what actually happened last year, though expectations of future reductions are creeping up again.
- **Honest limitation:** This is a survey of self-reported opinions and perceptions from 1,719 people, not an audit of actual company financial statements. When a respondent says "AI improved my productivity" or "AI added 5% to our EBIT," that is their personal belief or estimate, not a verified accounting figure. Treat the numbers as directionally informative, not precise measurements.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | Plain meaning |
|------|---------------|
| EBIT | "Earnings Before Interest and Taxes" — a common way to measure a company's core profit, before accounting for debt payments and taxes. Think of it as "how much money the business itself made, ignoring loans and the tax bill." |
| Agentic AI | AI that doesn't just answer a question but can take a series of actions on its own to complete a task (e.g., booking a trip, not just suggesting one). |
| Agentic coding tools / coding agents | AI systems that can write, test, and fix software code with minimal human step-by-step guidance — used by developers to build software faster or in-house instead of buying it. |
| Scaling phase | When a company moves a technology from small pilot projects into full, enterprise-wide, everyday use — the difference between "a few people testing it" and "everyone using it." |
| High performer (in this report) | A company that reports both a 5%+ boost to EBIT from AI AND describes AI's overall impact as "significant" — about 6% of survey respondents qualify. |
| Tokenomics | The economics of "tokens" — the small chunks of text an AI model processes to generate a response. More complex AI tasks use more tokens, so "tokenomics" is shorthand for managing AI's running costs. |
| Token / token costs | The basic unit AI language models are billed by, roughly like word-fragments. More usage (longer conversations, more complex tasks) means more tokens consumed, and more cost. |
| ICT budget | "Information and Communication Technology" budget — the total money a company spends on computers, software, and related tech, of which AI is now a growing slice. |
| Head count | The total number of employees a company has — used here when discussing whether AI is shrinking or growing the workforce. |
| Rewiring / workflow redesign | Restructuring how work actually gets done (who does what, in what order, with what tools) around AI's capabilities, instead of just adding an AI tool on top of an unchanged process. |
| Build vs. buy | The choice a company faces between building its own software in-house (now easier thanks to AI coding tools) versus purchasing an existing software product. |
| GDP-weighted survey | A statistical adjustment where each country's survey answers are given more or less influence on the final results based on how large that country's economy is, so bigger economies count proportionally more. |
