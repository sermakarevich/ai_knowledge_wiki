# Research and the performance gap

**In one sentence:** A McKinsey survey of nearly 300 publicly traded companies finds that top-quintile AI adopters outperform bottom-quintile peers by 15 percentage points, with gains of 16 to 30 percent in productivity, customer experience, and time to market and 31 to 45 percent in software quality.

## Key points

- The study surveyed nearly 300 senior leaders from publicly traded companies on AI adoption and practices, with 100 of them assessing impact across software quality, time to market, team productivity, and customer experience.
- Top performers are the top quintile of respondents across the four outcome metrics and bottom performers the bottom quintile, and the performance gap between them is 15 percentage points.
- The highest performers report 16 to 30 percent improvements in team productivity, customer experience, and time to market, plus 31 to 45 percent improvements in software quality.
- High performance shows up organizationally as higher artifact consistency and quality, shorter sprint cycles, smaller team sizes, and stronger customer satisfaction scores.
- The article's central thesis is that capturing AI's revolutionary promise requires a complete overhaul of processes, roles, and ways of working, not mere tool adoption, to keep pace with accelerating model intelligence.
- The outperformers' formula is two key shifts supported by three critical enablers, and nearly two-thirds of top performers use at least three of these five factors compared with only 10 percent of lower-performing peers.

---

## The puzzle the survey addresses

For all of software's technological advances and world-changing impacts over the past half century, its potential has historically been limited by a shortage of skilled developers, finite coding capacity, and the complexity of coordinating large projects. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) and then agentic AI were supposed to break those constraints and unlock untold productivity and value creation. The survey confirms broad but shallow progress — many organizations see some positive impact — while a small subset reaps particularly large gains, and the rest of the article is an anatomy of that subset.

## Methodology in the authors' framing

The survey methodology covered five core dimensions of the product operating model: structure, strategy and governance, ways of working, culture and talent, and tooling. Respondents spanned multiple sectors including technology, financial services, healthcare, energy, and retail, and geographies across the Americas, Asia, and Europe. The quintile design matters because it compares sustained outcome leaders against laggards rather than adopters against non-adopters, so the 15-point gap measures maturity of use, not presence of tools.

## Why adoption alone does not explain the gap

The article's thesis sentence is explicit: realizing the revolutionary promise of AI on software product development "will require a complete overhaul of processes, roles, and ways of working to keep pace with accelerating tool and model intelligence." Tooling is therefore the moving target — autocomplete giving way to reasoning-driven agents — while operating-model change is the durable differentiator. That is why the authors organize their prescription as practices embedded across the product development life cycle rather than as a tooling recommendation.
