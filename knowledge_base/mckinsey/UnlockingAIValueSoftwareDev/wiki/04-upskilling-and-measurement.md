# Upskilling and measuring what matters

**In one sentence:** Top performers replace passive on-demand courses with hands-on workshops and one-on-one coaching (57 percent versus 20 percent of bottom performers) and track outcome metrics like quality gains (79 percent) and speed gains (57 percent) instead of vanity adoption counts.

## Key points

- The first two enablers are intensive upskilling and outcome-based impact measurement, without which the two practice shifts do not convert into sustained performance gains.
- While most companies offer on-demand courses, top performers invest in hands-on workshops and one-on-one coaching at 57 percent versus only 20 percent of bottom performers.
- Prompt engineering — breaking down problems to communicate clearly with a large language model (LLM) — is cited as one example of a complexity that demands intensive training rather than tool handout.
- Top performers track outcomes, monitoring quality improvements at 79 percent and speed gains at 57 percent, while bottom performers watch only adoption metrics such as tool usage frequency or code acceptance rates that barely correlate with performance.
- Sonar CEO Tariq Shaukat supplies the measurement maxim: count what the code achieves — secure, maintainable, useful software — not lines of code or AI contribution percentages.
- Impact metrics must stay flexible because the gold-star measures evolve with tooling capability, for example as generating code from a design document becomes routine.

---

## Why training must be intensive

"Upskilling engineers and product managers to use AI is not as simple as providing them with a tool." The article's example is prompt engineering as problem decomposition for LLM communication, but the 57-versus-20 gap is the load-bearing number: format predicts results, with passive courses as the modal failure and coached practice as the differentiator. The implication is budgetary as much as pedagogical — coaching hours, not license seats, are the scarce input.

## Outcomes over adoption

"High-performing organizations know it's not sufficient to focus only on adoption metrics such as tool usage frequency or code acceptance rates." The outperformers' pair is quality improvements (79 percent) and speed gains (57 percent), and "by holding teams accountable for impact, leading organizations sustain momentum and adjust quickly when needed." The causal claim is directional: outcome accountability sustains momentum, while adoption-only tracking shows "little correlation with performance" on its own.

## Shaukat's quality test

"Too often, companies measure AI's impact by counting how much code it produces rather than what that code achieves," says Tariq Shaukat, CEO of Sonar. "Lines of code or AI contribution percentages don't reveal whether the output is secure, maintainable, or even useful. The real progress comes from tracking how these tools help teams ship higher-quality, more reliable software — not just more of it." The three adjectives — secure, maintainable, useful — double as a review checklist for any AI-generated change.

## Metrics that move with the tools

"As tooling evolves and allows for more capabilities (such as generating code from a design document), the 'gold star' impact metrics will also evolve, forcing organizations to be flexible and adaptable." Measurement systems must therefore be versioned like software: today's acceptance-rate dashboard becomes tomorrow's design-to-code quality gate, and rigidity in metrics repeats the adoption-vanity failure at a higher level of sophistication.
