# End-to-end use cases across the life cycle

**In one sentence:** Top performers embed AI across the whole product development life cycle instead of running isolated pilots, making them six to seven times more likely than peers to scale four or more use cases from design and coding through testing, deployment, and adoption tracking.

## Key points

- The first of the two shifts is a holistic approach that embeds AI across the entire product development life cycle (PDLC) rather than limiting it to isolated use cases.
- Top performers are six to seven times more likely than peers to scale to four or more use cases spanning design, coding, testing, deployment, and adoption tracking.
- Nearly two-thirds of leaders report four or more use cases at scale, compared with just 10 percent of bottom performers.
- Cursor, the fast-growing AI-native start-up profiled as the case study, operates as an internal lab where teams trial workflow solutions on their own pain points and productize the ones that gain traction.
- Cursor engineers combine AI agents, Bugbot code review, and human review, set team commands and rules at file or codebase scope toward a paved-path PDLC, and use Plan Mode to plot changes before implementation.
- The Cursor workflow runs real-time agent collaboration including voice alongside parallel background agents, in-editor browser control for visual iteration, and Bugbot validation before human review, lifting feature throughput with a lean team.

---

## From experimentation to impact

The article frames this shift as the bridge "between experimentation and impact": isolated pilots in coding assistance do not compound, while coverage across the life cycle does. The two practices are described as mutually reinforcing, meaning breadth of use cases and role redesign amplify each other rather than adding independently.

## The Cursor internal lab

Cursor's team "effectively operates as an internal lab for AI-driven engineering workflows, where teams trial solutions to their own pain points and 'productize' those that gain traction." The dogfooding loop is the mechanism: pain points surface in real work, fixes are built with the team's own tooling, and the winners become product. Sprints explicitly balance new-feature delivery against process improvement, so workflow investment is scheduled work rather than spare-time tinkering.

## Paved-path PDLC and Plan Mode

Engineers set "team commands, prompts, and rules at different levels of scope, whether for an individual file or the entire codebase," moving "from sparse documentation to a 'paved-path PDLC.'" Scoped rules encode organizational design decisions where the work happens, so the easy path and the sanctioned path coincide. "The team uses the Plan Mode feature of their flagship coding tool to plot their changes before implementation, starting on customer feature requests in the editor or directly from their team communication tools" — planning before execution, initiated where requests already live.

## Parallel agents and layered review

During development engineers "collaborate with agents in real time, including through voice," while simultaneously triggering background agents — "sometimes running multiple agents locally in parallel on the same task and later evaluating the best output directly within the editor." Iteration stays visual: engineers "control and display a web browser within their editor to visualize changes during development, allowing them to audit accessibility, convert designs into code, and provide visual feedback during debugging." Quality gates are layered rather than replaced: "Bugbot, Cursor's AI code review tool, examines the resulting code before it is passed to other developers on the team for a final check." The payoff the article claims is throughput with head-count discipline: "this new development workflow enables Cursor to increase their feature throughput with a lean team."
