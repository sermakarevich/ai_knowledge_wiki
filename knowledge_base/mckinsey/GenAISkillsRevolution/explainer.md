# Plain-language explainer — The gen AI skills revolution: rethinking your talent strategy

## What it is

A McKinsey Quarterly article from August 2024 arguing that companies using generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI) — software that writes code, drafts documents, and builds prototypes from plain-language prompts — must stop planning their workforce job title by job title and start planning skill by skill. The authors are five McKinsey consultants — Alharith Hussin, Anna Wiesinger, Charlotte Relyea, Martin Harrysson, and Suman Thareja — writing for technology and business leaders at software-driven companies.

## Why it matters

Think of a construction crew that just got power tools for every worker. The crew can suddenly build far faster, but only if people learn the new tools, someone checks the machine-made work for hidden flaws, and the foreman redesigns who does what. Companies face exactly that moment with gen AI: early tests show developers and product managers working dramatically faster, yet most firms just hand out software licenses without training anyone, then wonder why results disappoint. The article says the winners will treat this as a people transformation first and a tool rollout second.

## How it works

The mechanism runs through the product development life cycle, the assembly line that turns an idea into working software through requirements, design, coding, testing, and deployment. Gen AI speeds up every station on that line: it drafts prototypes from rough ideas, writes first-draft code, generates tests, and learns from system logs and user feedback. That compression — from months of work to weeks or days — changes what humans are for. Engineers stop being bricklayers and become building inspectors and architects: they review machine-written code for subtle defects, combine multiple AI models so one checks another's work, and spend freed time on user needs and business goals. Product managers become fluent tool users who weigh the running costs of AI models and act as trust-builders, spotting why users doubt AI answers and embedding safety checks into every step. Job descriptions blur accordingly — engineers absorb testing, managers and coders may fuse into one product developer — while new specialists appear for AI safety, model selection, and day-to-day model operations. None of this self-organizes, so executives must standardize on one approved toolset, set risk ground rules, and automate compliance checks directly into the code pipeline. Underneath it all, personnel teams map every skill the company has against what it needs, using AI itself to chart retraining paths, and revive old-fashioned apprenticeship where senior experts code side by side with juniors.

## Where it is used

Software organizations adopting gen AI coding assistants, the life-sciences firm mapping its digital talent with an AI skills-inferencing tool, the hardware-and-software company that discovered its product managers needed as much AI retraining as engineers, and the audio company running train-the-trainer boot camps of a dozen engineers at a time.

## Takeaways

- Productivity gains are proven but rarely captured, because licenses without training and strategy fail.
- Gen AI automates tasks, not whole jobs, so head-count debates miss the point and skills inventories hit it.
- Reviewing machine-made work is a distinct, teachable skill that juniors lack and seniors must pass on deliberately.
- Trust is a product feature: someone must own user skepticism and risk checks at every development stage.
- Standardize the toolset, make risk training unavoidable, and let automation enforce policy while everything keeps changing.

## Jargon decoder

- **Gen AI:** software that generates text, code, or designs from prompts rather than following fixed rules.
- **Product development life cycle:** the staged journey from product idea through design, coding, testing, and release.
- **Upstream skills:** higher-value early-stage work such as defining user needs and system design, done before detailed coding.
- **Agentic frameworks:** setups where several AI models cooperate to finish a multi-step task.
- **LLM operations:** the ongoing monitoring and maintenance that keeps language models performing reliably.
- **Policy as code:** writing compliance rules as automated tests so software is checked against company policy by machines.
- **Skills inventory:** a company-wide database of what employees can actually do, tagged by expertise level.
- **Inference costs:** the computing expense incurred each time a deployed AI model processes a request.
