# Retrieval practice — The gen AI skills revolution: rethinking your talent strategy

Answer from memory before re-reading anything. Aim for numbers first, mechanisms second.

## Page 1 — Productivity evidence and the life-cycle view

**Q1 (recall):** The article cites three productivity numbers from early gen AI coding experiments. What are they?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Product manager productivity up 40 percent with documentation and coding time halved, and 30 to 40 percent productivity jumps for developers at IBM Software.

</details>

**Q2 (recall):** What two percentages describe the adoption gap, and what does each measure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

65 percent of McKinsey Global Survey respondents use gen AI regularly, but only 13 percent use it systematically in software engineering — broad dabbling versus deep rewiring.

</details>

**Q3 (why):** Why do bold-looking moves like buying hundreds of developer tool licenses produce predictably poor outcomes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because they are made without a clear understanding of the potential gains and with insufficient training of developers — tools without strategy and skill-building cannot capture the productivity.

</details>

## Page 2 — Engineer skills

**Q4 (recall):** Name the two named engineer skill shifts plus the model-orchestration skill the article demands.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Review (doer-to-reviewer, including advanced forensics and training up tools), Design (upstream skills such as user stories, code frameworks, business outcomes, user intent), and selecting and combining gen AI applications and models so one model quality-controls another.

</details>

**Q5 (what breaks if):** What breaks if leaders assume every proficient coder is automatically a good reviewer of AI-generated code?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Defects slip through because reviewing demands different judgment — compatibility with repositories and architectures, maintainability by other teams, and forensic detection of subtle issues — which juniors in particular have not yet built.

</details>

## Page 3 — Product manager skills

**Q6 (recall):** What did the hardware/software organization's skills assessment reveal about PMs, and what four tooling competencies must PMs build?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

PMs needed just as much AI upskilling as any other role. They must master low-code and no-code tools, iterative prompting, agentic LLM frameworks, and inference-cost planning for working with models.

</details>

**Q7 (transfer):** A team ships a gen AI feature users quietly stop using after a week. Which PM skill from the article applies, and what would exercising it look like?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Adoption-and-trust stewardship: use empathy skills to surface implicit and explicit barriers to trust (for example, users not believing the answers), then address them and bring in risk experts to harden checks at each life-cycle stage.

</details>

## Page 4 — Emerging and merging roles

**Q8 (recall):** Describe the merged product-developer role and list four new-role areas the article names.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

One high-performing person uses gen AI tools to create mock-ups, develop requirements, and generate code from those requirements. New-role areas: AI safety and data responsibility (with code review and approval), LLM selection and management, gen AI agent training and management, third-party model liability, and LLMOps for model performance oversight.

</details>

**Q9 (why):** Why does the article say team-size debates ("will teams shrink?") miss the point?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because higher productivity may justify maintaining or enlarging teams to do more work, while the real change is roles absorbing adjacent responsibilities and merging — a nuanced, messy shift that head-count arithmetic cannot capture.

</details>

## Page 5 — Leadership oversight

**Q10 (recall):** What are the two leadership focus areas, and what concrete actions sit under each?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Standardization (converge on a single set of tools, models, processes, and approaches; explicit license-build-partner decisions driven by available skills) and Risk (clear guidelines; basic risk literacy plus safeguard habits plus testing tools such as SonarQube, Checkmarx, and Coverty; incentives and mandatory training; policy-as-code automation).

</details>

**Q11 (what breaks if):** What breaks if every team runs its own gen AI pilots with no standardization?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A proliferation of tools, platforms, and architectures fragments skills across incompatible stacks, so learning does not transfer, governance cannot scale, and the license-build-partner choice is never made deliberately.

</details>

## Page 6 — Skills-based talent transformation

**Q12 (recall):** What are the two pillars of the talent transformation, and what three design rules govern the skills classification?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Strategic workforce planning and apprenticeship capabilities. The classification must use clear consistent enterprise-wide language, capture expertise levels, and be organized around hierarchies.

</details>

**Q13 (recall):** Reproduce the audio-company apprenticeship mechanics in numbers.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

About a dozen top-performing volunteer engineers took a gen AI boot camp, then each led a three-to-four-day boot camp for 10 to 15 engineers, followed by two open question-and-sharing sessions per week for three months — with apprenticing built into performance evaluations.

</details>

**Q14 (transfer):** Your company plans head count purely by roles for next year's gen AI rollout. Using the article's logic, what is wrong with this plan and what would you replace it with?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Roles are the wrong unit because gen AI takes over tasks, not roles — a "we need five senior data engineers" plan says nothing about which tasks tools cover. Replace it with a skills inventory fact base (have, need, tools-can-cover), AI-assisted reskilling maps, continuous gap measurement, and apprenticeship to transfer judgment.

</details>
