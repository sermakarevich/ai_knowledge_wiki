# The talent transformation: plan by skills, teach by apprenticeship

**In one sentence:** Human resources (HR) leaders, together with chief executives and technology leadership, must abandon role-based planning for skills-based strategic workforce planning — with a classified, Artificial Intelligence (AI)-tagged skills inventory that tracks what the company has, needs, and can delegate to tools — and rebuild apprenticeship so senior experts actively code, review, and shadow alongside juniors.

## Key points

- Bolting generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI) onto existing talent programs will not work for long; HR, chief executive, and tech leadership must transform how talent is found and nurtured around planning and apprenticeship.
- Role-based planning is insufficient because gen AI takes over tasks, not roles — knowing you need a software engineer says nothing about which of their tasks machines now cover.
- The skills inventory is the fact base: it records what skills the company has, which it needs, and which gen AI tools can cover, built with Human resources (HR)–engineering collaboration.
- Classification must use clear enterprise-wide language, capture expertise levels, and sit in hierarchies; tagging skills lets AI and large language models map reskilling paths, prioritize development, and tailor learning programs.
- A life-sciences company using an AI skills-inferencing tool to map its digital talent shows the pattern: inferred skills are confirmed, then written to both individual profiles and the company database.
- Apprenticeship works only with active senior participation — coding and reviewing with juniors, shadowing, go-and-see visits — because credibility and institutional risk knowledge cannot be lectured.
- An audio company made the model concrete: a dozen top engineers took a gen AI boot camp, then each led a three-to-four-day camp for 10 to 15 engineers plus twice-weekly open sessions for three months, with apprenticing wired into performance reviews.

## Grounding strategic workforce planning in business needs and skills

### From roles to tasks to skills

"Current approaches to talent management tend to focus on how to integrate gen AI into existing programs. That will not work for long." The replacement starts at the top: "HR leaders, working with CEOs and tech leadership, must instead transform how they find and nurture talent, with a focus on two areas in particular: strategic workforce planning and apprenticeship capabilities." The core argument is that "the talent transformation starts with HR leaders developing a strategic workforce plan that's built around skills. Companies often focus on roles during workforce planning, but that's insufficient. Identifying the need for a software engineer or senior data engineer role, for example, isn't useful with gen AI tools taking over tasks, not roles."

### The inventory as fact base

"This collaboration is critical for developing an inventory of skills, which provides companies with a fact base that allows them to evaluate what skills they have, which ones they need, and which ones gen AI tools can cover." Discipline in taxonomy matters: "This skills classification should use clear and consistent language (so it can be applied across the enterprise), capture expertise levels, and be organized around hierarchies to more easily organize the information." Then AI turns the inventory into an active system: "By adding skills with relevant tags (for example, expertise levels) to a database, companies can use AI and LLMs to determine relationships and connections between skills for reskilling, prioritize which skills to develop, enable workforce planning to determine specific skill needs by program or team, and develop tailored learning programs." The worked example is that "one example includes a life sciences company that is working to use an AI skills inferencing tool to create a comprehensive skills view of their digital talent," where "once confirmed, those skills are added not only to the individuals' profiles but also to the company's skills database for future assessments." Two operating duties keep it alive: "HR teams will have to work with engineering leaders to evaluate tools and understand the skills that they can replace, and what new training is needed," and "companies have to continually measure progress against their identified skill gaps and revisit the strategy to determine if other needs have emerged, especially as new gen AI tools and capabilities come online."

## Apprenticeship

### Seniors must do, not just bless

"Apprenticing offers hands-on learning to demystify change and role modeling to demonstrate hard-to-teach skills, such as problem-solving mindsets and how to use good judgment in evaluating code suitability. But for apprenticing to be effective, senior experts must be active participants rather than just checking a box." Their value is specific: "They have the credibility and often institutional knowledge that can be useful, such as navigating risk issues specific to the company. Experts will need to code and review code with junior colleagues, shadow them as they work, and set up go-and-see visits so they can discover how teams work with gen AI."

### The audio-company boot-camp model

Incentives make it real: "To ensure that apprenticeship programs succeed, companies should create incentives by making apprenticing part of performance evaluations and provide sufficient time for people to participate. One audio company, in fact, has made apprenticeship an explicit part of its learning program." The mechanics are quantified: "It ran a boot camp covering gen AI skills for about a dozen top-performing engineers who volunteered for the program. In return for this training, participants were required to train others," and "each agreed to lead a three- to four-day boot camp for ten to 15 engineers, followed by two sessions per week for three months, in which anyone could ask questions and share their own learnings." The article's closing note is humble about the horizon: "While gen AI's capabilities will eventually become more stable and proven, in the short term, companies will need to navigate a great deal of uncertainty."
