# Leadership oversight: standardize and de-risk

**In one sentence:** Leaders must impose order on gen AI chaos through standardization — one set of tools, models, processes, and approaches with explicit license-build-partner choices driven by available skills — and through risk governance that gives software talent basic risk literacy, testing-tool fluency, mandatory training, and policy-as-code automation.

## Key points

- Deciding which skills matter to strategy is a standing leadership duty, but generative Artificial Intelligence (gen AI) uncertainty demands special focus on standardization and risk.
- Unchecked pilots proliferate tools, platforms, and architectures, so companies should converge on a single set of standardized capabilities with consistent skill expectations.
- Leaders must standardize tools, models, processes, and approaches and decide whether to license, build, or partner — a choice driven largely by which skills the business already has.
- Risk requires clear leadership guidelines: software talent need not become deep risk experts but must know risk types, build safeguards into code habitually, and use testing tools such as SonarQube, Checkmarx, and Coverty (spelling as indexed).
- Some organizations incentivize frontline gen AI literacy and even make certain training mandatory rather than voluntary.
- Because risk and compliance concerns shift as fast as gen AI itself, leaders should invest in policy-as-code tooling that tests code against designated policies automatically.
- Standardization and risk are two halves of one governance bet: without the first, skills fragment across incompatible stacks; without the second, speed compounds exposure.

## Standardization

### From pilot proliferation to one stack

"Determining what skills matter to the business and its strategy is a long-standing leadership responsibility. The unique uncertainties and opportunities associated with gen AI, however, require special leadership focus. Two areas stand out as particularly important." First: "* Standardization. As groups and individuals roll out gen AI pilots, a proliferation of tools, platforms, and architectures emerges. Instead, companies should focus on a single set of standardized capabilities and develop consistency regarding the types of skills needed." The operational consequence reaches sourcing strategy: "Leadership will need to standardize the gen AI tools, models, processes, and approaches, and determine, for example, whether it's best to license a capability, build it, or partner with a provider (largely driven by what skills are available within the business)."

## Risk

### Basic risk literacy for every technologist

"* Risk. The abiding concerns about the risks related to gen AI require leadership to develop clear guidelines and expectations for employees." The expected literacy level is bounded but non-optional: "While software talent can't be expected to become deep risk experts, they can be expected to develop basic skills, such as understanding what kinds of risk exist, developing the habit of integrating safeguards into their code, and knowing how to use emerging testing tools (for example, SonarQube, Checkmarx, or Coverty)." Compliance is being pushed into incentives and mandates: "Some organizations are also putting in place incentives for frontline users to understand the opportunities, risks, and boundaries of gen AI, and are even making certain kinds of training mandatory." And because rules decay at model speed, automation is the answer: "As risk and compliance concerns are likely to shift as quickly as gen AI itself, leadership should invest in tools to automatically test code against designated policies (that is, policy as code)."
