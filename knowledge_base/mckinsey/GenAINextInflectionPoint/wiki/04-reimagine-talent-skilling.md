**In one sentence:** Talent strategy — not hiring but role-by-role upskilling and reskilling backed by serious people investment — is where early adopters pull away, with nearly two-thirds holding a clear view of talent gaps versus one-quarter of experimenters, and one executive prescribing five dollars on people for every dollar on technology.

## Key points

- Gen AI's automation potential is massive: half of today's work activities could be automated between 2030 and 2060, a full decade earlier than pre-gen-AI projections, forcing rapid workforce planning.
- A gen AI talent transformation cannot be hired out or outsourced away, because it touches the entire organization and its ways of working, and outsourcing risks starving strategic-skill development.
- Early adopters lead on every talent metric: 64 percent have a clear view of talent gaps plus a strategy to close them (versus 25 percent of experimenters), 80 percent are reskilling or upskilling for gen AI gaps (versus 56 percent), and 40 percent offer extensive adoption support (versus 9 percent).
- The investment rule of thumb from one executive is stark: for every $1 spent on technology, $5 should go to people through training and organizational adaptation.
- New enterprise-wide skills everyone must learn include prompt writing, contextualization, and data-driven decision making, layered over durable cognitive, strategic-thinking, and social-emotional skills for the complex tasks that complement AI.
- Workforce planning must be granular and cohort-specific, as shown by Asian financial institutions that measured freed capacity role by role and built targeted learning journeys including large language model operations and responsible AI policy.
- Applied proof comes from a European telecommunications company whose AI coach assessed service agents on 20 skills and delivered hyperpersonalized feedback, cutting average handling time 10 percent while lifting customer satisfaction 20 percent and first-time-right responses 15 percent.

## The automation clock

"According to other McKinsey research, half of today's work activities could be automated between 2030 and 2060, accelerating previous, pre-gen-AI projections by a decade." The implication the authors draw: organizations must "understand their talent and skill needs quickly, adopt various strategies to close skill gaps, and invest in upskilling and reskilling." And the blunt constraint: "A gen-AI-based talent transformation isn't something companies can simply hire their way out of, as it affects the entire organization and its ways of working."

## Early adopters prioritize the human side

"Our research shows that early adopters prioritize talent and the human side of gen AI more than other companies." The Exhibit 3 gaps are wide: talent-gap visibility and closure strategy 64 percent versus 25 percent; reskilling or upskilling underway 80 percent versus 56 percent; extensive adoption support 40 percent versus 9 percent. "Early adopters focus heavily on upskilling and reskilling as a critical part of their talent strategies, as hiring alone isn't enough to close gaps and outsourcing can hinder strategic-skills development."

## The $5-to-$1 rule and HR as copilot

"Companies can capitalize on employees' enthusiasm for gen AI by investing in both technology adoption and skills," echoing macro research that investment in both enables productivity gains. Execution demands "a tailored approach to reskilling and upskilling and close collaboration between business and tech leaders and HR." The article assigns HR a double role — "both by transforming the people domain and by acting as a gen AI copilot for all employees" — and quotes the executive rule: "for every $1 spent on technology, $5 should be spent on people."

## Three talent cohorts, three learning agendas

Enterprise-wide, "employees must adapt to the new skills (such as prompt writing, contextualization, and data-driven decision making) that gen AI demands," with "a dynamic approach to talent development" since "building skills is an ongoing process," plus "strong cognitive, strategic thinking and social and emotional skills to handle more complex tasks that complement AI." Tech talent scaling gen AI needs immersive learning in software development, cloud integration, and security, plus judgment to translate business needs into technology; new tech-adjacent and brand-new roles (chief AI officers are named) must cover governance, operational, HR, and legal aspects. Domain talent gets intensive, often on-the-job upskilling — e.g., healthcare professionals studying personalized treatment planning and AI-driven diagnostics with mentoring and real-world projects. All employees including leaders need responsible-use and augmentation training, such as "gen AI coaching that allows managers to practice giving feedback."

## Granular workforce planning: the Asian banks

"A collection of Asian financial institutions did a thorough assessment of gen AI's implications on its roles and skills. They first analyzed the potential capacity that could be freed up across all roles. Then, based on gen AI's potential impact on certain roles and cohorts, the institutions determined their upskilling, reskilling, and employee redeployment needs." From that fact base they "defined specific interventions to prepare each cohort," including "shifting skill proficiencies toward technical areas (such as application development and integration) and designing learning journeys for technical team members in areas such as large language model operations and responsible AI policy."

## The AI coach: hyperpersonalized reskilling that paid off

"A European telecommunications company put tailor-made reskilling into practice by implementing an AI coach for its customer service agents. By analyzing call transcripts from frontline employees, the AI coach assessed people across 20 different soft and hard skills," with dashboards for members and leaders delivering "real-time feedback using customer quotes and examples" plus suggested improvements and learning content. Outcomes: "a 10 percent reduction in average handling time, a 20 percent increase in customer satisfaction, and a 15 percent increase in the rate of first-time-right responses."
