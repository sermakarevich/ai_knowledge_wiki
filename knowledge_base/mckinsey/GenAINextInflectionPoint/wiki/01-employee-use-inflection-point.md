**In one sentence:** Employees have embraced generative artificial intelligence (gen AI) far ahead of their employers, with 91 percent using it for work while only 13 percent of organizations have moved beyond pilots — a gap that defines the article's call for organizational transformation.

## Key points

- Nearly all surveyed employees (91 percent) say they use gen AI for work, and the vast majority are enthusiastic about it, with 9 in 10 believing the tools could positively affect their work experience.
- Optimism is near-universal but scales with usage: 98 percent of heavy users expect a positive effect on overall work experience, versus 91 percent of light users and 80 percent of nonusers.
- Heavy users (21 percent of respondents, using gen AI tools essentially every day) expect the biggest skill gains, including 87 percent for communication, 84 percent for critical thinking and decision making, and 84 percent for collaboration.
- Organizational maturity is strikingly low: only 13 percent of respondents' companies qualify as early adopters with six or more gen AI use cases implemented, while 60 percent are experimenters and 22 percent have no implementation at all.
- Early-adopter organizations employ a much larger share of heavy users (43 percent) than experimenter organizations (26 percent), linking implementation depth to workforce fluency.
- A global heavy-industry chief information officer confirms the pattern firsthand: public and embedded tools such as ChatGPT and Copilot are raising curiosity, yet there is no easy-to-prove business case for bottom-up, piecemeal adoption.
- The core diagnosis is that technology alone will not create value, so capturing gen AI's trillion-dollar opportunity requires transforming how the whole organization works, not just letting individuals experiment.

---

## The survey behind the claims

The findings rest on a McKinsey Global Survey in the field from February 27 to March 8, 2024, with 592 participants spanning regions, industries, company sizes, functions, and tenures. Usage bands are defined precisely: 127 respondents use public or internal gen AI tools "almost always" (every day); 51 use public tools never or rarely and never use internal tools (or have none); the remaining 414 use tools sometimes (weekly), often (two to three times weekly), or at mixed frequencies. Frequency scales were harmonized across public, internal, and nonwork use.

## Employee enthusiasm in numbers

As the article puts it, gen AI is "a broadly accessible technology that puts AI's potential at everyone's fingertips," and employees are "forging ahead." Beyond the headline 91 percent usage rate, nine in ten respondents believe the tools could positively affect their work experience, and most expect help across skills from critical thinking to creativity. The gradient matters: for communication, 87 percent of heavy users versus 59 percent of nonusers expect gains; for creativity, 81 percent versus 59 percent. Even nonusers are net optimistic, which the authors read as organization-wide readiness waiting to be harnessed.

## The organizational lag

Against high individual usage, "organizational maturity with gen AI is strikingly low." Only 13 percent of companies have implemented multiple use cases (the early-adopter group, defined as six or more). Early adopters concentrate heavy users (43 percent heavy, 49 percent light, 8 percent nonusers) while experimenters skew lighter (26 percent heavy, 62 percent light, 12 percent nonusers). Heavy users, the article notes, "use gen AI for a range of work activities and report greater productivity gains" — so the maturity gap is also a productivity gap.

## The CIO anecdote

The chief information officer of a global heavy industry company grounds the statistics: employees experimenting with publicly available and embedded tools "is increasing curiosity and encouraging greater openness to experimentation," yet "there's no easy-to-prove business case for employee-driven adoption and the piecemeal implementation of use cases." This sets up the article's pivot from individual experimentation to strategic value capture.
