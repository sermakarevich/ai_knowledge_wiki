# Questions — Unleashing developer productivity with generative AI

## Speed gains and caveats

1. How much faster did developers complete documentation, new-code, and refactoring tasks with generative AI assistance?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Documentation took half the time, writing new code took nearly half the time, and refactoring took nearly two-thirds of the unassisted time.</details>

2. Why would an engineering leader be wrong to promise a uniform 2x speedup across all work after reading this study?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Savings fell below 10 percent on highly complex tasks, and junior developers sometimes took 7 to 10 percent longer with the tools, so the headline average hides strong dependence on complexity and experience.</details>

3. A team adopts AI coding tools and ships faster but code reviews get sloppy. What does the study say breaks, and what practice prevents it?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Quality held only when developer and tool collaborated; developers must understand what makes code good and prompt for the right outputs, otherwise speed gains come with unchecked errors.</details>

## Research design

4. Who participated in the lab, and what three task areas did they perform?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>More than 40 McKinsey developers across the United States and Asia with varied experience, working on code generation, refactoring, and documentation over several weeks.</details>

5. How did the test/control rotation isolate the tool's effect from individual skill differences?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Each developer spent half the tasks in the AI-assisted test group and half in the unassisted control group, so every participant served as their own baseline.</details>

6. Suppose the study had used only self-reported completion times with no demos or automated reviews. What breaks?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Speed claims would be unverifiable and quality effects invisible; the judge evaluations and automated readability, maintainability, and bug checks are what let the authors claim quality did not fall as speed rose.</details>

## Where generative AI shined

7. Name the four areas where the tools delivered productivity gains, with the key number attached to complex tasks.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Expediting manual and repetitive work; jump-starting first drafts; accelerating updates to existing code; brushing up on unfamiliar code, languages, or frameworks — with 25 to 30 percent higher on-time completion of complex tasks.</details>

8. What extra gain came from using both AI tools together rather than one, and why does that matter for rollout?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>An additional 1.5 to 2.5 times time improvement, which motivates pursuing combined and advanced use cases instead of deploying a single code-generation tool.</details>

9. Developers report higher happiness and flow with the tools. If a company treats that as a mere perk, what retention logic is it missing?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assisted developers were more than twice as likely to report happiness, fulfillment, and flow, and the article argues that experience directly helps retain and excite top talent in a scarce labor market.</details>

## Human oversight

10. What two fully documented oversight areas did participants flag, with one verbatim detail each?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examining code for bugs and errors — a developer had to "spoon-feed" the tool to debug correctly; contributing organizational context — developers must prompt in how code is used, interfaces, systems, and data because off-the-shelf tools know none of it.</details>

11. A manager assigns an AI assistant to a new graduate with no mentoring. What does the study predict, and what breaks?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The graduate may take 7 to 10 percent longer on some tasks, because without foundations in syntax, data structures, algorithms, patterns, and debugging they cannot steer the tool or catch its errors.</details>

## Leadership priorities

12. List the four leadership priorities and the four governed risks.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Priorities: skill development (training and coaching), advanced use cases beyond code generation, planning for skill shifts, risk management. Risks: data privacy and third-party security; legal and regulatory change including the GDPR; ethics and reputation around copyrighted or contested code; security vulnerabilities in generated code.</details>

13. Why does the article insist on foundational coursework for juniors plus ongoing senior coaching, rather than tool licenses alone?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Licenses multiply existing skill: juniors lack the judgment to prompt well and discern good from bad recommendations, so without basics plus mentoring the tools slow them down and compound errors at higher speed.</details>

14. Your company redirects all AI-freed hours into simply writing more features. What skill-shift opportunity does the article say you are wasting?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Applying talent to business expansion and more frequent product updates while building software design and architecture skills — using capacity to raise the team's level rather than just its output volume.</details>
