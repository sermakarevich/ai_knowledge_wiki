# Research design: the developer lab

**In one sentence:** McKinsey ran a controlled lab experiment with more than 40 of its own developers across the United States and Asia, comparing AI-assisted and unassisted work on generation, refactoring, and documentation tasks.

## Key points

- The lab enrolled more than 40 McKinsey developers in the United States and Asia with varied software development experience, and continues as an ongoing test bed for new tools.
- Participants performed common tasks in three areas — code generation, refactoring, and documentation — over several weeks, with a test group using two generative AI tools and a control group using none.
- Each developer served in the test group for half the tasks and the control group for the other half, giving a within-subject comparison that controls for individual skill.
- Time measurement relied on self-recorded start times, end times, and break times, paired with per-task surveys of perceived complexity and developer experience.
- Output quality was judged two ways: human judge evaluations during code demos identified successful submissions, and automated open-source-platform reviews scored readability and maintainability and detected bugs.
- A demographics survey captured years of experience, expertise, and prior knowledge, and a post-experiment survey collected impressions of the tools across tasks.

---

## Setup

### Who took part

"More than 40 McKinsey developers who are located across the United States and Asia and have different amounts of software development experience." The lab was set up to persist: it "will serve as an ongoing test bed to understand developments in the industry, including the impact of new tools and developments in existing tools."

### Test versus control

"Each task was performed by a test group that had access to two generative AI–based tools and a control group that used no AI assistance." Rotation was built in: "Each developer participated in the test group for half of the tasks and in the control group for the other half." The two tools spanned the era's options: "one that used a foundation model trained to respond to a user's prompt and another that used a fine-tuned foundation model trained specifically on code."

## Measurement

### Time and perception data

"We collected data in different formats. A demographics survey was used to gauge their years of experience, expertise, and prior knowledge. For measuring time spent on each task, participants recorded start time, end time, and break times. Task surveys after each task captured perceived complexity of the task and developer experience."

### Quality and overall impressions

"Judge evaluations during code demos were used to identify successful submissions. Automated reviews of code quality using an open-source platform assessed code readability and maintainability and detected bugs. And a post-experiment survey gathered insights on participants' impressions of the tools and experience across tasks."
