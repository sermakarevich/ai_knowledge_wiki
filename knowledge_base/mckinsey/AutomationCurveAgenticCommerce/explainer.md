# The automation curve in agentic commerce — In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Imagine hiring a brilliant personal assistant to do your shopping. At
first you only ask for advice, then you let them fill the cart, then
you give them spending rules, and finally they just keep your household
running while you step in only for big decisions. This article maps
that journey as six levels, from automatic refills of everyday items up
to a future where your assistant negotiates directly with the stores'
assistants.

The authors — researchers from McKinsey's artificial intelligence
practice, QuantumBlack — argue this is already starting. Their research
suggests computer helpers could be involved in $3 trillion to $5
trillion of the world's shopping by 2030. The catch: shoppers will not
hand over every kind of purchase equally, so the goal is the right
amount of delegation, not the maximum.

## Why does it matter?

Because the stores that win may change. When a helper does the
choosing, glossy ads matter less than boring reliability: correct
prices, items actually in stock, deliveries on time, easy returns.
Shops that publish clean, machine-readable facts about their products
and policies get picked; shops that do not may become invisible, no
matter how loved their brand is.

It also matters beyond everyday shopping. The same pattern applies to
business buying — committees, contracts, and compliance included —
where the helpers could one day keep factories supplied and budgets
balanced automatically, competing on dependability rather than the
lowest sticker price.

## How does it work?

1. Start with what already exists: automatic refills for things that
   run out, like coffee or detergent. About 23 percent of United
   States Amazon shoppers already use the Subscribe & Save refill
   service, proving people delegate when it is reliable and reversible.
2. Add advice without action: the helper compares options and explains
   trade-offs, for example four gifts under $75 that arrive by Friday,
   while you still decide and buy.
3. Let the helper build the basket: you state the goal — a winter
   outfit under $150, a home office under $2,000 — and it returns one
   coherent set of items ready to check out, handling taxes, delivery,
   and loyalty points.
4. Give the helper spending rules: buy the groceries if they stay under
   $120 and arrive Friday evening, or grab the sneakers below $80 from
   trusted shops. It acts alone and only asks when something falls
   outside your rules.
5. Hand over standing goals: keep monthly essentials under $300, never
   run out of baby supplies, keep your airline status at lowest cost.
   You step in only for exceptions.
6. In the emerging future, your helper negotiates with specialist
   helpers for price, delivery, payment, and loyalty, settling deals
   through shared rules while commerce runs quietly in the background.
7. Expect uneven progress: everyday basics race ahead, luxury and
   milestone buys stay human at the final decision, and complex trips
   like travel split the difference — helper does the research, you
   make the judgment calls.

## Where can this be used?

Grocery and household shopping show the fast track: predictable,
low-regret errands where approval fades into the background and the
most dependable shop becomes the default supplier.

Luxury shopping shows the plateau: a helper may study how handbags hold
value or find a style in store, but the purchase itself stays human
because identity, touch, and emotion are part of what is being bought.

Travel and electronics show the middle path: the helper builds the
itinerary or narrows the gadgets by specs and reviews, then surfaces
the real trade-offs — time versus comfort, cost versus flexibility —
for a person to settle.

Business buying shows the institutional version: helpers compare
suppliers, assemble order packages for approval, and eventually manage
restocking and renewals inside company spending rules.

## Conclusions & takeaways

The core message is simple: do not chase full automation everywhere.
Push helpers where they remove hassle, and protect the human moments
where people themselves are the point.

Reliability earns delegation. Shoppers hand over more each time the
helper performs well and handles surprises gracefully — and pull back
when it cannot explain itself or undo mistakes.

Details become strategy. Products need rich, machine-readable facts —
fit, materials, origins, guarantees — because a helper cannot sense
what a person grasps at a glance, and what the helper cannot read, the
helper cannot recommend.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | What it means in plain language |
| --- | --- |
| Agentic commerce | Shopping where computer helpers act on a buyer's behalf |
| Automation curve | The six-step scale from automatic refills to helpers trading with helpers |
| Delegation | Which parts of shopping you hand to the helper |
| Optimal delegation | The right amount of handover for each situation, not the maximum |
| Machine-readable | Product and policy facts published so helpers can use them |
| API-first merchandising | Running the online shop so helpers can read stock, prices, and returns reliably |
| Ceiling of delegation | The point where shoppers stop handing over more, per product type |
| Procurement as a service | Business supplies managed continuously in the background |
| Disintermediation | Shops losing direct contact with shoppers as helpers sit in between |
