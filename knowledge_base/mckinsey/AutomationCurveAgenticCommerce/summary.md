# The automation curve in agentic commerce

**Article:** [The automation curve in agentic commerce](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-automation-curve-in-agentic-commerce)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine hiring a brilliant personal assistant for your shopping. First you ask only for advice, then you let them fill the cart, then you give them spending rules, and finally they keep the household running while you step in just for big decisions. McKinsey maps this journey as six levels, from automatic refills of everyday items to a future where your assistant negotiates directly with the stores' assistants, and argues shoppers will not hand over every kind of purchase equally. The punchline for stores: glossy ads will matter less than boring reliability — correct prices, real stock, on-time delivery, easy returns — because the helper chooses from facts, not feelings.

---

## TL;DR

McKinsey's QuantumBlack team (January 2026) introduces the "agentic commerce automation curve": six levels of shopping delegation — programmed convenience, assist, assemble, authorize, autonomize, networked autonomy — driven by decision-grade agent usefulness, open transaction rails (Model Context Protocol, Agent-to-Agent, Agent Payments Protocol, Agentic Commerce Protocol, Universal Commerce Protocol, plus the December 2025 Agentic AI Foundation), and intent shifting upstream into conversations, calendars, and device signals. The central thesis is curve-not-ladder: the six levels describe what agents are technically capable of, not what consumers will allow, so the goal is optimal delegation, with autonomy accelerating for low-regret utility goods, plateauing for identity and high-regret goods, and going selective amid complexity. Retailer prescriptions escalate per level from verifiable data to application-programming-interface-first merchandising to limitable, auditable, reversible payment authorization, ending in a value-pool shift where the funnel compresses into a single agent-mediated moment and advantage accrues to agent-readable, dependable executors.

---

## Problem & Motivation

Artificial intelligence agents stopped being an experiment and became part of everyday shopping — comparing specs, assembling baskets, resolving trade-offs — and McKinsey estimates they could mediate $3 trillion to $5 trillion of global consumer commerce (goods only) by 2030 even under moderate scenarios. Retailers built for capturing human attention (search ranking, recommendations, conversion) now face a world where the customer is still human but agents increasingly mediate key decisions, starting from upstream moments like a birthday conversation or a low-supplies device signal. The gap this article fills is a decision framework: without a model of how much of the journey consumers will actually delegate, retailers cannot tell where agent readiness pays off and where pushing automation destroys the human value of the experience.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **The six-level automation curve** — Delegation mapped from level 0 programmed convenience (rules-based replenishment) through level 1 assist (analysis without execution) and level 2 assemble (purchase-ready baskets), to level 3 authorize (rule-bounded execution), level 4 autonomize (standing-goal stewardship), and level 5 networked autonomy (agent-to-agent marketplaces), each level paired with the shopper's shifting role and the retailer's readiness price.
2. **Curve-not-ladder with a ceiling of delegation** — Higher automation is not inherently better; time-and-trust accumulation plus category dynamics (ticket size, emotional salience, identity signaling, regret risk) set a plateau per category, so consumers deliberately stop short where human involvement is intrinsic to the experience's value.
3. **Personal vs. institutional delegation** — Consumer delegation is personal (save my time and money) while business-to-business delegation flows from procurement policies, budget owners, risk teams, and legal frameworks, so autonomy advances more slowly in enterprises but scales more powerfully once governance permits, with supervised execution as the inflection point.
4. **Per-level retailer readiness stack** — Verifiable data beats marketing gloss at level 1, application-programming-interface-first merchandising at level 2, limitable/auditable/reversible payment authorization at level 3, and exposed rules and policies (loyalty, eligibility, substitutions, guarantees) rather than mere catalogs at level 4, with machine-readable everything as the precondition throughout.
5. **Metadata as strategy** — Agents cannot infer fit, feel, mood, or occasion-suitability the way people do, so products legible to humans but semantically opaque to machines risk invisibility in agent-mediated flows, making rich machine-readable attributes — and knowing when to escalate to the human — a competitive discipline.

## Key Findings

| Level | Delegation mode | Shopper's role | Retailer price of admission |
|-------|----------------|----------------|------------------------------|
| 0 — Programmed convenience | Rules-based refills | Set and forget | Reliability + reversibility |
| 1 — Assist | Analysis, no execution | Compare and decide | Verifiable, comparable data |
| 2 — Assemble | Purchase-ready basket | Approve or adjust | API-first merchandising |
| 3 — Authorize | Rule-bounded execution | Set bounds, handle exceptions | Limitable, auditable, reversible authorization |
| 4 — Autonomize | Standing-goal stewardship | Episodic, exceptions only | Exposed policies + deep integration |
| 5 — Networked autonomy | Agent-to-agent markets (emerging) | Goal-setting | Machine-readable policies, guarantees, loyalty logic |

- Delegation bends three ways: it accelerates for utility, repetition, and low-regret purchases (groceries, essentials), where approval becomes implicit and being agent-readable and dependable beats being distinctive; it plateaus for identity, aspiration, and regret risk (luxury, milestones), where the agent stays analyst and curator and lower autonomy does not imply lower value; and it goes selective for complexity and trade-offs (travel, electronics, home goods), where trust comes from explainability and reversibility rather than perfect execution.
- Value pools migrate as search, comparison, and consideration collapse into one agent-mediated moment: continuous commerce replaces episodic decisions, loyalty becomes policy rather than sentiment, and margins track service guarantees, fulfillment reliability, and policy clarity — a scale unlock for strong executors and a disintermediation risk for discovery-dependent players.
- The article's evidence base is thin by design: one external statistic (about 23 percent of United States Amazon shoppers held a Subscribe & Save order in 2024, via CIRP) plus the authors' own October 2025 opportunity report behind the $3–5 trillion figure; levels, ceilings, and patterns are argued from examples, not measured behavior.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

1. **Become machine-readable first** — Publish structured attributes, eligibility rules, inventory, pricing, shipping promises, and return policies so agents can find, compare, and assemble with human-level fidelity.
2. **Build the level-3 authorization stack** — Offer purchasing authority that is limitable by budget, time, merchant, or category, fully auditable, and easily reversible, since delegated spending requires all three.
3. **Expose policies, not just catalogs** — Let agents reason about loyalty, eligibility, substitutions, and service guarantees so the merchant earns a place in the level-4 ongoing plan rather than a single checkout.
4. **Invest by bend, not uniformly** — Push autonomy where it removes friction without sacrificing meaning, preserve human commitment moments where involvement is the product, and staff the selective middle with explainability and graceful edge-case handling.
5. **Prepare governance for institutional delegation** — Encode spending thresholds, preferred vendors, and compliance rules now, since business-to-business autonomy scales powerfully once authority is granted and the open question is readiness to delegate without losing control.
6. **Open questions the article leaves** — Where exactly each category's ceiling sits (no consumer data is presented), how agent errors, fraud, and liability are handled in delegated payments, and what happens when platform-owned assistants steer choices toward preferred merchants.
