# AutomationCurveAgenticCommerce — Digest

The whole source at medium depth: every chapter's headline claim and key points, in order. ~10 min. Descend into a wiki page only where you need the detail.

## 1. [[wiki/01-setup-stakes|The setup: agents go from experiment to shopping habit]]

**In one sentence:** McKinsey argues 2025 was the year artificial intelligence agents stopped being an experiment and became part of everyday shopping, an early signal of a shift in which agents could mediate $3 trillion to $5 trillion of global consumer commerce by 2030.

- The article opens on the 2025 holiday season: assistants suggesting gifts that relatives might like while filtering for pre-holiday arrival, navigating headphone specs, and assembling ready-to-buy baskets across five retailers before asking "Should I go ahead?"
- 2025 is framed as the year agents "stopped being an experiment and became part of how people shop," working in everyday moments of sense-making, basket assembly, trade-off resolution, and moving toward action.
- McKinsey's research estimates that even under moderate scenarios, artificial intelligence agents could mediate $3 trillion to $5 trillion of global consumer commerce (goods only) by 2030.
- Agents scale quickly because they navigate the same internet as humans — visiting websites, engaging with application programming interfaces, and interacting with loyalty programs.
- As agents scale, they reshape three things: how intent forms, how products are discovered, and where value pools can be found.
- This article builds on the October 17, 2025 McKinsey report "The agentic commerce opportunity: How AI agents are ushering in a new era for consumers and merchants," adding the "automation curve" lens and a retailer playbook for a world where the customer is still human but agents increasingly mediate key decisions.

## 2. [[wiki/02-three-forces-curve|Three forces and the curve-not-ladder frame]]

**In one sentence:** Agentic commerce is driven by decision-grade agent usefulness, open transaction rails plus the Agentic AI Foundation, and intent shifting upstream — but adoption follows a curve of optimal delegation shaped by trust and category dynamics, not a ladder to maximum autonomy.

- Force one is decision-grade usefulness: agents now let consumers delegate not only inspiration but shortlisting, assembly, and even execution.
- Force two is rails for real autonomy: open-source protocols — Model Context Protocol, Agent-to-Agent, Agent Payments Protocol, Agentic Commerce Protocol, and Universal Commerce Protocol — let agents read data, negotiate with other agents, and transact safely.
- The Linux Foundation's December 9, 2025 Agentic AI Foundation, backed by Anthropic, Block, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, and others, targets the interoperability, identity, and payments building blocks for autonomous commerce at scale.
- Force three is intent shifting upstream: agents act when goals surface — a birthday-party conversation, a trip calendar reminder, a low-supplies device signal — so catalogs, policies, and value propositions that are not machine-readable simply will not be found, "no matter how beloved your brand is."
- The six levels describe what agents are technically capable of doing, not what consumers will always choose to allow — capability and permission are explicitly separated.
- Delegation is shaped by two forces, time and trust plus category dynamics (ticket size, emotional salience, identity signaling, regret risk), which together set a ceiling of delegation where autonomy naturally plateaus.
- The model is "a curve rather than a ladder": higher automation is not inherently better, and the goal is optimal delegation, not maximum autonomy.

## 3. [[wiki/03-b2b-sidebar|Sidebar: how agentic commerce plays out in B2B]]

**In one sentence:** The same automation curve applies to business-to-business commerce, but delegation there is institutional rather than personal, so autonomy advances more slowly and then scales more powerfully once governance allows it.

- Corporate buying involves committees, contracts, compliance, and months-long cycles rather than impulse and inspiration — yet the same automation curve applies, with stakes, constraints, and optimization targets changed.
- In consumer commerce delegation is personal (a shopper authorizes an agent to save time or money), while in business-to-business commerce delegation is institutional, with authority flowing from procurement policies, budget owners, risk teams, and legal frameworks.
- Autonomy therefore advances more slowly in business-to-business settings, but once unlocked it scales far more powerfully than in consumer shopping.
- At early levels, agents assist by synthesizing complex information and comparing suppliers against technical specifications, historical performance, sustainability criteria, and contract terms.
- Assembling a "basket" in business-to-business means a procurement-ready package — products, volumes, pricing tiers, service levels, and obligations ready for approval rather than checkout.
- The inflection point is supervised execution: agents authorized within spending thresholds, preferred vendors, and compliance rules manage replenishment, renewals, substitutions, and exceptions automatically, escalating only when something breaks — optimizing reliability and continuity, not convenience.
- At higher levels agents run on standing enterprise goals codified by leadership, audited by policy, and bounded by reversible authority — e.g., uptime at lowest risk-adjusted cost, supplier-concentration balance, environmental/social/governance targets — and competition shifts from unit price to predictability, transparency, and policy alignment.
- The sidebar's closing contrast: "In consumer commerce, autonomy feels like magic. In B2B, it feels like governance finally catching up with complexity and turning it into an advantage."

## 4. [[wiki/04-levels-0-2|Levels 0–2: from programmed convenience to the personal shopper]]

**In one sentence:** Levels 0 through 2 move from rules-based replenishment (level 0, proven by ~23 percent Subscribe & Save penetration) through analytical assistance without execution (level 1) to purchase-ready basket orchestration (level 2), with retailer demands escalating from reliability to verifiable data to application-programming-interface-first merchandising.

- Level 0, "programmed convenience" ("set it and forget it"), is the pre-agentic baseline: subscriptions and scheduled refills for run-out goods like coffee pods, detergent, diapers, and shampoo — rules-based, useful but brittle and blind to context, breaking when needs change.
- Level 0 proves consumers delegate when automation is reliable and reversible: around 23 percent of United States Amazon shoppers had at least one active Subscribe & Save order in 2024 (CIRP, via Levin and Lowitz, February 18, 2025).
- Level 1, "assist" ("the cognitive sidekick"), is analytical only: the agent scans catalogs, parses reviews, compares features, and synthesizes short lists — e.g., four sub-$75 sustainable gifts shippable by Friday, or three noise-canceling headphones compared on sound, battery, and comfort — with no cart, no basket, and no readiness to transact.
- Level 1 "replaces search and comparison but leaves assembly and execution entirely with the shopper," who evaluates options, weighs trade-offs, and decides next steps.
- Level 1 retailer implication: "Verifiable data beats marketing gloss" — agents need structured attributes, clear eligibility rules, sizing and fit certainty, and substantiable claims.
- Level 2, "assemble" ("the personal shopper"), is the qualitative shift from analysis to orchestration: the shopper states intent ("cozy winter outfit under $150," vegan-guest pantry, sub-$2,000 dual-4K-monitor home office with next-day delivery) and the agent returns a coherent purchase-ready configuration, resolving trade-offs and constraints rather than surfacing them.
- Level 2 agents balance performance against price, availability against delivery speed, and promotions against eligibility, handling taxes, shipping windows, loyalty benefits, and substitutions by default — and the shopper's role shifts from comparing options to approving or adjusting a proposed solution.
- Level 2 retailer implication: "API-first merchandising" — inventory, pricing, shipping promises, promotions, and returns logic exposed cleanly so agents assemble baskets with human-level fidelity.

## 5. [[wiki/05-levels-3-5|Levels 3–5: from supervised execution to multiagent commerce]]

**In one sentence:** Levels 3 through 5 escalate from rule-bounded execution (level 3, demanding limited, auditable, reversible authorization) to standing-goal stewardship (level 4, where merchants compete for a place in the agent's ongoing plan) to still-emerging agent-to-agent marketplaces (level 5, where laggards risk becoming interchangeable price-competed suppliers).

- Level 3, "authorize" ("the supervised executor"), delegates rules, not just actions: "If groceries are under $120 and arrive Friday 6–8 p.m., place the order" or "If my preferred sneakers drop below $80 from merchants I trust, buy them" — the agent runs the workflow end to end, substitutes within approval, applies loyalty benefits, and escalates only on out-of-rule exceptions.
- Level 3 retailer implication is a three-part authorization stack: purchasing authority limitable by budget, time window, merchant, or category; auditable activity (what was bought and why); and reversible actions (easy cancellations, refunds, overrides).
- Level 4, "autonomize" ("the intent steward"), runs on standing goals rather than transactions: "Keep household essentials under $300 per month," "Maintain my airline loyalty status at the lowest total cost over the course of 2026," "Make sure we never run out of baby supplies" — with continuous monitoring, cross-merchant comparison, and operational follow-through on changes, returns, and replacements.
- At level 4 the shopper becomes episodic, stepping in mainly for meaningful decisions or exceptions, while the agent optimizes longer-term outcomes such as loyalty status.
- Level 4 retailer implication: competition moves from winning a purchase to earning a place in the agent's ongoing plan, requiring deeper integration around loyalty, eligibility, substitutions, and service guarantees — "it's no longer enough to expose a catalog; retailers must expose the rules and policies that determine what 'good' looks like."
- Level 5, "networked autonomy" ("multiagent commerce"), is explicitly forward-looking and still emerging: personal agents negotiate directly with specialized agents for pricing, logistics and delivery, payment authorization, and loyalty, forming multiagent marketplaces where intent is brokered, trust travels via reputation signals, and shared protocols settle transactions — "procurement as a service" running continuously in the background.
- Level 5 retailer implication: advantage goes to merchants already proficient at level 4 that expose policies, guarantees, and loyalty logic in machine-readable form; the rest "risk becoming interchangeable suppliers competing primarily on price in machine-negotiated flows."

## 6. [[wiki/06-bending-value-pools|How the curve bends: accelerate, plateau, select — and where value pools move]]

**In one sentence:** Delegation accelerates for low-regret utility purchases (where operational trust beats brand storytelling), plateaus for identity and high-regret categories (where the agent stays analyst and curator), and goes selective amid complexity (where explainability, reversibility, and metadata-as-strategy decide) — compressing the funnel and migrating value to merchants that execute reliably against agent constraints.

- Consumers do not climb uniformly or aspire to full autonomy everywhere: delegation accelerates where automation removes friction without sacrificing meaning, plateaus where human involvement is intrinsic to value, and turns selective amid trade-offs and uncertainty.
- Acceleration zone (utility, repetition, low regret): groceries, household essentials, and basic consumables, where shopping is a task valued for efficiency, reliability, and predictability — approval becomes implicit and intervention the exception as agents assemble accurately, execute within guardrails, and handle substitutions gracefully.
- In accelerated categories "being agent-readable and dependable matters more than being distinctive": agents optimize delivered value (price, availability, service reliability, reversibility), so merchants with clean inventory data, predictable fulfillment, and transparent substitution and return policies become default suppliers without ever winning a traditional consideration moment.
- Plateau zone (identity, aspiration, regret risk): luxury and milestone purchases, where shopping is about identity, intent, and emotional assurance — the luxury-handbag example has the agent evaluating value retention, resale markets, aesthetics, and in-store availability while the final decision and transaction stay firmly human.
- On plateaus "lower autonomy does not imply lower value" because human involvement is itself part of the product; brands should expose provenance, craftsmanship, and long-term value signals to shape deliberation rather than push full automation.
- Selective zone (complexity, trade-offs, context): travel, consumer electronics, and home goods, where agents handle research, comparison, monitoring, and assembly but escalate judgment calls — time versus comfort, cost versus flexibility, design or brand preference.
- In selective categories "trust is built not through perfect execution but through explainability and reversibility": shoppers want to know why an option, substitution, or escalation happened, and graceful edge-case handling matters more than happy-path success.
- "Metadata becomes strategy": agents cannot infer fit, feel, mood, or occasion-suitability the way humans do, so products legible to people but semantically opaque to machines risk invisibility — retailers must invest in rich machine-readable attributes plus knowing when to elevate questions to humans.
- Value-pool shift: the funnel compresses as search, comparison, and consideration collapse into one agent-mediated moment; continuous commerce replaces episodic decisions; loyalty becomes policy rather than sentiment; margins track service guarantees, fulfillment reliability, and policy clarity — unlocking scale for strong executors while threatening discovery-dependent players with disintermediation.
- The close restates the thesis: "The future of commerce is not about maximizing automation. It is about placing autonomy where it enhances experience, economics, and trust."

## The argument in five moves

1. Agents became everyday shopping helpers, previewing a $3–5 trillion agent-mediated market by 2030.
2. Three forces (decision-grade agents, open transaction rails, upstream intent) make delegation, not traffic, the unit of competition.
3. Six levels map delegation from rules-based replenishment to agent-to-agent marketplaces, each with a retailer readiness price.
4. Delegation bends by category — accelerating for utility, plateauing for identity, selective for complexity — because the goal is optimal, not maximum, autonomy.
5. Value migrates to agent-readable, dependable executors as the funnel compresses and loyalty hardens into policy.
6. Retailers should invest where autonomy removes friction and deliberately preserve human moments where involvement is the product.
