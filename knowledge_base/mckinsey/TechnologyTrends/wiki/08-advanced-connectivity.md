# Advanced connectivity
**In one sentence:** Advanced connectivity is a mature but strategically selective frontier where AI workloads are driving demand for AI-ready fiber, software-defined and autonomous networks, 5G stand-alone with slicing, and complementary satellite coverage, with value going to providers who sell reliability, resilience, and sovereignty rather than basic data transport.
## Key points
- Connectivity is no longer just moving data from point A to point B; it is an infrastructure stack supporting real-time intelligence, distributed computing, and demanding enterprise workloads as AI spreads to factories, stores, hospitals, vehicles, and towers.
- Advanced connectivity led all technology trends in patent filings (5G-Advanced, optical transport, software-defined networking); equity investment was $60.5 billion in 2025, below the 2022 peak of $73.8 billion, anchored by HPE's $13.6 billion acquisition of Juniper Networks.
- AI is reshaping network requirements toward lower latency, stronger reliability, local connectivity, and higher uplink capacity: gen AI traffic was 26 percent of uplink traffic versus roughly 10 percent in typical mobile networks (Ericsson, June 2025 mobility report).
- Telcos are responding with AI-native automation (AIOps) plus software-defined capabilities such as network slicing and cloud-based functions, while Cisco's Unified Edge moves compute, networking, storage, and security closer to sites for real-time inference and agentic workloads.
- 5G stand-alone is moving into broader operation (AT&T nationwide deployment announced October 2025), and slicing is becoming a commercial product: Verizon Business launched SLA-backed 5G slice fixed-wireless, Vodafone Business launched a commercial slicing service in the UK.
- Security, resilience, and sovereignty are now core design requirements: submarine cables carry 99 percent of intercontinental internet traffic (EU 2025 Action Plan on Cable Security), driving redundancy, route diversity, and multi-orbit plus direct-to-device complements.
- Nonterrestrial connectivity is integrating into the telecom stack but remains a complement, not a substitute: Starlink had almost 10,000 active satellites as of August 2026, with 225 publicly announced operator–satellite partnerships across 88 countries as of January 2026; executives expect LEO to capture close to 20 percent of the addressable market for existing fiber in hard-to-reach areas.
- Hiring has shifted from pure build-out to serving customers: job postings remain below 2022 levels with a modest 2025 uptick, network engineers still anchor hiring, momentum shifting to software engineers and sales/support roles; cloud computing is required in 73 percent of postings, while CI/CD talent is scarcest (0.1× availability-to-demand); adoption score is 4, scaling in progress.

## Why it matters: connectivity as compute infrastructure
**Covers:** pp. 66–67

- AI workloads are increasing demand for fiber, optical transport, and data center connectivity, giving telcos new revenue streams.
- Cloud, edge, mobile, subsea, and satellite infrastructure are converging into a more interconnected digital layer that could expand connectivity to rural and remote areas.
- Convergence raises the strategic value of connectivity but makes network planning, security, and operations more complex.
- In practical terms, connectivity enables an infrastructure stack supporting real-time intelligence, distributed computing, and demanding enterprise workloads.
- Telcos use AI-native automation (AIOps) to monitor conditions, detect problems, adjust capacity, and improve performance with less manual intervention, restructuring networks around software-defined capabilities such as network slicing and cloud-based functions.
- Nonterrestrial connectivity complements terrestrial networks: LEO services and direct-to-device offerings extend coverage and resilience; executives expect LEO to eventually capture close to 20 percent of the addressable market for existing fiber, particularly in hard-to-reach areas, reinforcing a hybrid model.
- In some markets LEO could improve fixed-wireless-access economics and serve harder-to-reach areas more efficiently.
- Resilience and sovereignty are becoming design requirements: governments and enterprises view connectivity as critical infrastructure as subsea cables, satellites, and mobile networks face geopolitical, cyber, and physical risks, increasing attention on route diversity, supply-chain exposure, secure design, and regionally controlled infrastructure.
- The next phase is more selective and strategic: strongest opportunities where customers pay for more than basic connectivity — reliable factory networks, AI-ready fiber and data-center links, satellite-enabled remote coverage, secure resilient networks.

> "Connectivity is no longer just about moving data from point A to point B. As AI workloads become more distributed, networks become part of the compute infrastructure itself. Competitive advantage will come from delivering low-latency, resilient, AI-ready connectivity that supports real-time intelligence where work actually happens."
> — Anton Lysenko, associate partner, Bay Area

## Latest developments
**Covers:** pp. 67–69

### AI is increasing demand for faster, more reliable networks
- AI workloads moving into factories, retail stores, healthcare facilities, vehicles, telecom towers, and other physical locations need lower latency, stronger reliability, and more local connectivity near data generation.
- Cisco's Unified Edge announcement (November 3, 2025) moves compute, networking, storage, and security closer to sites such as retail stores, healthcare facilities, and factory floors to support real-time AI inference and agentic workloads.
- Uplink needs may rise: Ericsson's June 2025 mobility report found gen AI traffic accounted for 26 percent of uplink traffic, compared with roughly 10 percent in typical mobile networks.
- AI training and inference increase demand for high-capacity links between data centers and computing clusters.
- Lumen invested nearly $13 billion in private connectivity fabric deals in 2025 for secure connections across company locations, cloud services, data centers, and applications without public-internet transit.

### Security, resilience, and sovereignty as core requirements
- Geopolitical tensions, cyber vulnerabilities, and strategic importance of subsea cables, satellites, and mobile networks make connectivity a matter of national and enterprise resilience.
- The EU's 2025 Action Plan on Cable Security (February 21, 2025) notes submarine cables carry 99 percent of intercontinental internet traffic and calls for stronger prevention, detection, response, repair, and deterrence.
- Governments and enterprises emphasize redundancy, route diversity, and security across terrestrial, subsea, and nonterrestrial networks.
- Multi-orbit satellite architectures and direct-to-device connectivity add resilience in scenarios including remote coverage and emergency communications, but remain complements to — not substitutes for — secure, well-managed terrestrial networks.

### 5G stand-alone and network slicing
- Migration from 5G non-stand-alone to 5G stand-alone (dedicated 5G core) enables lower latency, improved uplink, more reliable connectivity, edge integration, and slicing.
- AT&T said in October 2025 its 5G stand-alone network was deployed nationwide with millions of customers using it as device support and provisioning expanded; monetization depends on translating the architecture into services enterprises will pay for.
- Network slicing partitions a single 5G network into multiple virtual networks, each optimized for an application or customer; it is moving from demos to targeted enterprise offerings where guaranteed performance, reliability, or uplink capacity matters.
- Verizon Business launched a 5G network-slice fixed-wireless service with service-level agreements, no data caps, and higher uplink capacity for AI inference, computer vision, augmented reality, and IoT applications.
- Vodafone Business launched a commercial 5G slicing service in the UK for stadiums, logistics hubs, campuses, and construction sites.
- These are early commercial examples, not broad-based slicing monetization, but they signal forward momentum.

### Nonterrestrial networks joining the telecom stack
- Satellite connectivity is evolving from stand-alone broadband and emergency messaging toward an integrated telecom layer.
- As of August 2026, Starlink had almost 10,000 active satellites plus carrier partnerships worldwide.
- Vodafone and Amazon Leo signed an agreement (March 2, 2026) to connect 4G and 5G mobile sites in remote Europe and Africa areas via LEO broadband where fiber or fixed wireless is costly or slow.
- Industry tracking shows 225 publicly announced operator–satellite partnerships across 88 countries and territories as of January 2026.
- Satellite is a complement for rural coverage, critical services, and resilience — not yet a wholesale substitute given economic and technical constraints.

## Talent and labor markets
**Covers:** pp. 69–70

### Demand
- Job postings remain below 2022 levels, though hiring ticked back up modestly in 2025.
- Early 5G/fiber scale-up drove heavy demand for network engineers, who still anchor hiring.
- Momentum has shifted toward software engineering and operational/customer-facing roles (sales representatives, account executives, technical support specialists) — from pure build-out to growing and serving the customer base.
- Demand for specialized engineering and architecture roles remains more subdued than in 2022, pointing to measured appetite for new platform development.
- Top titles by volume include network engineer, software engineer, sales representative, technician, solution architect, site reliability engineer, technical support specialist, security engineer, account executive, and network architect.
- Business-function mix in 2025: R&D 38%, sales and marketing 16%, operations 23%, general and administrative 10%, other 13%.

### Skills availability
- Cloud computing sits at the center of demand, required in roughly three-quarters (73%) of postings — more than any other capability — with supply broadly keeping pace (1.0× availability-to-demand).
- Talent is hardest to find in CI/CD (continuous integration and continuous delivery), key as networks become software-driven: required in 13% of postings, availability ratio 0.1×.
- Other required-skill shares and availability ratios: software development 21% / 3.9×; Python 18% / 1.8×; routing 14% / 0.9×; Cisco 14% / (Cisco-relevant skills readily available per text); Azure 13% / 1.4×; Amazon Web Services 12% / 0.9×.
- Software development and Cisco-relevant skills are more readily available, reflecting that legacy infrastructure skills may not map to next-generation needs.

## Adoption developments across the globe
**Covers:** pp. 70–71

- Adoption score: 4 — Scaling in progress.
- Enterprises across telecom, cloud infrastructure, manufacturing, logistics, public safety, and energy are scaling; adoption is uneven across use cases, regions, and technology layers, segmented by subdomain rather than a single leaderboard.
- North America: fiber build-out, satellite services extending mobile coverage, dedicated capacity for public safety, high-capacity AI data-center links.
- Europe: private wireless, industrial connectivity, network APIs, spectrum policy, digital sovereignty.
- China, Japan, South Korea, and other Asian markets: 5G-Advanced, 6G research, dense mobile networks, advanced connectivity in factories, ports, logistics.
- Emerging markets: expanding coverage via fixed wireless access, LEO satellite broadband, low-power IoT, rural connectivity where fiber and terrestrial mobile are hard to deploy cost-effectively; value centers on basic wireless coverage.
- Private wireless concentrates in high- and upper-middle-income markets (manufacturers, mines, ports, campuses, utilities justifying dedicated networks); nonterrestrial has a broader geographic role for rural, offshore, and disaster-prone areas.
- Adoption is strongest when technology solves a specific problem: coverage gaps, congestion, public safety, industrial automation, AI-ready data transport, enterprise security and reliability.

## Key uncertainties and big questions
**Covers:** pp. 71–72

- Monetization beyond basic connectivity: APIs, slicing, quality-on-demand, edge connectivity, private wireless could create new revenue, but customers pay only where value is clear, measurable, tied to business outcomes.
- Impact of AI on network architecture: AI devices and applications may raise upload, latency, data-center-interconnect, and optical-transport demand; timing, scale, and location remain uncertain as workloads shift among devices, enterprise sites, clouds, and data centers.
- Commercial scale of private, programmable, differentiated networks: technically useful, but broad adoption depends on willingness to pay; strongest in operationally intense manufacturing, logistics, utilities, slower general-enterprise growth.
- Economics of nonterrestrial connectivity: LEO, direct-to-device, hybrid networks are operational, but opportunity size and durability are uncertain; business cases hinge on capacity, terminal costs, performance, willingness to pay.
- Complexity, resilience, and energy demands: AI-enabled radio access networks, cloud-based functions, open interfaces, and 6G could improve flexibility and performance but raise integration, security, operational, and energy challenges; geopolitical and critical-infrastructure pressures elevate resilience and sovereignty.
- Big questions: where can operators create sustainable revenue beyond data transport; how will AI change network demand (device-to-cloud flows, data-center links, faster responses); what mix of public, private, terrestrial, subsea, and satellite fits each market and use case; where will customers pay for guaranteed performance, priority, resilience, or dedicated connectivity versus Wi-Fi/public cellular; what security, resilience, and governance models fit connectivity's critical role.
