# Explainer — State of AI trust in 2026: Shifting to the agentic era

## What it is

Every year McKinsey surveys several hundred companies about how responsibly they run their Artificial Intelligence. The 2026 edition is the first to focus on the agentic era, meaning Artificial Intelligence systems that do not just answer questions but take actions on their own: moving money, changing bookings, editing systems, talking to other software. About 500 organizations answered, and each got a maturity grade from 0 to 4 across five areas such as strategy, risk management, data and technology, governance, and a brand-new category for controlling autonomous agents.

## Why it matters

Think of the difference between an assistant who drafts a letter and one who is allowed to sign and post it. The second needs far more supervision, because a mistake now causes real damage instead of just embarrassment. Companies are rapidly handing Artificial Intelligence the pen, and this survey checks whether their supervision habits are keeping up. The answer is mostly no: the average grade crept up from 2.0 to 2.3, but only about a third of firms score well on the oversight parts. As agents spread into hiring, lending, customer service, and supply chains, weak oversight stops being an internal embarrassment and starts affecting customers, workers, and the public.

## How it works

The survey works like a health checkup with five stations. Companies report what practices they actually run — who owns Artificial Intelligence risk, which controls exist, how data is governed, how agents are monitored — and McKinsey converts the answers into grades. The pattern that emerges is consistent: the technology stations score fine while the human stations fail. Firms can build agents but cannot say who is accountable for them, cannot staff the training, and do not mitigate the risks they themselves flag. Two numbers tell the story: companies with a named owner average 2.6 while ownerless ones average 1.8, and companies spending 25 million dollars or more on responsible practices are far likelier to report profits from Artificial Intelligence above 5 percent of earnings.

## Where used

The findings apply to any organization moving Artificial Intelligence from experiments into daily operations, especially banks, telecoms, technology firms, hospitals, and government agencies where agents touch money, private data, or safety. The regional result — Asia–Pacific ahead, but every region showing the same governance lag — means the advice travels: fix ownership and training before chasing more agents. The barrier ranking is also practical guidance: since security and risk worries block scaling more than regulation or technical limits, budgets should flow to safety testing, monitoring, and incident response rather than to faster model adoption.

## Takeaways

First, trust is now the speed limit on Artificial Intelligence value, not a side project. Second, the scarcest resources are named owners and trained staff, not better models. Third, knowing the risks is worth little without mitigation: firms list dangers they do nothing about, then rate their own incident response poorly. Fourth, spending on responsibility correlates with profit, which reframes governance from a tax into an investment. Fifth, the window matters: building trustworthy habits early decides who captures lasting value, because retrofitting oversight onto sprawling agent fleets is far harder.

## Jargon decoder

- Responsible Artificial Intelligence: the set of practices that keep Artificial Intelligence lawful, safe, and fair, covering strategy, risk controls, data care, governance, and agent oversight.
- Agentic Artificial Intelligence: Artificial Intelligence that perceives, decides, and acts toward goals across software tools with limited human supervision.
- Maturity model: a 0–4 grading scale describing how complete an organization's practices are, from ad hoc beginnings to proactive programs.
- Governance: who decides, who is accountable, and what rules bind Artificial Intelligence work.
- Mitigation gap: the distance between naming a risk as relevant and actually running controls against it.
- EBIT impact: profit effect measured as earnings before interest and taxes, used here as proof that responsibility spending pays.
