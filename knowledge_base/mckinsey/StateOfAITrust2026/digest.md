# Digest — State of AI trust in 2026: Shifting to the agentic era

## 1. [[wiki/01-survey-and-maturity-model|Survey design and the AI Trust Maturity Model]]

**In one sentence:** McKinsey's 2026 AI Trust Maturity Survey of about 500 organizations scores responsible Artificial Intelligence practices on a 0–4 scale across five dimensions, and finds overall maturity rising even as strategy, governance, and agentic controls lag.

- The survey ran December 2025–January 2026 across roughly 500 organizations, with respondents holding direct responsibility or expertise in Artificial Intelligence governance, risk management, or Artificial Intelligence investment decisions.
- Responses are scored with the McKinsey Artificial Intelligence Trust Maturity Model across five dimensions of responsible Artificial Intelligence: strategy, risk management, data and technology, governance, and agentic Artificial Intelligence governance and controls, the last new this year.
- Maturity is ranked across four levels, from developing foundational practices to running a comprehensive and proactive program, on a 0–4 scale.
- The average maturity score rose to 2.3 in 2026 from 2.0 in 2025, a 0.3-point gain that still leaves the typical organization mid-journey rather than mature.
- Only about 30 percent of organizations reach level 3 or higher in strategy, governance, and agentic Artificial Intelligence governance, the three dimensions that provide alignment and oversight.
- The article frames trust as serving two outcomes: unlocking value from Artificial Intelligence investments through sustained adoption, and managing an expanding risk landscape as systems gain autonomy.
- The agentic shift reframes the trust problem from systems saying the wrong thing to systems doing the wrong thing, such as unintended actions, tool misuse, or operating beyond guardrails.

## 2. [[wiki/02-maturity-by-industry-region-and-investment|Maturity by industry, region, and investment]]

**In one sentence:** Technology, media, and telecom plus financial services lead on maturity, Asia–Pacific leads across regions, and organizations investing 25 million dollars or more in responsible Artificial Intelligence report markedly higher maturity and material business value.

- Technology, media, and telecommunications and financial services lead in maturity, driven by stronger risk management and data foundations.
- Asia–Pacific leads overall maturity across regions, while governance and agentic Artificial Intelligence controls trail data and technology in every region, a globally consistent governance gap.
- Organizations investing 25 million dollars or more in responsible Artificial Intelligence initiatives report significantly higher maturity scores than lower-spending peers.
- Those large investors are far more likely to realize material Artificial Intelligence benefits, including earnings before interest and taxes impact above 5 percent.
- The article presents this as evidence that responsible Artificial Intelligence spending enables rather than taxes innovation, calling it "a key enabler of sustained value creation".
- The regional pattern implies the governance shortfall is structural and worldwide, not a quirk of one market's regulation or culture.
- Industry leadership by regulated and data-heavy sectors suggests prior risk-management muscle transfers directly into Artificial Intelligence trust maturity.

## 3. [[wiki/03-barriers-and-top-risks|Blockers to agentic scale and the top-cited risks]]

**In one sentence:** Nearly two-thirds of respondents name security and risk worries as the top barrier to scaling agentic Artificial Intelligence, while inaccuracy at 74 percent and cybersecurity at 72 percent remain the most cited Artificial Intelligence risks overall.

- Nearly two-thirds of respondents cite security and risk concerns as the top barrier to fully scaling agentic Artificial Intelligence, well ahead of regulatory uncertainty or technical limits.
- The binding constraint is therefore confidence in safe deployment at scale, not the ability to run experiments or build agents.
- 74 percent of respondents rate inaccuracy as a highly relevant risk and 72 percent say the same for cybersecurity, the two most cited risks as adoption grows.
- Traditional model risks stay foundational even as newer agentic risks appear, because autonomy widens the threat surface without removing the old failure modes.
- Organizations must manage both the classic risks and the autonomy-driven ones at the same time rather than trading one program for the other.
- The ranking implies governance and assurance work, not model capability work, is what unlocks the next wave of deployment.
- Regulatory uncertainty scoring below security and risk concerns suggests firms see their own readiness gap as bigger than the policy gap.

## 4. [[wiki/04-awareness-mitigation-and-incidents|The mitigation gap and incident response]]

**In one sentence:** Awareness of Artificial Intelligence risks runs well ahead of active mitigation, incident frequency holds steady near 8 percent, yet almost 60 percent of affected respondents rate their organization's response as merely satisfactory or negative.

- Across almost all risk types there is a meaningful gap between risks deemed relevant and risks actively mitigated, with the gap widest for intellectual property infringement and personal privacy.
- Awareness is outpacing controls, processes, and tooling, so risk registers describe dangers the organization is not yet equipped to manage.
- The share of organizations reporting Artificial Intelligence incidents holds steady at roughly 8 percent, showing no surge in frequency.
- Among those that experienced incidents, almost 60 percent report satisfactory or negative views of their organization's response, a deterioration in perceived response quality.
- Preparedness and response capacity are failing to keep pace with growing system complexity even though incidents are not rising.
- Privacy and intellectual property stand out as the categories where talk most exceeds action, pointing to missing ownership or tooling rather than missing awareness.
- The pattern reframes the problem from detection to readiness: firms see the risks and still cannot respond well when they materialize.

## 5. [[wiki/05-talent-ownership-and-accountability|Skills gaps and accountability]]

**In one sentence:** Nearly 60 percent name knowledge and training gaps as the top barrier to implementing responsible Artificial Intelligence, while organizations with explicit ownership score 2.6 on maturity against just 1.8 for those without clear accountability.

- Nearly 60 percent of respondents cite knowledge and training gaps as the primary barrier to implementing responsible practices, up from about 50 percent last year, making it the leading blocker.
- Executive support has improved, yet firms still lack the skills, awareness, and operational muscle to embed responsible Artificial Intelligence consistently across teams.
- Organizations with clear ownership, especially Artificial Intelligence-specific governance roles or internal audit and ethics teams, average 2.6 on maturity, the highest of any accountability structure.
- Organizations with no clearly accountable function average just 1.8, a 0.8-point gap that the article ties to explicit ownership and decision rights.
- The training gap widening year over year suggests demand for responsible-Artificial Intelligence skills is growing faster than supply or internal upskilling.
- Audit and ethics teams scoring at the top implies independent oversight functions adapt well to Artificial Intelligence governance when given the mandate.
- Together the two findings prescribe the fix: named owners plus broad workforce training, since leadership support alone does not reach the teams doing the work.

## 6. [[wiki/06-trust-as-business-enabler|Trust as business enabler and the road ahead]]

**In one sentence:** Respondents report positive business, efficiency, and customer-trust outcomes from Artificial Intelligence trust work more often than negative ones, regulatory pressure as a motive is fading, and the article closes by arguing early investment in trustworthy innovation decides who captures long-term agentic value.

- Respondents report improvements in business outcomes, operational efficiency, and customer trust more frequently than negative outcomes from trust initiatives.
- The perceived influence of some regulatory frameworks has declined, signaling a move from compliance-led to value- and performance-driven motives for trust work.
- The closing argument warns that governance and risk gaps will grow more costly as systems become more autonomous and embedded in critical workflows.
- Organizations lacking accountability, controls, and monitoring face slower adoption, higher incident impact, and weaker stakeholder trust.
- Trust capability cannot be bought and installed; it demands a combined program of policies, processes, people, and technology.
- The prize is framed as building foundations for "agents, robots, and people to work together in new ways", the human-machine collaboration of the agentic era.
- The verdict: "building a trustworthy innovation engine early will define those who capture long-term value from Artificial Intelligence in the agentic era."

## The argument in five moves

1. Trust maturity is rising (2.3 from 2.0) but oversight dimensions lag badly.
2. Money and ownership predict maturity: $25M+ spenders and named owners lead.
3. Fear of unsafe deployment, not technology, blocks agentic scale.
4. Firms see risks, under-mitigate them, and respond to incidents poorly.
5. Trust is shifting from compliance chore to value engine that decides winners.
