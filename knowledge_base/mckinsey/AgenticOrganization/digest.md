# AgenticOrganization — Digest

The whole source at medium depth: every chapter's headline claim and key points, in order. ~10 min. Descend into a wiki page only where you need the detail.

## 1. [[wiki/01-paradox-pillars|The AI paradox and first pillars]]

**In one sentence:** McKinsey's Krivkovich argues companies face an AI paradox — heavy investment with little bottom-line impact — and the way out is redesigning the business model, team structures, and end-to-end workflows for an agentic era.

- More than 80 percent of companies say they are not yet seeing bottom-line impact from AI investments, despite expecting massive transformation (Krivkovich).
- The first of five pillars of the agentic organization is the business model: near-zero marginal cost of delivery enables hypersegmentation down to the "unit of one."
- Customer-side agents (e.g., an agent moving money across bank accounts for the best rate) could dissolve long-standing moats such as those in financial services (Krivkovich).
- Agentic AI adds "superhuman capabilities" to teams, but requires changed daily workflows, agent oversight, and new governance and risk controls (Krivkovich).
- Org-chart answers differ by domain: pharma envisions huge agent squads supercharging R&D scientists, while HR, finance, and legal aim to transform cost-center execution work.
- Companies added at least one layer (often two to three) in the past decade, slowing decisions; AI-enabled broader spans could let structures flatten and speed up.
- 75 percent of roles need fundamental reshaping in the next two to three years, and nearly half of leaders report skill gaps — real value comes from reimagining whole workflows (e.g., insurance underwriting, hire-to-onboard), not point solutions.

## 2. [[wiki/02-pillars-close|Remaining pillars and close]]

**In one sentence:** Krivkovich argues the agentic shift demands leaders transform how they spend their own time, move humans from in-the-loop to above-the-loop judgment, and rebuild talent and culture around continuous learning and reversible experimentation.

- Krivkovich describes two live tech-org models for enablement: central SWAT-type teams drawn from tech versus embedding AI expertise inside each business area.
- Leaders must ask whether AI changes 50 percent of how they spend their time or whether they are merely dabbling — tightening an email here, querying there — and talk openly about that change.
- A large trust gap remains: real hallucinations and "slop" from poor AI use create more work, so leaders must pair excitement with judgment, risk management, and an explicit learning-mode posture.
- On job fear, Krivkovich cites the line "you won't be replaced by AI, but you may be replaced by someone who embraces AI before you do," pushing reskilling and adjacent-role moves.
- The American Arbitration Association example shows agents trained on closed case files assembling timelines from hundreds to thousands of data points — photographic exhibits, contracts, emails — reviewing both sides and drafting a summary decision, sometimes better than the old process, with a human judging the outcome.
- Junior development is the "billion-dollar question": eliminating every new software engineer leaves an expensive seniors-only model and, rolled forward ten years, no next generation of talent.
- Culture winners foster curiosity, joint problem-solving with optimism not fear, and highly iterative two-way-door experiments they can pull back from rather than one-way bets that risk the company.

## The argument in five moves

1. Companies face an AI paradox of heavy investment with little bottom-line impact.
2. The way out is rebuilding the business model around near-zero marginal cost and hypersegmentation.
3. Teams gain superhuman capabilities only by redesigning end-to-end workflows with agent oversight and governance.
4. Org structures flatten to broader spans while enablement runs via central teams or embedded expertise.
5. Leaders must change how they spend their time and move humans above-the-loop in judgment.
6. Talent and culture close the gap through reskilling, continuous learning, and reversible experimentation.
