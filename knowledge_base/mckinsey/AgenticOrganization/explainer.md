# AI is everywhere. The agentic organization isn't — yet — In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Imagine a company buys expensive gym equipment for everyone, but nobody
changes their daily routine. The equipment sits there, and fitness
does not improve. That is the situation McKinsey's Krivkovich describes
with AI: heavy investment, but more than 80 percent of companies say
they are not yet seeing bottom-line impact.

The way out, in this view, is not another tool or pilot. It is
redesigning the business model, team structures, and end-to-end
workflows for what she calls the agentic era. Agents add superhuman
capabilities to teams, but only if daily work, oversight, and risk
controls change with them.

## Why does it matter?

Because the economics could change what a business can offer. With
near-zero marginal cost of delivery, a company can hypersegment down
to the "unit of one" — a product or service shaped for each person,
not for an average group.

That same shift can dissolve old defenses. The digest gives the
example of a customer-side agent moving money across bank accounts
for the best rate, which could weaken long-standing moats in areas
like financial services.

Inside companies, the pressure is already visible. Structures added
at least one layer, often two to three, in the past decade, slowing
decisions. About 75 percent of roles need fundamental reshaping in
the next two to three years, and nearly half of leaders report skill
gaps. Point solutions do not fix that; reimagined workflows such as
insurance underwriting or hire-to-onboard do.

## How does it work?

1. Start from the paradox: admit the tools are here but the payoff
   is missing, so the target is bottom-line impact, not demos.
2. Redesign the business model for near-zero marginal cost and
   hypersegmentation to the "unit of one."
3. Expect customer-side agents that act for the buyer, as in the
   bank-accounts-for-best-rate example, and plan for weaker moats.
4. Change daily team workflows and add agent oversight plus new
   governance and risk controls around superhuman capabilities.
5. Pick an org shape for the domain: huge agent squads supercharging
   specialists, as in pharma R&D, or transformed execution work in
   cost centers such as HR, finance, and legal.
6. Flatten decision speed: use broader spans to remove extra layers
   added over the past decade.
7. Rebuild whole workflows end to end instead of adding point
   solutions, because most roles need reshaping and skill gaps persist.
8. Change how leaders spend time, asking if AI changes 50 percent of
   their week or is just dabbling with emails and queries.
9. Move humans from in-the-loop checking to above-the-loop judgment,
   keeping excitement paired with risk management and learning-mode.
10. Grow talent and culture through reskilling, adjacent-role moves,
    curiosity, joint problem-solving, and reversible two-way-door
    experiments rather than one-way bets.

## Where can this be used?

Pharma research is one picture: large agent squads that supercharge
R&D scientists instead of replacing them.

HR, finance, and legal show another: transforming cost-center
execution work so routine processing gets faster and more consistent.

Whole-workflow redesigns like insurance underwriting and
hire-to-onboard show where value comes from fixing the full chain,
not one step.

The American Arbitration Association example shows agents trained on
closed case files assembling timelines from hundreds to thousands of
data points — exhibits, contracts, emails — reviewing both sides and
drafting a summary decision for a human to judge, sometimes better
than the old process.

Tech organizations show two enablement paths: a central SWAT-type
team drawn from tech, or AI expertise embedded inside each business
area.

## Conclusions & takeaways

The core message is simple: stop bolting AI onto old work. Redesign
the model, the teams, and the workflows, or the paradox continues.

Trust is the brake. Real hallucinations and "slop" from poor AI use
create more work, so leaders must stay open about limits and keep
judgment and risk management close.

People remain central. The line quoted is "you won't be replaced by
AI, but you may be replaced by someone who embraces AI before you
do," which points to reskilling and moves into adjacent roles.

Do not hollow out the future. Eliminating every new software engineer
leaves an expensive seniors-only model and, ten years out, no next
generation — the "billion-dollar question" for junior development.

Favor curiosity, optimism over fear, and small reversible experiments
you can pull back from instead of risky one-way bets.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | What it means in plain language |
| --- | --- |
| AI paradox | Spending a lot on AI but seeing little bottom-line payoff |
| Agentic organization | A company redesigned around agents in models, teams, and workflows |
| Business model | How the company creates and earns value from what it delivers |
| Near-zero marginal cost | Serving one more customer costs almost nothing extra |
| Hypersegmentation / unit of one | Tailoring offers down to each individual person |
| Moat | A lasting advantage that protects a business from rivals |
| Agent oversight | People checking and steering what agents do |
| Above-the-loop | Humans judge outcomes rather than checking every step |
| SWAT team vs embedded | Central AI helpers versus AI experts placed in each team |
| Hallucination / slop | Confident but wrong output, or low-quality output that adds work |
| Two-way-door experiment | A reversible trial you can undo if it fails |
| Reskilling | Learning new skills to move into changed or adjacent roles |
