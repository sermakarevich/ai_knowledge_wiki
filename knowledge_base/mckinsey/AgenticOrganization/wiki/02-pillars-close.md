# Remaining pillars and close

**In one sentence:** Krivkovich argues the agentic shift demands leaders transform how they spend their own time, move humans from in-the-loop to above-the-loop judgment, and rebuild talent and culture around continuous learning and reversible experimentation.

## Key points
- Krivkovich describes two live tech-org models for enablement: central SWAT-type teams drawn from tech versus embedding AI expertise inside each business area.
- Leaders must ask whether AI changes 50 percent of how they spend their time or whether they are merely dabbling — tightening an email here, querying there — and talk openly about that change.
- A large trust gap remains: real hallucinations and "slop" from poor AI use create more work, so leaders must pair excitement with judgment, risk management, and an explicit learning-mode posture.
- On job fear, Krivkovich cites the line "you won't be replaced by AI, but you may be replaced by someone who embraces AI before you do," pushing reskilling and adjacent-role moves.
- The American Arbitration Association example shows agents trained on closed case files assembling timelines from hundreds to thousands of data points — photographic exhibits, contracts, emails — reviewing both sides and drafting a summary decision, sometimes better than the old process, with a human judging the outcome.
- Junior development is the "billion-dollar question": eliminating every new software engineer leaves an expensive seniors-only model and, rolled forward ten years, no next generation of talent.
- Culture winners foster curiosity, joint problem-solving with optimism not fear, and highly iterative two-way-door experiments they can pull back from rather than one-way bets that risk the company.

---

## Tech function organization

Krivkovich (answering Rahilly on org of the tech function) says different models are playing out: many companies create SWAT-type teams drawn from within tech that business units can draw on to enable change, while others embed expertise inside each area of the business. She frames it as the classic difficulty of changing the tech function itself while transforming the organization at the same time.

## Leadership in the paradigm shift

Rahilly notes the research calls this the biggest paradigm shift since the industrial and digital revolutions and asks how executives should lead change at that scale. Krivkovich responds with two leadership questions: how do leaders need to show up differently, and are they radically transforming themselves first — one leader's formulation being "if this will radically transform everything about our business, we as a leadership team need to start by radically transforming ourselves." The concrete test she offers is whether 50 percent of a leader's time is spent differently because of AI access, versus dabbling without rethinking the hours of the day, and whether leaders narrate that shift inside the organization.

## Trust gap and fear

Krivkovich stresses a huge trust gap: early experimentation has real faults, the hallucination stories in headlines are real, and "slop" moving around a company through poor AI use creates more work, not less. Leaders therefore must get people excited about what is possible while overlaying good judgment and risk management, and acknowledge the organization is in learning mode rather than mature deployment. On fear-driven resistance about job loss, she says the technology will not stop and repeats the line that you will not be replaced by AI but may be replaced by someone who embraces AI before you do; the leader's job is to get employees excited about new skills and tooling that make them more successful, including adjacent roles when the current role transforms dramatically.

## Judgment as the job: in the loop vs above the loop

Asked about essential new talent profiles, Krivkovich says expected skill impacts (AI is strong at research, math, data science) sit alongside rising demand for strategic thinking, systems orientation, people-management soft skills, oversight, judgment, problem-solving, and the operational capabilities human leaders provide as the overlay. Rahilly flags the piece's shift from humans "in the loop" to humans "above the loop," and Krivkovich confirms both will exist: in-the-loop means agents do pieces of a process and hand off to humans for other pieces, while above-the-loop means teams of AI agents do most or all of the core process and the human role becomes judgment on top. Her example is the reimagined American Arbitration Association case-review process: the traditional path gathers hundreds if not thousands of data points, including photographic exhibits of contracts and email exchanges, reviews the file, and decides per agreement terms over a long time; trained on closed case files, agents assemble the timeline, review the fact base, weigh both sides, and come to a summary decision — in some cases better than before — while a human still must look at the whole and ask "do I agree with the decision," preserving the critical judgment layer.

## Juniors and perpetual change

Rahilly asks how juniors develop oversight judgment without the "hazing grunt work" of the pre-agentic era. Krivkovich calls this the billion-dollar question: eliminating every new software engineer yields a very expensive seniors-only model and, ten years out, a missing next generation. She argues learning and development, today a periodic sidecar that is valued but not central, must move to the center of the employee journey, since the next generation gets day-one access to the tools — lacking 20 years of pattern recognition from reviewing case law but also lacking the hurdle of mid-career disruption. Leaders should ask how to make that day-one access an advantage. Capability building must also teach that change management is no longer episodic but a perpetual state, requiring comfort with constant change without introducing chaos and risk.

## Talent shape: pods and fluid flows

On the talent hierarchy, Krivkovich says it is too early to name a final shape, though AI will absolutely enable change. She cites excitement about organizing around pods of work — forming, reforming, reusable, moving nimbly — as opposed to traditional job hierarchies, but notes it is hard in practice because hierarchies are needed for evaluation and managerial support, so most companies remain far from reorganizing on that principle. The nearer-term shift is increasing fluid talent flows, and organizations whose HR function enables and supports nimbler movement of talent will hold a competitive advantage.

## Culture and close

Asked about culture, Krivkovich says organizations fostering curiosity and continuous learning are well placed because so much remains unknown and experimentation is required. At a premium will be people who do joint problem-solving across the organization and coach others into new models with optimism not fear, plus people who balance risk-taking against not betting the company. The operating mode must be highly iterative: two-way doors, not one-way doors — experiments to explore, pull back if they fail, and move to the next pathway. Rahilly thanks her and the conversation closes.
