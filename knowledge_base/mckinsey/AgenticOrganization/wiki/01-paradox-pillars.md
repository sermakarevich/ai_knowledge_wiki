# The AI paradox and first pillars

**In one sentence:** McKinsey's Krivkovich argues companies face an AI paradox — heavy investment with little bottom-line impact — and the way out is redesigning the business model, team structures, and end-to-end workflows for an agentic era.

## Key points

- More than 80 percent of companies say they are not yet seeing bottom-line impact from AI investments, despite expecting massive transformation (Krivkovich).
- The first of five pillars of the agentic organization is the business model: near-zero marginal cost of delivery enables hypersegmentation down to the "unit of one."
- Customer-side agents (e.g., an agent moving money across bank accounts for the best rate) could dissolve long-standing moats such as those in financial services (Krivkovich).
- Agentic AI adds "superhuman capabilities" to teams, but requires changed daily workflows, agent oversight, and new governance and risk controls (Krivkovich).
- Org-chart answers differ by domain: pharma envisions huge agent squads supercharging R&D scientists, while HR, finance, and legal aim to transform cost-center execution work.
- Companies added at least one layer (often two to three) in the past decade, slowing decisions; AI-enabled broader spans could let structures flatten and speed up.
- 75 percent of roles need fundamental reshaping in the next two to three years, and nearly half of leaders report skill gaps — real value comes from reimagining whole workflows (e.g., insurance underwriting, hire-to-onboard), not point solutions.

## The great AI paradox

Rahilly opens by noting companies invest in agentic AI without corresponding returns, and McKinsey research confirms how hard scale-up is. Krivkovich calls this "the great paradox": companies invested expecting massive transformation, yet over 80 percent report no bottom-line impact yet.

The leadership question, per Krivkovich, is how to "meet this moment and create the agentic organization of the future." Leaders feel on the precipice and wrestle with a connected set of questions: how roles change, which future skills are needed, how to bring the workforce along with excitement rather than fear, and how to drive change into every corner of the organization.

## Pillar 1: the business model

Rahilly introduces the research frame — five pillars of the agentic organization — and starts with the business model. Krivkovich poses a thought experiment: suppose delivery moves toward near-zero marginal cost. That changes what a company can offer customers, how finely it can tailor and hypersegment to the "unit of one," and how it mobilizes growth.

His example is a tech content-distribution player tailoring experience to the individual across millions of consumers, opening new ways to create and enable commerce. Pushed one step further: what if the small business or individual on the receiving end also has agents acting on their behalf? He gives the example of a customer agent moving money frictionlessly across bank accounts to chase the best rate — something not done today only because the process is too hard. If that friction disappears, Krivkovich says, the moat that has protected financial services "since the beginning of time" fundamentally changes, and with it both the opportunity and the competitive threat embedded in the business model.

## Rethinking teams for the agentic era

Rahilly, noting Krivkovich leads the People & Organizational Performance Practice, asks how team structures function in the agentic era. Krivkovich draws a line between generative AI and agentic: the real promise of agentic is adding the equivalent of superhuman capabilities to teams. But capturing it requires fundamentally changing day-to-day workflows and the rituals of how work gets done — hours of the day happen differently, teams oversee a population of agents, problem-solving as a team changes, all with governance and risk controls on top.

Most companies face this as new ground nearly everywhere at once, because use cases are so broad. On org charts specifically, Krivkovich cautions it is too early for one answer and that it will differ by domain:

- **Pharma / life sciences R&D:** companies envision huge squads of agents. Researchers and scientists stay on the org chart but are supercharged in innovation speed.
- **Corporate functions (HR, finance, legal):** seen as cost centers delivering critical execution rather than front-end value creation, so the hope is agentic capability transforms how that work gets done and who does it, freeing capacity elsewhere.

A structural tailwind: most companies added at least one layer from CEO to front line over the past decade, some two or three. That is expensive and slows decisions since more people weigh in. Krivkovich's hope is that AI gives leaders superhuman capacity to manage broader scopes, letting companies flatten and move faster.

## Skills and spans

Asked what leaders must do to manage broader spans of talent and oversee agentic tech, Krivkovich is blunt: just about everybody will need a new job description in the next two to three years. Most roles do not disappear but are reshaped — 75 percent of roles need fundamental reshaping right now, leaders and their reports alike.

On capability building, nearly half of leaders say they see skill gaps, and most say they would benefit from more training, capability building, and support — all the way up to senior leadership.

## What 'reimagining workflows' really means

Rahilly presses on the phrase "reimagining workflows end to end." Krivkovich says the best scalable-impact use cases reimagine an entire workflow, because such workflows cut across multiple teams and areas where traditionally much effort goes into connecting dots across touchpoints and people. Agentic is valuable precisely as part of that "connection stream" in a fast, multifaceted way.

The contrast he draws is point solutions (one task done better and faster with AI) versus whole workflows: rethinking insurance underwriting end to end, or the HR "hire to onboard" process end to end — "that's where we're seeing the magic start to happen."

That is deep, granular work. It means asking for each process: where does an agent help, where must a human be in the loop or above the loop to supervise, where is a team of agents needed, and how are agents made reusable so once trained they deploy in multiple places. Krivkovich calls this the systems thinking that unlocks opportunities at scale, requiring problem-solvers who can do that work and leaders who can pick ripe lighthouse use cases.

On team composition for such redesign, Krivkovich calls multi-level involvement one of the exciting opportunities: the potential runs up and down the chain of command, so people at all levels must rethink the process. A big unlock is executive function — reviewing work output. Human judgment stays, but quality control, review, and pattern recognition historically done by managers or senior leaders can increasingly run through the agentic process.
