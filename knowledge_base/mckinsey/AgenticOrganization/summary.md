# AI is everywhere. The agentic organization isn't — yet

**Article:** [AI is everywhere. The agentic organization isn't — yet](~/Downloads/ai-is-everywhere-the-agentic-organization-isnt-yet_FINAL.pdf) — McKinsey Podcast transcript, April 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

McKinsey's Aneesh Rahilly interviews Bryan Krivkovich (People & Organizational Performance Practice) about the "AI paradox": over 80 percent of companies invest heavily in AI but see no bottom-line impact. Krivkovich's answer is to redesign five pillars — business model (near-zero marginal cost, hypersegmentation to the "unit of one"), team structures (superhuman capabilities need new workflows, oversight, and governance), org shape (flatter spans, domain-specific answers like pharma agent squads vs. transformed HR/finance/legal execution), leadership (spend 50 percent of your time differently, not dabbling), and talent/culture (move humans from in-the-loop to above-the-loop judgment, reskill instead of replace, run reversible two-way-door experiments). The throughline: point solutions don't capture value — only reimagining whole workflows end to end does.

## TL;DR

Krivkovich frames the paradox as a leadership challenge, not a technology gap. Pillar one, the business model, hinges on near-zero marginal delivery cost enabling hypersegmentation — illustrated by a customer-side agent that shops for the best bank rate, which could dissolve financial-services moats built on friction. Pillar two, teams, requires changing daily workflows and adding agent oversight, not just adopting generative AI. Org structure has added layers over the past decade that slow decisions; AI-enabled broader spans could flatten them, but the right shape differs by domain (huge agent squads in pharma R&D vs. transformed cost-center execution in HR/finance/legal). Leadership must model the change themselves — asking whether AI touches 50 percent of their own time — while managing a real trust gap from hallucinations and "slop." The American Arbitration Association case shows what above-the-loop judgment looks like in practice: agents assemble timelines from thousands of data points and draft a decision, a human judges the outcome. The hardest open problem is junior talent: eliminating entry-level engineering work saves money now but breaks the pipeline that produces future seniors. Krivkovich closes on culture — curiosity, joint problem-solving, and reversible experiments beat one-way bets.

## Problem & Motivation

Companies invested in AI expecting massive transformation, yet over 80 percent report no bottom-line impact — McKinsey's Krivkovich calls this "the great paradox." Leaders feel on the precipice, wrestling with how roles change, which skills the future needs, how to bring the workforce along with excitement rather than fear, and how to drive change into every corner. Point solutions — one task done better and faster — don't close the gap; only reimagining entire end-to-end workflows (insurance underwriting, hire-to-onboard) captures scalable value, because such workflows cut across teams where effort goes into connecting dots. The interview offers a practitioner's five-pillar answer to what must change beyond buying tools.

---

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Business model under near-zero marginal cost** — If delivery cost tends to zero, firms can hypersegment to the "unit of one." Customer-side agents (an agent moving money across banks for the best rate) could dissolve moats built on friction — financial services' protection "since the beginning of time" — turning the business model into both opportunity and threat.
2. **Teams redesigned for superhuman capabilities** — Agentic AI adds superhuman capacity, but capturing it means changing daily workflows and rituals, overseeing a population of agents, and adding governance and risk controls. Nearly half of leaders report skill gaps; ~75 percent of roles need fundamental reshaping within 2–3 years.
3. **Flatter orgs, domain-specific shapes** — A decade of added layers slowed decisions; AI-enabled broader spans could flatten them. The right shape differs by domain: huge agent squads supercharging pharma R&D scientists versus transformed execution in HR/finance/legal cost centers. Two live tech-org models: central SWAT teams versus expertise embedded per business area.
4. **Leadership starts with itself** — "If this will radically transform everything, we must start by radically transforming ourselves." The test: does AI change 50 percent of how a leader spends time, or is it dabbling? Leaders must narrate the shift, pair excitement with judgment over hallucinations and "slop," and treat the org as in learning mode.
5. **Above-the-loop judgment as the human job** — In-the-loop (agents do pieces, humans do the rest) gives way to above-the-loop (agent teams run the core process, humans judge the outcome). The American Arbitration Association case: agents trained on closed files assemble timelines from thousands of data points and draft summary decisions — sometimes better than before — while a human asks "do I agree."
6. **The junior-talent billion-dollar question** — Eliminating entry-level engineering saves money now but yields an expensive seniors-only model and, ten years out, no next generation. Learning and development must move from periodic sidecar to the center of the employee journey; nearer-term, fluid talent flows and work pods over rigid hierarchies.

## Key Findings

- The paradox is quantified: 80%+ of companies see no bottom-line AI impact despite heavy investment; ~75% of roles need reshaping; ~half of leaders see skill gaps.
- Whole-workflow redesign (underwriting, hire-to-onboard, arbitration review) is where "the magic starts to happen"; point solutions don't move the needle.
- The AAA case is the concrete above-the-loop proof: agent-assembled timelines and draft decisions from photographic exhibits, contracts, and emails, with human judgment preserved on top.
- Culture winners share three traits: curiosity with continuous learning, joint problem-solving with optimism not fear, and iterative two-way-door experiments rather than company-betting one-way doors.

---

## Suggestions & Future Directions

1. **Pick lighthouse workflows and redesign them end to end** — For each process ask where agents help, where humans stay in/above the loop, where agent teams are needed, and how agents become reusable across deployments.
2. **Make leaders go first** — Apply the 50-percent-time test to the leadership team and talk openly about it inside the organization.
3. **Solve the junior pipeline deliberately** — Turn day-one AI access for juniors into an advantage via centered learning and development, instead of trading the future talent supply for present savings.
4. **Run reversible experiments** — Two-way doors: explore, pull back on failure, move to the next pathway; balance risk-taking against not betting the company.
