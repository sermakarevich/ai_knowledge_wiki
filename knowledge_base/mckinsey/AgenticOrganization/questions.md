# AgenticOrganization — Retrieval Practice

## Section 1: The AI paradox and first pillars

### Q1 (core recall). What is the "AI paradox," and what share of companies report it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Heavy investment in AI with little bottom-line impact: more than **80 percent** of companies say they are not yet seeing bottom-line impact from AI investments, despite expecting massive transformation (Krivkovich). The way out is redesigning the business model, team structures, and end-to-end workflows — not just adding tools.

</details>

### Q2 (core recall). What does the first pillar (business model) claim, and what workforce numbers go with the workflow-redesign argument?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

- Business model pillar: near-**zero marginal cost** of delivery enables hypersegmentation down to the **"unit of one."**
- Example: customer-side agents (e.g., an agent moving money across bank accounts for the best rate) could dissolve long-standing moats such as those in **financial services**.
- Workforce numbers: **75 percent** of roles need fundamental reshaping in the next **two to three years**, and **nearly half** of leaders report skill gaps — so real value comes from reimagining whole workflows (e.g., insurance underwriting, hire-to-onboard), not point solutions.

</details>

### Q3 (elaboration). Why do point solutions fail to capture AI value, and what breaks if org structures stay layered?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Point solutions fail because value comes from reimagining **whole end-to-end workflows** (underwriting, hire-to-onboard) — a point tool leaves the surrounding handoffs, roles, and governance unchanged. On structure: companies added **at least one layer (often two to three)** in the past decade, slowing decisions; without AI-enabled broader spans and flatter structures, agent-augmented teams ("superhuman capabilities" plus agent oversight and new risk controls) stay bottlenecked by slow approvals. Domain answers differ: pharma envisions huge agent squads supercharging R&D scientists, while HR/finance/legal target cost-center execution work.

</details>

## Section 2: Remaining pillars and close

### Q4 (core recall). What "50 percent" question must leaders ask themselves, and what trust gap must they manage?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Leaders must ask whether AI changes **50 percent** of how they spend their own time — or whether they are merely dabbling (tightening an email here, querying there) — and talk openly about that change. The trust gap: real **hallucinations and "slop"** from poor AI use create more work, so leaders must pair excitement with judgment, risk management, and an explicit **learning-mode** posture. Two live enablement models: central **SWAT-type** teams drawn from tech vs. embedding AI expertise inside each business area.

</details>

### Q5 (elaboration). What breaks if a company eliminates all junior software engineers — and why is it a "ten-year" problem?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cutting every new/junior engineer leaves an expensive **seniors-only** model today and, rolled forward **ten years**, no next generation of talent — the "billion-dollar question" of junior development. It breaks the talent pipeline: seniors are costly now, and without juniors learning and growing there is nobody to become the senior cohort later. Krivkovich's counter to job fear: "you won't be replaced by AI, but you may be replaced by someone who embraces AI before you do" — push reskilling and adjacent-role moves instead. Contrast the AAA pattern: agents trained on closed case files assemble timelines from **hundreds to thousands** of data points (exhibits, contracts, emails), draft a summary decision, and a human judges the outcome — humans moved **above-the-loop**, not removed.

</details>

### Q6 (transfer). Your team proposes one big irreversible AI rollout. How would you redesign it using this digest's culture and judgment lessons?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Replace the one-way bet with highly iterative **two-way-door experiments** you can pull back from, plus curiosity and joint problem-solving with optimism not fear. Keep humans **above-the-loop** (judgment on outcomes, as in the AAA summary-decision example) with explicit governance/risk controls and learning-mode posture, since hallucinations/slop create rework. Have leaders model it: name what share of their own time changes (the 50-percent test), pick one end-to-end workflow to reimagine (not a point tool), and plan reskilling/adjacent moves so the team embraces AI rather than fearing replacement.

</details>

Cite the survey, not the podcast, when the number matters.

</details>
