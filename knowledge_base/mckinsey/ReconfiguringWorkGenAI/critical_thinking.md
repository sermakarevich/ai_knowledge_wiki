# Critical Thinking: Reconfiguring Work

## Claims versus evidence

The article's load-bearing empirical claims arrive with numbers attached: two-thirds adoption, 3x shadow use, the 2/7/21–30 percent participation ladder tied to excess total shareholder returns (TSR, stock gains above the market), the 62/50/22 percent age-expertise split, and the Lilli dashboard (July 2023 launch, 92 percent ever-used, 74 percent regular, 30 percent-plus time saved on gathering and synthesis, ~19 million prompts, ~150 central plus ~17,000 federated agents). These read as McKinsey survey and internal-telemetry evidence, but the reconstruction carries no visible sample sizes, dates, or methodologies for the survey figures — a reader cannot check the 7-percent doubling claim or the generational split without the underlying reports. The honest caveats the piece does include (mixed structured/unstructured training "remains challenging," monitoring frameworks "still emerging," "not everyone will make the transition smoothly") are real but brief. Strongest-evidenced: Lilli's internal metrics, which are specific, dated, and falsifiable. Weakest: the MVO (minimum viable organization, ultra-lean AI-run unit) future — invoice processing and sales hints are plausible illustrations, not documented transformations.

## Genuinely new versus repackaged

Three ideas feel genuinely of the Gen AI moment: the tool-versus-capability reframe (Gen AI as medium of work, not software to install), the "marketplace of information" as an unstructured-data advantage, and the federated agent result — 17,000 employee-built agents with no marginal tech debt, which inverts the standard IT-governance story. The MVO-versus-augmented-team portfolio is a sharp new packaging of an old decentralization question, and "middle-out" change gives a fresh name to the familiar insight that middle managers carry transformations. The rest — North Star visions, two-in-the-box partnerships, oversight committees, superuser networks, reskilling academies — is classic change management relabeled for the Gen AI age. That is not a dismissal: the article's contribution is synthesis into one CEO-readable sequence, not novel science.

## Weaknesses

First, survivorship and self-interest: the deepest case is McKinsey studying McKinsey, and the firm sells exactly the transformations it prescribes — Lilli's triumphs are unaudited by outsiders. Second, costs are invisible: no price tag for Lilli, the Singtel academy, MVO monitoring stacks, or the reskilling of displaced workers, so no reader can compute return on investment. Third, the hard parts are hurried past: what happens to the workers an MVO displaces if reskilling fails, how human-in-the-loop checkpoints scale when agents number in the thousands, and who is liable when a grounded-but-wrong answer drives a bad decision. Fourth, the generational claim (millennials over Gen Z) is reported without any theory of why, inviting stereotype-driven talent decisions. Fifth, the evidence base is single-vendor: one consultancy's surveys plus two showcase cases (Lilli, Singtel) carry a universal five-step prescription for all industries and all firm sizes.

## Applicability

The playbook fits large, knowledge-heavy firms with real data estates, compliance functions, and training budgets — banks, insurers, telecoms, professional services — where the marketplace-of-information advantage and the academy model are affordable. It fits poorly where it is most needed: small firms without a chief data officer or legal team to staff an oversight committee, regulated settings where "open the data, then govern it" collides with privacy law, and frontline-heavy businesses where most work is physical rather than informational. The participation economics (push past 7 percent) assume a white-collar workforce that can be redeployed to "higher-value activities" — an assumption worth testing in any firm whose higher-value activities are themselves automatable.

## What this changes

Taken seriously, the article moves Gen AI budgets from licenses to reconfiguration: fewer seat purchases, more workflow redesign, governance staffing, and participation programs — with success metrics shifting from logins to involvement rates and exception-handling quality. It legitimizes employee-built automation as strategy rather than shadow IT, provided federated risk controls hold. And it reframes the CEO's job from technology sponsor to mobilizer-in-chief: the North Star, the visible personal tool use, and the honest communication about which units become MVOs are all non-delegable leadership acts.

## Verdict

A strong practitioner synthesis with a genuinely useful decision architecture (outcomes North Star, trust before scale, bolt-on versus rebuild, MVO-versus-augment portfolio, participation economics) and one outstanding worked example in Lilli — weakened by unaudited self-reported evidence, missing costs, and breezy treatment of displacement and accountability. Read it as a credible change-management hypothesis grounded in early-adopter experience, not as proven science; adopt the sequence, but demand the missing numbers (costs, methods, failure cases) before betting a workforce on it.
