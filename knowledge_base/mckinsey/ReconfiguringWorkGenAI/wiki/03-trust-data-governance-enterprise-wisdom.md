# Step 2: Trust Through Data, Governance, and Enterprise Wisdom

**In one sentence:** Nobody scales what nobody trusts, so leaders must ground generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI) in the company's own knowledge, open up data access, and wrap both in governance with human checks — because untrusted Gen AI output means untrusted Gen AI decisions and zero chance of scale.

## Key points

- Foundational trust is the precondition for scale: if employees do not trust Gen AI output, they will not trust the decisions it makes, and the technology cannot scale.
- The most trusted platforms are grounded in organizational context — they reveal how answers are derived and which sources were used — and should be augmented with institutional knowledge such as proprietary research, customer interaction logs, and decades of engineering know-how, plus dynamic external information.
- Leaders must treat data accessibility as a first-class work stream, because Gen AI's ability to consume unstructured data inside the firm's "marketplace of information" is a distinct competitive advantage for surfacing hidden insights and patterns.
- That advantage is hard to capture: training models on structured and unstructured data for high-quality trustworthy answers remains challenging, and the practices and frameworks for monitoring response quality are still emerging.
- Governance must be explicit and C-suite-owned: the chief executive with the chief information officer (CIO, the top technology executive) and chief data officer (CDO, the top data executive) prioritizes accessible data, then wraps it in an AI oversight committee, acceptable-use policies, compliance and risk guidelines built with risk and legal teams, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints.
- Human-in-the-loop checkpoints exist to catch hallucinations (confident false outputs), biases, and data leakage before they reach decisions.

---

## Why trust is the gating factor

The logic is a chain: use requires trust in output, decisions require trust in the system making them, and scale requires trusted decisions. "If employees don't trust gen AI output, they won't trust the decisions it makes. And, thus, the technology will have little chance of attaining scale." Trust is therefore not a communications exercise but the load-bearing precondition of the entire five-step program — which is why it comes second, before any workflow redesign.

## Grounding in enterprise wisdom

" The most trusted gen AI platforms are those grounded in an organization's own context, revealing how answers are derived and the sources used." Grounding means connecting models to verifiable company material: "proprietary research, customer interaction logs, or decades of engineering know-how," optionally "boosting this internal knowledge base with dynamic external information." The payoff is behavioral: "When gen AI delivers knowledgeable answers that users can trust, they will be far more apt to incorporate it into their daily workflows." Trustworthy answers convert skeptics into daily users.

## The marketplace of information

The article's most original image in this step is that "almost every organization has a 'marketplace of information' shaped by hierarchies and policies that define where information is housed and who can access it." Gen AI's edge is consuming the unstructured data locked in that marketplace — emails, documents, logs — to "extract hidden insights and patterns." But the honest caveat follows immediately: delivering "high-quality, trustworthy answers" from mixed structured and unstructured data "remains challenging," and monitoring practices "are still emerging, making it critical for leaders to establish trust now" — before failures calcify into distrust.

## The governance apparatus

The article assigns ownership precisely: "Together with the CEO, the CIO and chief data officer (CDO) can prioritize accessible data and then wrap robust AI governance around it." The required machinery is an AI oversight committee, acceptable-use policies, compliance and risk guidelines co-built with risk and legal teams, and "human-in-the-loop checkpoints to spot hallucinations, biases, or data leakage." Accessibility and control are deliberately paired — open the data, then govern it — resolving the tension between transparency for usefulness and restriction for safety.

**Covers:** trust as the precondition for scale, grounding in institutional knowledge, the "marketplace of information" and unstructured-data advantage, and the C-suite-owned governance apparatus with human-in-the-loop checks.
