# Step 3: Reimagining Workflows Toward AI Teams

**In one sentence:** Bolting generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI) onto existing processes yields incremental gains at best, so business and technology teams must jointly rebuild workflows around Gen AI through three phases — solo agents, supervised agent groups, autonomous swarms — while employees co-design the change and leaders make daily use a habit.

## Key points

- Bolting Gen AI onto existing processes "will not encourage widescale uptake and will deliver incremental, if any, impact," because Gen AI is a capability — a new way of thinking, working, and creating — not another enterprise software tool.
- Redesign must use a "two-in-the-box" approach pairing business teams (who guarantee the new way of working delivers business results) with technology teams (who guarantee technical feasibility).
- The evolution runs in three phases: stand-alone agents for discrete tasks, groups of agents completing end-to-end processes under human oversight, and fully automated agentic swarms acting independently as minimum viable organizations (MVOs, ultra-lean units delivering full business outcomes).
- Even in the final swarm phase people are not eliminated: a few employees keep MVOs safe and effective while many others are redeployed to higher-value activities.
- In the first two phases, leaders must involve employees directly — inviting them to build their own agents and flag where Gen AI belongs — and start with work streams where value is clear, feasibility is high, and required investment is manageable.
- McKinsey's own Lilli platform proves the habit thesis: since its July 2023 launch, 92 percent of global staff have used it, 74 percent use it regularly, it saves over 30 percent of time on information gathering and synthesis, and it has answered nearly 19 million prompts.

---

## Why bolting on fails

The step's central claim is causal, not aesthetic: grafting Gen AI onto unchanged processes fails because the technology's nature demands new processes. "Gen AI is not a tool like most enterprise software and is instead a capability — providing a new way of thinking, working, and creating." Old workflows encode pre-AI assumptions about who does what in which order; accelerating them with Gen AI preserves the inefficiency at higher speed. Only "putting gen AI at the center of workflows, entirely reconfiguring how work takes place" unlocks uptake and impact.

## Two-in-the-box change

" It is critical to use a two-in-the-box approach to change, where business and technology teams work together to define the new way of working." The division of labor is explicit: "Business teams ensure the new working mode delivers the expected business results, while the technology team ensures the feasibility of architecting the technical change." This guards against both failure modes — technically elegant redesigns nobody needs, and business wish-lists nobody can build.

## The three-phase evolution

Phase one is "stand-alone AI agents that humans use to complete very discrete tasks"; phase two is "groups of AI agents that complete full end-to-end processes overseen by humans"; phase three is "fully automated agentic swarms that act independently as MVOs to deliver full business outcomes." The phasing is a change-management device as much as a technical roadmap: it lets "people adapt to new ways of working" gradually, and it gates progress on the trust built in Step 2 — "effective change management during this three-phase evolution cannot begin without first building trust among employees that gen AI is a core capability required for their jobs." The reassurance is explicit: "Even in this last phase, people are not eliminated."

## Employee co-design and sequencing

" In the first two stages, involving employees directly in the process maximizes the change's impact": invite people to "create their own agents and provide feedback on areas where gen AI could be woven into their workflows," with the expectation that "all employees are part of the process." Sequencing matters for buy-in: pick "work streams where value is clear, feasibility is high, and the amount of investment required to make the change happen is manageable," because early wins that "make their jobs easier" convert skeptics.

## The Lilli proof: from hobby to habit

" Integrating gen AI into daily workflows moves it from a hobby to a habit — and encourages employees to see it as a team member, not a tool." McKinsey's internal platform Lilli is the worked example: leaders role-model by asking in every meeting "Have you asked Lilli?"; onboarding teaches it; firm-wide risk trainings reinforce proper use; continuous feature rollouts (firm-template slides, spreadsheets, draft client proposals) keep it daily-relevant. The numbers: "Since Lilli's launch in July 2023, 92 percent of McKinsey's global staff have used the AI platform, and 74 percent now use it regularly, saving more than 30 percent of their time on information gathering and synthesis. To date, Lilli has answered nearly 19 million prompts."
