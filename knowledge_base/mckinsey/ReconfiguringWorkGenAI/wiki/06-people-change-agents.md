# Step 5: People as Change Agents

**In one sentence:** Reconfiguration lives or dies on mass participation — involving far more than the usual 2 percent of employees, mobilizing millennial superusers as mentors, having leaders visibly use the tools, and letting staff build their own agents — as McKinsey's Lilli (92 percent adoption, ~17,000 employee-built agents) and Singtel's 10,000-person academy show.

## Key points

- Participation is the performance lever: typically only about 2 percent of employees join transformation efforts, but involving at least 7 percent doubles the chances of positive excess total shareholder returns (TSR, stock gains above the market), and top performers involve 21 to 30 percent.
- Gen AI ambassador programs matter more than in past technology rollouts because everyone is still discovering use cases together, so "superusers" who drive uptake must be found and supported as cultural change agents.
- Enthusiasm skews millennial, not Gen Z: 62 percent of employees aged 35 to 44 report high AI expertise, versus 50 percent of 18-to-24-year-olds and 22 percent of baby boomers over 65 — so CEOs should deploy millennial champions as peer mentors and practice-group leads.
- Change must run middle-out, not just top-down or bottom-up, with CEOs leading by example through visible personal use of Gen AI tools.
- McKinsey's adoption playbook — user segmentation with tailored training, superuser Lilli Clubs, one-on-one coaching for senior leaders, self-guided "ask Lilli" help — plus a federated agent model produced ~150 centrally built agents and nearly 17,000 employee-built ones with no added tech debt.
- Singtel's skills-first counterpart, the October 2024 AI Acceleration Academy with Nanyang Technological University and the National University of Singapore, trained more than 10,000 employees across roles to use data and Gen AI in their own workflows.

---

## The participation numbers

" On average, only about 2 percent of employees are directly involved in a typical transformation effort. Yet organizations that expand their participation see far better outcomes. In fact, companies involving at least 7 percent of employees in transformation initiatives double their chances of delivering positive excess total shareholder returns (TSR), with the highest performers involving 21 to 30 percent of employees." The article thus converts participation from a morale nicety into a shareholder-value predictor — and sets a quantitative bar (7 percent minimum) that most firms currently miss by a factor of three.

## Superusers and the millennial surprise

" Inviting employees to become gen AI ambassadors is even more important than in a typical technology transformation because we are all still learning the extent of gen AI's use cases and capabilities." The mechanism: identify "superusers" who "can become powerful change agents who boost overall uptake" and support them "to drive cultural change." The demographic surprise: "the most enthusiastic adopters of gen AI are millennial managers. Some 62 percent of employees aged 35 to 44 report high levels of expertise with AI, compared with 50 percent of 18-to-24-year-old Gen Zers and 22 percent of baby boomers over 65." The action: "encourage these millennial change champions to mentor their peers in gen AI adoption and lead practice groups to share tips and tricks."

## Middle-out change and role modeling

" Most critically, CEOs should lead by example, visibly using gen AI tools in their own work. This leads to more of a middle-out approach to change versus top-down or bottom-up." Middle-out is the article's distinctive change vector: energized middle managers (the millennial cohort) carry adoption sideways and downward, avoiding both executive-mandate resistance and grassroots fragmentation.

## The Lilli adoption machine

" At McKinsey, we established an adoption and engagement team to involve everyone in gen AI. First, we conducted segmentation analysis to classify user types and then tailored training to specific groups." Tactics included "Lilli Clubs composed of superusers who gathered to share tips and make suggestions on how to improve the platform and one-on-one training sessions for the most senior leaders," plus self-service design so users could "'ask Lilli' how to best use it." The federated payoff: "While there are about 150 centrally developed agents, the firm's federated development model (with built-in risk controls) has led employees to create nearly 17,000 additional agents so far. Lilli walks users through the agent building process and generates its own custom model instructions." The kicker for technology leaders: "Each new agent created comes with no additional tech debt or complexity for the technical organization to manage" — in contrast to "a traditional top-down, centrally controlled enterprise software rollout."

## Singtel's academy at scale

" Singtel is another example of a company that has taken a skills-first approach to rolling out gen AI tools. In October 2024, Singtel created an AI Acceleration Academy in conjunction with Nanyang Technological University and the National University of Singapore to train more than 10,000 employees across many roles how to leverage data and gen AI in their workflows." Where Lilli shows federated self-service, Singtel shows structured university-partnered skilling — two complementary routes to the same participation goal.

## The closing charge

" For CEOs, the charge is clear: Plan for a company-wide reconfiguration today so that humans and AI together can achieve extraordinary outcomes tomorrow." The article ends on culture: "foster a culture of experimentation, where employees are not just passive recipients of new technology but active participants in it," framing Gen AI "as a superpower rather than a threat," because "true transformation occurs when gen AI becomes an invisible but indispensable coworker."
