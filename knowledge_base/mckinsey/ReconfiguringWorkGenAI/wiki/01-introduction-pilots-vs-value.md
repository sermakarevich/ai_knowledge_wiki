# The Setup: Piloting Is Easy, Value Is Hard

**In one sentence:** Two-thirds of companies already use generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI, meaning AI that creates text, code, and other content from plain-language prompts), yet almost none see company-wide bottom-line impact, because Gen AI fails like no earlier enterprise software — handing people the technology without changing how work happens produces experimentation without value.

## Key points

- Two-thirds of global companies use Gen AI, but these deployments behave nothing like past enterprise software projects, and leaders remain unclear how to convert potential into meaningful value.
- Gen AI's natural-language interface plus growing reasoning and agentic capabilities let it interpret large volumes of information, write code, answer queries, and — in the most advanced agents — build spreadsheets and navigate web pages.
- Employees already use Gen AI three times more than their leaders realize, creating a shadow-adoption gap between grassroots experimentation and enterprise strategy.
- The article's prescription is a novel, non-linear change management approach that turns employees from Gen AI experimenters into Gen AI accelerators via five CEO-led steps.
- That approach demands employees become active participants — experimenting, co-creating products, continually reskilling — while acknowledging some will need extra support.
- The promised payoff is an era of growth in which reconfigured, fully Gen AI-enabled organizations give employees AI superpowers that strip away tedious, repetitive tasks.

---

## Why Gen AI deployments stall

The article opens with a paradox it never quantifies precisely but states bluntly: "Gen AI has invaded the workplace, but its impact remains elusive." Adoption is broad — two-thirds of global companies use it in at least one function — yet the transformation CEOs expected has not materialized. The diagnosis is that leaders treated Gen AI like conventional enterprise software: buy licenses, roll out licenses, expect productivity. Gen AI resists that playbook because its value comes from how people change their work around it, not from the tool itself.

## What makes Gen AI different as a technology

Three properties carry the argument. First, the natural-language interface makes Gen AI radically easy to start using — no training course required. Second, burgeoning reasoning and agentic capabilities (agents here meaning AI systems that act autonomously toward goals) let it take on increasingly complex tasks: interpreting large volumes of information, coding, answering queries. Third, the frontier keeps moving: "the most advanced agents are even starting to perform tasks such as creating spreadsheets and navigating web pages." Ease of piloting is therefore the trap — it looks like adoption while delivering no reconfiguration.

## The shadow-adoption gap

"Employees are clearly eager to use it; they are already doing so three times more than their leaders realize." This single statistic does heavy lifting: it proves demand is not the problem, shows top-down rollouts are blind to actual usage, and foreshadows the governance risk (unmonitored use) that Step 2 addresses. It also justifies the article's bottom-up-plus-middle-out change model — the energy already exists; leadership must channel it.

## From experimenters to accelerators

The article's central slogan — turning people "from gen AI experimenters into gen AI accelerators" — reframes the workforce problem. Experimentation is individual, sporadic, and disconnected from business outcomes; acceleration means employees co-create Gen AI products against shared goals. The process "is not a linear process": unlike staged software rollouts, it asks employees to experiment, co-create products, and commit to continual skill development simultaneously, with explicit recognition that "not everyone will make the transition smoothly, and that some employees will need additional support."

## The five steps at a glance

The article promises "five steps that CEOs can use to lead their companies through these uncharted waters": craft a North Star based on outcomes rather than tools; build trust with accessible data, governance, and enterprise wisdom; reimagine workflows to evolve toward AI teams; rethink organizational structures with a mix of minimum viable organizations and augmented teams; and empower employees to learn and become change agents.
