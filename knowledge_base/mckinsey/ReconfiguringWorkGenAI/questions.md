# Retrieval Practice: Reconfiguring Work

## The setup: piloting is easy, value is hard

1. What share of global companies uses Gen AI, and what does the article say about the payoff so far?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Two-thirds use it, but impact "remains elusive" — deployments are nothing like past enterprise software projects and leaders are unclear how to create meaningful value.</details>

2. Why does Gen AI's ease of use make the value problem worse rather than better?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The natural-language interface makes piloting trivially easy, so adoption looks complete while no work has been reconfigured; its reasoning and agentic capabilities (spreadsheets, web navigation) keep raising the ceiling without changing how companies operate.</details>

3. Employees use Gen AI three times more than leaders realize — what breaks if leadership never closes that gap?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Grassroots use stays disconnected from strategy: inconsistent outcomes, ungoverned risks (bad outputs, data leakage), and no path from scattered experimentation to scaled value — the "experimenters never become accelerators" trap.</details>

## Step 1: North Star

4. State the three design rules for a Gen AI North Star and the tool-versus-capability distinction behind them.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Simple enough to be universally understood, bold enough to inspire, and durable enough to absorb frontier models; it rests on treating Gen AI as a capability (a way of working) rather than a tool to install, with humans and agents working seamlessly together.</details>

5. A CEO announces "we will deploy copilots to every employee by Q3." Why would the article call this a non–North Star?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It names a tool rollout, not business outcomes; it is neither inspiring nor technology-proof, and it skips both required components — a resourced change plan and end-to-end workflow reimagining.</details>

## Step 2: trust, data, governance

6. What is the "marketplace of information," and why is it a competitive advantage?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>The firm-specific maze of hierarchies and policies deciding where information lives and who may see it; Gen AI can consume its unstructured contents to extract hidden insights and patterns competitors cannot copy.</details>

7. Name the four governance elements the article requires, and who owns them.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>An AI oversight committee, acceptable-use policies, compliance and risk guidelines built with risk and legal teams, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints for hallucinations, bias, and leakage — owned jointly by the CEO, CIO, and CDO.</details>

8. Suppose grounding answers in company sources slows responses. What breaks if a firm skips grounding to ship faster?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Per the article's trust chain, ungrounded answers erode output trust, which kills trust in AI-made decisions, which makes scale impossible — speed now trades away adoption later.</details>

## Step 3: workflows

9. Recite the three phases of workflow evolution and the "two-in-the-box" pairing.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stand-alone agents for discrete tasks, groups of agents running end-to-end processes under human oversight, fully automated agentic swarms acting as MVOs; business teams own business results while technology teams own feasibility.</details>

10. Quote the Lilli statistics (launch date, adoption, regular use, time saved, prompts answered).
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Launched July 2023; 92 percent of global staff have used it, 74 percent use it regularly, saving over 30 percent of time on information gathering and synthesis; nearly 19 million prompts answered.</details>

11. Why start automation with work streams where value is clear, feasibility high, and investment manageable?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Early wins make employees' jobs easier, converting skeptics into co-designers; hard-first sequencing breeds resistance and stalls the whole evolution.</details>

## Step 4: MVOs and augmented teams

12. Contrast where MVOs belong versus where augmented teams belong, with one example of each.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>MVOs suit repetitive logic-based work (invoice matching, approvals, entries with near-zero human touch plus exception handlers); augmented teams suit human-touch functions (salespeople with AI upsell hints carrying larger portfolios; service reps resolving faster) where full automation would erode customer experience and brand.</details>

13. Name the two emerging MVO roles and the two MVO prerequisites.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>AI workflow optimizer and automation product owner; robust AI operations/monitoring and a rethought talent strategy (operators skilled in AI systems, data analysis, exception handling), plus reskilling the displaced.</details>

14. A CFO proposes making customer support an MVO to cut costs. How does the article's logic respond?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>It would refuse: support is a human-touch function where removing people risks the customer experience and brand damage; augment the reps instead and reserve MVOs for work where human contact adds no value.</details>

## Step 5: people as change agents

15. Give the three participation numbers and what each signifies.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>~2 percent: typical involvement in transformations; ≥7 percent: doubles chances of positive excess TSR; 21–30 percent: the involvement band of the highest performers.</details>

16. Which age cohort reports the highest AI expertise, with what percentages, and what should CEOs do with that fact?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ages 35–44 (millennial managers) at 62 percent, versus 50 percent for 18–24s and 22 percent for over-65s; recruit them as mentors and practice-group leads in a middle-out (not just top-down or bottom-up) change.</details>

17. How many centrally built versus employee-built agents does McKinsey report, and why does the article stress "no additional tech debt"?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>About 150 central plus nearly 17,000 employee-built under a federated model with risk controls; zero marginal tech debt proves federated co-creation scales without burdening the technology organization — unlike top-down enterprise rollouts.</details>

18. Your firm has strong top-down mandates but only 2 percent participation. What breaks, and what is the first fix?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Per the article's economics, success odds stay at baseline and Gen AI ambassadors never emerge; first fix is segmentation plus superuser mobilization (clubs, tailored training, visible leader use) to push past 7 percent, as in the Lilli playbook.</details>

## Transfer

19. A hospital wants Gen AI for discharge paperwork and bedside manner. Apply the MVO/augmented split.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Paperwork processing goes MVO-like (repetitive, logic-based, exception handlers only); bedside care stays an augmented team (clinicians with AI-drafted notes and summaries), since removing humans would damage patient trust — the article's brand-risk boundary generalized.</details>

20. Design a North Star sentence for a 200-person logistics firm using the article's three rules.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Strong answers are one sentence, outcome-based (e.g., "every shipment planned, priced, and resolved overnight by human dispatchers commanding agent teams"), simple, bold, and model-proof — check yours against all three rules plus the required change-plan and workflow-redesign companions.</details>
