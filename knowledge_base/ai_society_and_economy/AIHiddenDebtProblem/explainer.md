# AI Hidden Debt Problem — In Plain Language

## What is this about?

Imagine five wealthy friends decide to each build a giant mansion, but instead of taking out a normal mortgage, they sign promises: "I'll pay the builder later," "I'll rent it for 20 years once it's built," and "if my side-company can't repay its loan, I'll cover it." On paper each friend still looks rich, because those promises don't count as debt yet. That is what Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle are doing with AI data centers.

The digest says these five firms carry over $1 trillion in hidden off-balance-sheet borrowings — elsewhere summed as over $2.6 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI buildout obligations — plus OpenAI alone committing over $600 billion in spending by 2030. The once cash-rich "money printers" have become "money furnaces," burning cash fast. Some use complex ownership structures that seem designed to move borrowings off the balance sheet, and headline numbers ($1 trillion vs $3 trillion) confuse people because they mix different kinds of obligations. The good news in the digest: these amounts are not secret — each company lists them in a commitments or contractual obligations section of its filings — and on-balance-sheet debt still looks healthy for four of five firms.

## Why does it matter?

- The scale is huge: purchase obligations alone exceed $1.5 trillion, mostly for AI data centers, and uncommenced leases exceed $1 trillion — more than doubling the roughly $700 billion of on-book debt including leases.
- Alphabet (~$800B) and Meta (~$350B) commit roughly the majority of their total assets to future spending obligations, so a large share of their size is already promised away.
- Operating leases for data-center space are legally binding and debt-like: missing a payment has consequences similar to defaulting on debt.
- Guarantees concentrate risk: Meta's Hyperion data center (~$50B, Louisiana) sits in SPV Beignet Investor (Meta owns 20%), which raised $27.3B in bonds in October 2025 while Meta is sole future lessee from 2029, with Meta guaranteeing the full bond amount via a residual-value guarantee.
- The pattern repeats — Meta–BlackRock SPV Soapia Investor in El Paso, Texas issued $12.3B in secured notes backed by Meta plus a $14.7B contingent purchase commitment, and Oracle shows $3.3B of similar guarantees — raising fears about circular financing deals, e.g. with Nvidia.
- Oracle is the clear exception: while Alphabet and Microsoft hold more cash than debt, Oracle has much higher leverage, and it looks weakest even next to a pure data-center firm like Equinix.

## How does it work?

1. **Sign purchase contracts.** The firms order AI data-center goods and services for future delivery — over $1.5 trillion across the five. Because nothing has been delivered yet, standard accounting keeps this off debt totals.
2. **Pledge funding and construction money.** About $100B more goes to joint ventures and build-out pledges, including Microsoft's OpenAI funding obligations.
3. **Pre-sign leases for buildings not yet open.** These "uncommenced leases" top $1 trillion, with Meta and Microsoft each over $300B. The rent starts later, so it sits off the balance sheet today.
4. **Create a side company (SPV).** Example: Meta puts the $50B Hyperion project into Beignet Investor, keeps only 20%, and the SPV borrows $27.3B in bonds from investors.
5. **Guarantee the side company's debt quietly.** Meta as sole future lessee from 2029 pays rent that funds the SPV's bond payments, and adds a residual-value guarantee for the full bond amount — so the debt lives off Meta's balance sheet but the risk stays with Meta.
6. **Repeat the template.** Soapia Investor ($12.3B notes + $14.7B contingent commitment) and Oracle's $3.3B guarantees show the same debt-shifting shape.
7. **Disclose it in the fine print.** All of this appears in each firm's explicit commitments or contractual obligations section — four buckets in the digest: non-cancellable contractual commitments, funding and construction commitments, uncommenced leases, and financial backstops and guarantees, with the last being most controversial.

## Where can this be used?

- **Reading Big Tech filings:** look past headline debt to the commitments and contractual obligations section, split into the four buckets above.
- **Comparing the five hyperscalers:** separate Alphabet and Microsoft (cash exceeds debt) from Oracle (high leverage), instead of treating a single $1 trillion headline as one shared debt pile.
- **Judging new AI data-center deals:** ask who the sole lessee is, who guarantees the SPV bonds, and when lease payments start — as in Hyperion/Beignet (2029) and Soapia.
- **Stress-testing the story:** check guarantees (only two firms carry large ones, both under $100 billion) separately from purchase contracts and uncommenced leases, which are future payments for goods and services the firms expect to use anyway.
- **Watching what comes next:** track whether AI revenue arrives fast enough and whether these obligations keep growing beyond today's level.

## Conclusions & takeaways

- The "over $1 trillion in hidden debt" alarm is partly overblown: only two firms carry large financial guarantees, both under $100 billion, coverable in theory with less than one year of earnings.
- The bigger totals are not the same as debt — they are future payments for things the firms plan to use — and headline sums overstate the burden because they are nominal sums, not PV (Present Value, what future payments are worth in today's money).
- Even adding these commitments to debt hurts credit ratios versus history, but looks normal next to Equinix — except Oracle, which is weakest.
- The real worry is structural: a shift from light, flexible spending to heavy, fixed AI assets that are harder to exit.
- Complex ownership structures can make borrowings harder to find, which is why the digest stresses reading filings company by company.
- Honest limits from the digest: headline numbers mix obligation types without company-by-company breakdowns; the key unknowns are whether AI revenue arrives in time and whether obligations keep growing. No claim here goes beyond the digest.

## Jargon decoder

| Term | What it means in plain language |
|---|---|
| Off-balance-sheet obligations | Future promises to pay that don't count as debt in today's headline number |
| Purchase obligations | Signed orders for goods/services not yet delivered, e.g. data-center gear |
| Uncommenced leases | Rents for buildings not yet open — binding, but payments start later |
| Funding / construction commitments | Pledged money for joint ventures and building, e.g. OpenAI funding |
| SPV (special-purpose vehicle) | A side company set up to own one project and borrow against it |
| Residual-value guarantee | A promise to cover the SPV's full bond amount if its value falls short |
| Financial backstop / guarantee | A pledge to pay someone else's debt if they can't |
| Nominal sum vs PV (Present Value) | Raw total of future payments vs what they are worth in today's money |
| Credit ratios | Health scores comparing debt to earnings or assets |
| Money printers → money furnaces | Cash-rich firms turning into fast cash-burners for AI buildout |
