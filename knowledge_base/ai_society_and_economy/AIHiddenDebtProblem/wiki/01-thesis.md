# Thesis: AI's hidden debt

**In one sentence:** Big AI hyperscalers are taking on large debts to build data centers, and a lot of the risk sits off the main balance sheet in contracts and guarantees, but it is disclosed in filings and still looks manageable on paper so far.

## Key points

- Reports claim the five big AI hyperscalers — Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle — carry over $1 trillion in hidden off-balance-sheet borrowings [00:10].
- OpenAI alone has committed to over $600 billion in spending by 2030, raising fears about circular financing deals with Nvidia and others [00:36].
- Former cash-rich "money printers" have become "money furnaces," burning cash fast to fund AI data centers [00:47].
- Some firms use complex ownership structures that seem designed to move borrowings off the balance sheet and make them harder to find [01:04].
- Headline numbers ($1 trillion vs $3 trillion) confuse people because they mix different kinds of obligations without a company-by-company breakdown [01:35].
- The so-called hidden amounts are not really secret: each company lists them in an explicit commitments or contractual obligations section of its filings [02:14].
- On-balance-sheet debt still looks healthy for four of five firms, with Alphabet and Microsoft holding more cash than debt, while Oracle is the one clear exception with much higher leverage [04:09].
- Off-balance-sheet risk falls into four buckets: non-cancellable contractual commitments, funding and construction commitments, uncommenced leases, and financial backstops and guarantees, with the last one being most controversial [06:09].

## The hidden-debt question

The video opens with a simple question: does AI have a hidden debt problem [00:00]?

The claim going around is that the biggest tech companies in the world have over $1 trillion in hidden debt — money borrowed but kept off their balance sheets [00:10].

The companies in focus are the big five AI hyperscalers building data centers to power AI models: Alphabet or Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle [00:21].

## Why people are worried now

AI spending was already facing doubt and criticism [00:30].

OpenAI itself has committed to over $600 billion in spending by 2030 [00:36]. There are accusations of circular financing deals with Nvidia and other players [00:41].

Companies that used to print money now burn cash quickly — faster, the speaker jokes, than Studio Ghibli prompts burn GPUs [00:47].

Because they need so much money, they have started to pile up debt [00:58]. Instead of borrowing in a plain simple way, some have entered complex ownership structures whose single purpose seems to be moving borrowings off the balance sheet to make them harder to find [01:04].

## Goal of this video: build one clear spreadsheet

The speaker proposes to go through company filings, collect all off-balance-sheet items into one simple spreadsheet, and review the numbers together [01:19].

The aim is a company and category breakdown, because headlines alone confuse [01:28].

One article says $1 trillion, another says $3 trillion, and while articles often describe what the amounts are, headline figures alone make it hard to grasp what they represent [01:35].

So the plan is to compile the numbers in one place and give a better picture of what the so-called hidden obligations are [01:54].

## So-called hidden, but actually disclosed

The speaker says "so-called" on purpose [02:01].

It is true the amounts do not show up on the balance sheet, and that seems intentional in some cases [02:05].

But they are not hard to find: each company has an explicit commitments or contractual obligations section in its filings that lists all these details [02:14].

He still does the leg work to put everything in one place to make it easier to see where the companies stand [02:22].

## On-balance-sheet debt: still fairly calm

The first step is debt that is reported on the balance sheet [03:05].

Historically, with the one exception of Oracle, big US tech firms had clean balance sheets with very little borrowing and so much free cash flow they held more cash than they needed [03:10].

Today, with the AI spending spree, debt has crept higher, but balance sheets are still relatively healthy [03:26].

Figures are as of June 30 for all firms except Oracle, whose year ends May 31, with small tweaks for later news — for example Alphabet issued $25 billion in corporate bonds after its filing, added conservatively only to the debt side and color-coded [03:35].

Two firms, Alphabet and Microsoft, have negative net debt including recent bonds and leases — meaning they could in theory pay off borrowings with cash on hand [04:09].

For firms with positive net debt — more borrowed than cash held — net debt to income ratios are still fairly small [04:23].

Oracle is the exception: its debt is more substantive, with debt-to-equity and income ratios much higher than the others [04:34].

For the other four, debt-to-equity ratios sit at or below the US tech sector average of 0.51 times [04:47].

Net debt to income is very low across profit measures, and interest coverage shows they can cover debt costs many times over from revenue [04:56].

Interest costs are rising — the speaker annualizes the latest quarterly figure for a run-rate — yet two firms still have negative net interest expense, meaning they earn more interest on cash than they pay on debt [05:13].

One warning: since firms spend most earnings on future investment, they do not have much spare cash left if they tried to repay debt while growing so aggressively [05:33].

Overall, balance sheets paint a fairly muted debt picture: balances are rising but look manageable next to profits [05:44].

## Off-balance-sheet buckets: four categories

The video then turns to off-balance-sheet measures [05:57].

There are generally three or four categories behind the $1 trillion-plus hidden-debt headlines: non-cancellable contractual commitments, funding and construction commitments, uncommenced leases, and financial backstops and guarantees — with the last one the most controversial [06:00].

### 1. Non-cancellable contractual commitments

These are, as the name says, legal duties to buy goods or services in the future [06:21].

The mix varies by firm but usually covers three items [06:33]:

- Data-center parts such as semiconductors: with strong demand for Nvidia chips, firms sign future purchase deals to lock in supply [06:36].
- Power purchase agreements: to get enough electricity, firms sign long-term take-or-pay deals, paying for power whether they use it or not, to push providers to build capacity [06:52].
- Third-party cloud capacity: renting compute for AI models from other firms, again to secure supply for their own business [07:15].

These deals are legally binding [07:29].
