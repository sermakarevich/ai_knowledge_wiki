# Evidence and cases

**In one sentence:** Five big tech firms carry over $2.6 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI buildout obligations — purchase contracts, leases, and guaranteed SPV debt — that dwarf their reported debt but stay hidden by standard accounting rules.

## Key points
- Purchase obligations across five companies exceed $1.5 trillion, mostly for AI data centers, hidden only because goods/services have not yet been delivered [08:01] [08:06].
- Alphabet (~$800B) and Meta (~$350B) commit roughly the majority of their total assets to future spending obligations [08:31] [08:42].
- Funding and construction commitments add ~$100B more across the five, including joint ventures and Microsoft's OpenAI funding obligations [09:34] [09:16].
- Uncommenced leases exceed $1 trillion, more than doubling the ~$700B of on-book debt including leases, with Meta and Microsoft each over $300B [10:46] [10:55] [11:01].
- Operating leases for data-center space are legally binding and debt-like: missing a payment has consequences similar to defaulting on debt [10:17] [10:29] [10:32].
- Meta's Hyperion data center (Louisiana, ~$50B) sits in SPV Beignet Investor (Meta owns 20%), which raised $27.3B in bonds in October 2025 while Meta is sole future lessee from 2029 [12:22] [12:34] [12:40] [12:51].
- Meta gave a residual-value guarantee for the full SPV bond amount, so it is on the hook for SPV debt funded only by Meta's own lease payments — debt shifted off its balance sheet [13:22] [13:32] [13:44] [13:51].
- The pattern repeats: Meta–BlackRock SPV Soapia Investor in El Paso, Texas issued $12.3B in secured notes backed by Meta, plus a $14.7B contingent purchase commitment; Oracle shows $3.3B of similar guarantees [14:00] [14:08] [14:56] [15:02].

## Contractual purchase obligations: $1.5T+ off the balance sheet

The agreements are legally binding, but under standard accounting rules no liability or expense is recognized until the supplier delivers the good or service [07:31] [07:39] [07:41]. They therefore appear only as disclosures in financial filings until the contracts are actioned [07:47] [07:51].

The scale is large: five companies together commit over $1.5 trillion, predominantly for AI data-center buildout [08:01] [08:06]. Minor non-AI line items may be included, but the total is a multiple of the level five years ago, so the step-up is mostly AI-related [08:09] [08:15] [08:22].

Concentration matters: Alphabet has by far the largest amount at over $800 billion, followed by Meta at around $350 billion [08:31] [08:35]. As a share of total assets, these two stand out, committing roughly the majority of their total assets to future spending obligations [08:41] [08:44]. The speaker calls this first off-balance-sheet item "a doozy" [08:49].

## Funding and construction commitments: ~$100B

Segregated from purchase contracts, this second category covers investing/building obligations rather than buying goods and services [08:54] [08:59] [09:02]. Examples include joint-venture spend obligations and Microsoft's funding obligations around OpenAI, sometimes contingent on benchmarks [09:09] [09:16] [09:22].

Amounts are smaller — around $100 billion cumulatively across the five — but still material [09:32] [09:34] [09:39].

## Uncommenced leases: $1T+ that double reported debt

The third category is uncommenced leases: signed lease agreements with a future start date that, per standard accounting practice, do not yet appear on the balance sheet and live in the disclosures [09:47] [09:51] [09:56] [09:59].

Most appear to be operating leases (renting data-center space over time) rather than finance leases (mortgage-like pay-to-own) [10:07] [10:15] [10:17] [10:25]. Yet analysts treat operating leases as debt-like because they are legally binding — a missed payment can trigger consequences similar to a debt default [10:29] [10:32] [10:34].

Totals: over $1 trillion in uncommenced lease obligations across the five, which would more than double current total debt obligations including leases of around $700 billion [10:46] [10:51] [10:55]. They represent a good chunk of total assets; Meta and Microsoft have the largest dollar amounts at over $300 billion in future leases each [10:59] [11:01] [11:05]. The concern: hundreds of billions committed to an AI buildout whose long-term financial viability is unproven [11:11] [11:16].

## Guarantees and backstops: VIEs and SPVs where debt is hidden

Financial guarantees and backstops are presented as the most concerning category — where debt is arguably actively hidden [11:20] [11:24] [11:29].

Mechanism: companies set up variable interest entities / special purpose vehicles (VIEs and SPVs) in which they own less than 50% — substantial influence but not control — and account for them under the equity method [11:33] [11:41] [11:47] [11:51]. Instead of consolidating the vehicle's assets and liabilities, the parent shows the whole business as essentially one line item in its own asset section [11:56] [12:00] [12:06].

Case 1 — Meta Hyperion, Louisiana: a co-developed data-center project, one of the largest in the world at over $50 billion estimated cost, owned by SPV Beignet Investor (named after the local pastry), of which Meta owns 20% [12:20] [12:28] [12:34] [12:38]. In October 2025 the SPV raised $27.3 billion in one of the largest corporate bond issuances on record [12:40] [12:44]. The center is built entirely for Meta, sole lessee starting in 2029 [12:49] [12:53].

The structure alone could be defended (Meta wants the capacity without owning/operating the center; the SPV borrows so Meta is insulated on default) [12:56] [13:02] [13:09] [13:14]. The catch: to raise the debt, Meta provided a residual-value guarantee for the entire bond amount — it is on the hook to bondholders on a miss [13:19] [13:25] [13:31] [13:35]. Since Meta as sole lessee is the SPV's only revenue source for bond payments, the move looks like an elaborate, likely intentional shift of debt off the balance sheet [13:40] [13:46] [13:51] [13:53].

Case 2 — Meta Soapia Investor, El Paso, Texas: joint venture with BlackRock to build another data center; the SPV already issued $12.3 billion in secured notes, again backed by Meta [14:00] [14:03] [14:08] [14:13].

Disclosure caveat: all details are technically in the filings, and other reasons for SPVs may exist — but the effect is to make debt harder to find [14:17] [14:21] [14:27]. Combined with lease payments that service SPV debt, the guarantees mean the companies are in a roundabout way borrowing in their own name [14:32] [14:37] [14:42].

Tallies so far: only Meta and Alphabet (speaker says "Meta and Google") have sizable guarantee obligations; Meta's total shown includes a $14.7B contingent purchase commitment treated as a financial guarantee, while Oracle has $3.3 billion of similar guarantees [14:49] [14:53] [14:56] [15:02] [15:05].
