# Retrieval Practice: AI for Good

Answer from memory before opening any answer. Run sessions with `ai show summary/quiz`.

### Q1. What does Tyrangiel mean by "the frozen middle," and who does he cite as diagnosing it as government's real obstacle to AI-driven reform — more so than any technical limitation?

> [!tip]- Answer
> The "frozen middle" is the mid-level bureaucratic and procurement layer — risk-averse incentives, contracting law, institutional self-protection — that resists modern software practices regardless of what the technology can do. Eric Schmidt (Defense Innovation Board) concludes "you can't have AI in the government or the military until you solve the problem of software," while Jennifer Pahlka describes a dysfunction loop where rule-bound civil servants are punished either for following absurd process or for deviating from it. See [[wiki/04-part3-government|Part 3: Government]].

### Q2. By what percentage did sepsis mortality fall across Cleveland Clinic from 2021 to 2024 after the Bayesian Health model's rollout, and what two caveats do Dr. James Morrison and Allie Tallman attach to that number?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Sepsis mortality fell 40%. Morrison and Tallman caution that AI doesn't deserve sole credit: much of the improvement predates Bayesian's May 2024 pilot launch, and some of it may reflect the Hawthorne effect (people behaving differently when observed). Mihaljevic's takeaway is that "AI does not need to be perfect to be useful." See [[wiki/03-part2-medicine|Part 2: Medicine]].

### Q3. What happened to LAUSD's AI chatbot "Ed" and to AllHere Education founder Joanna Smith-Griffin within months of its March 2024 launch?

> [!tip]- Answer
> AllHere furloughed most of its staff, a whistleblower alleged mishandling of student data, the company filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy, and Smith-Griffin was arrested on securities fraud, wire fraud, and aggravated identity theft charges for allegedly misusing investor funds for her wedding and a home down payment. Tyrangiel calls it "the full Theranos." See [[wiki/02-part1-education|Part 1: Education]].

### Q4. What cost-saving proposal did Sahil Lavingia make at the VA using a Mistral AI model, and what happened to him after he discussed it in a media interview?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Veterans were still faxing in health claims for contractors to convert to digital text, so Lavingia proposed letting them upload smartphone photos instead, processed by a Mistral document model — projected to save up to $200 million a year. DOGE leadership wasn't interested in the "messy human work" of implementing it with VA staff and Congress, and Lavingia was fired after 55 days once he spoke publicly about it. See [[wiki/04-part3-government|Part 3: Government]].

### Q5. Rohit Chandra says identifying AI opportunities at Cleveland Clinic is easy, but "identifying the right people is everything." Using Eric Boose's ambient-scribe pilot, explain why the technology alone couldn't have produced the 2025 Ambience contract.

> [!tip]- Answer
> Across five vendors tested on 250 doctors, output quality varied wildly and struggled with accents and medical terminology — but the bigger obstacle was that many volunteer doctors never turned the app on or ghosted follow-up. Boose's low-key, "farmer-like" persistence — chasing feedback and pushing small vendors for fast fixes — kept the pilot alive long enough to generate the hard data (80% usage across 25,000 encounters) that justified the contract. Without a "samurai" willing to live with the problem, per Chandra, the pilot would have joined the many others treated as entertainment rather than a path to adoption. See [[wiki/03-part2-medicine|Part 2: Medicine]].

### Q6. What would be lost from the book's argument if Part 1 opened with Khan Academy's successes instead of LAUSD's "Ed" and the EdTech graveyard (Summit Learning, Knewton, AltSchool)?

> [!tip]- Answer
> The book's credibility rests on treating failure as part of the honest record rather than editing it out, and the Ed/Summit/Knewton/AltSchool failures establish the baseline of hype-driven collapse against which Khan Academy's slower, co-designed process (red-teaming, guardrails, iterative fixes) reads as meaningfully different rather than just another success to take on faith. Without that contrast, Khanmigo's own real flaws — hallucinations, unreliable math, students gaming or abandoning it — would look like isolated failures instead of evidence for the book's "no simple answer for student engagement" thesis. See [[wiki/02-part1-education|Part 1: Education]].

### Q7. Why did the Bayesian Health sepsis model perform better at Fairview Hospital than at Cleveland Clinic's Main Campus, even though both ran the same underlying algorithm?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Main Campus's extreme case mix (cardiac, digestive, surgical, neuro-cancer subspecialties) caused the model to confuse cardiac-related infections with sepsis, prompting Tallman's objection that "we can't treat Main Campus with a blanket algorithm." Fairview, a smaller, lower-case-mix community hospital, had standardized response protocols that eliminated most human friction, so "a resident on day one can follow that same process just as much as James can." The model's accuracy depended on the surrounding clinical process being standardized enough for its outputs to be consistently actionable, not on the algorithm alone. See [[wiki/03-part2-medicine|Part 2: Medicine]].

### Q8. Using Rosalind Picard's claim that "every AI product embeds a choice" between substituting for human connection and restoring it (illustrated by companion chatbots versus Kristy Johnson's Comalla/ROSCO), how would you tell an amplifying AI tool from a substituting one in a domain the book never covers, such as journalism?

> [!tip]- Answer
> An amplifying tool would use AI to do things a reporter can't scale alone — transcribing and translating sources, surfacing patterns across leaked documents, drafting a research summary the journalist then verifies and bylines — while keeping a human accountable for judgment and source relationships, the same way ambient scribes free Cleveland Clinic doctors for patients rather than replacing them. A substituting tool would generate bylined articles or engage readers directly in place of a journalist, monetizing attention the way Character.ai's bots do — Tyrangiel's line that "the user is the product... and the product should never log off" describes the failure mode to watch for. See [[wiki/05-part4-connection|Part 4: Human Connection]].
