# AI for Good: How Real People Are Using Artificial Intelligence to Fix Things That Matter

**Book:** [AI for Good (Josh Tyrangiel, Simon & Schuster, 2026 -- advance reader's proof)](source/ai_for_good.pdf)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Most AI coverage is a shouting match between people who think it'll save the world and people who think it'll end it. This book skips the shouting and follows the people actually using AI on the ground -- teachers, doctors, government workers, parents -- to fix specific, unglamorous problems: a struggling student, a hospital's overcrowded ER, an IRS backlog, a nonverbal child who can't be understood. Some of these stories are quiet triumphs. A lot of them are messy, half-finished, or outright failures. The book's bet is that this messy middle -- not the hype, not the doom -- is where AI's real story is being written.

## TL;DR

Journalist Josh Tyrangiel profiles the "AI counterculture" -- pragmatists in education, medicine, government, and personal life who adopt AI tools to amplify human judgment rather than replace it. Structured in four parts (education, medicine, government, human connection) covering 25 chapters, the book uses deep, named case studies (Khan Academy's Khanmigo, Cleveland Clinic's AI pilots, Palantir's role in Operation Warp Speed and the IRS, ELIZA-descended companion chatbots) rather than aggregate statistics to argue that AI succeeds only when paired with someone willing to navigate the human and institutional terrain around it, and fails -- sometimes badly -- when speed and technology are substituted for trust and collaboration.

## Problem & Motivation

Tyrangiel's stated motivation is a 2020s AI discourse he sees as dominated by two compromised camps: "accelerationists" promising utopia and "doomers" warning of catastrophe, both entangled with money (McKinsey, Morgan Stanley, Google, Meta, Musk) and ego. He traces his own "AI awakening" to a YouTube video of General Gustave Perna describing how Palantir's data platform let him run Operation Warp Speed's vaccine logistics. That anecdote convinced him AI's real value shows up in mundane, high-stakes operational work, not speculative futures -- prompting him, after a challenge from futurist Danny Hillis to "imagine the tech without the tech companies," to go find and profile the people actually doing that work.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **The "AI counterculture."** Tyrangiel's organizing concept: a loose, undramatic movement of teachers, doctors, bureaucrats, and parents who back into AI adoption to solve a specific problem, usually without software backgrounds, standing apart from both AI-industry triumphalism and reflexive AI doomsaying.
2. **Amplify judgment, don't replace it.** The book's recurring normative claim, made explicit in the Epilogue: the AI deployments that work are the ones that free humans (teachers, clinicians, caseworkers) to be more present and human, not the ones that try to automate the human out of the loop.
3. **The "frozen middle."** A term (Part 3) for the mid-level bureaucratic and procurement layer -- risk-averse incentives, contracting law, institutional self-protection -- that Tyrangiel identifies as the real obstacle to AI-driven reform in government, more so than any technical limitation.
4. **"Thousand flowers bloom" over top-down transformation.** Illustrated at Cleveland Clinic (Part 2): large institutions succeed with AI by backing many narrow, clinician-led pilots against specific problems rather than mandating one enterprise-wide platform.
5. **Every AI product embeds a choice.** Framed explicitly in Part 4 via MIT's Rosalind Picard: the same underlying model architectures can be built to substitute for human connection and monetize attention, or to restore connection between people who've been cut off from each other -- the difference is a design and business-model choice, not a technical one.

## Key Findings

| Domain (Part)        | Central case study                                                                                                                       | Outcome                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Education (1)        | Khan Academy's Khanmigo (built on GPT-4) in Hobart, IN and Newark, NJ pilots                                                             | Real wins (parent translation, anxious-student confidence) alongside hallucinations, unreliable math, and gamed/abandoned use; "no simple answer for student engagement"                 |
| Medicine (2)         | Cleveland Clinic AI pilots: digital-twin cardiac MRI, Ambience scribes, Palantir hospital-flow forecasting, Bayesian Health sepsis model | Sepsis mortality cut ~40% despite the model still missing on the hardest cases; CEO's framing: "AI does not need to be perfect to be useful"                                             |
| Government (3)       | Palantir/Operation Warp Speed, IRS modernization (Werfel/Pandya), DOGE's VA/IRS actions                                                  | Succeeds with political cover and collaboration (Perna, IRS mainframe modernization); fails when speed and contempt replace collaboration (DOGE)                                         |
| Human connection (4) | ELIZA-descended companion chatbots (Character.ai, Meta bots, Grok) vs. Kristy Johnson's Comalla/ROSCO project for nonverbal son Felix    | Companion bots deliver real comfort and real harm (a documented chatbot-linked suicide); assistive-communication AI shows the same technology restoring rather than replacing connection |

- Several stories end in stalled adoption or open failure (LAUSD's "Ed," Summit Learning, Knewton, AltSchool in Part 1) -- the book treats failure as part of the honest record, not an exception to edit out.
- The obstacles that recur across all four domains are institutional and human (trust, incentives, procurement, habit), not model capability.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The Epilogue closes with the author's direct recommendations to readers, not just to policymakers or AI labs:

1. Learn the technology's actual limits firsthand (an afternoon with a free chatbot tier) rather than trusting vendor claims.
2. Decide deliberately what AI features *not* to use -- adoption itself is the leverage: "companies will build what we tolerate."
3. Read (or have AI summarize) the privacy policy of any AI service you use -- every AI product is a swap of convenience for data, attention, or something else.
4. "Stay close to Team Human": support AI that amplifies human judgment and decline AI that substitutes for human relationships.
5. Redirect money, votes, and volunteer effort toward the hard, underserved problems the book profiles (public services, education, health access) rather than waiting for AI labs or government to solve them top-down.
