# Part 2: Physician, Heal Thyself (Medicine)

Part 2 is built almost entirely around a single institution, Cleveland Clinic, and its CEO Tomas "Tommy" Mihaljevic, who treats AI not as a magic fix but as a discipline for standardizing the mundane so people can focus on what still requires human judgment. Through a "thousand flowers bloom" strategy, Mihaljevic backs a scattered cast of clinician-entrepreneurs — a cardiologist building faster cardiac MRI, an outside tech executive recruiting "samurai" doctors willing to champion unglamorous pilots, a family physician steering ambient-scribe software into daily practice, a hospitalist turning the hospital into something closer to a well-run hotel, and a sepsis team fighting one of medicine's deadliest and most camouflaged killers — to find narrow, provable wins rather than sweeping digital transformation. The throughline is that AI in medicine succeeds only when paired with a person willing to "live and die with the problem," and that even imperfect AI, deployed inside grinding institutional politics, can save lives at scale.

## Tommy

Tomas Mihaljevic, a Croatian-born cardiac surgeon who fled the breakup of Yugoslavia, trained in Switzerland and the US, pioneered robotic cardiac surgery at Cleveland Clinic, and built its Abu Dhabi mega-facility, became Cleveland Clinic's CEO in 2018. He was early to AI in part because the UAE made it a national priority during his time there in the mid-2010s. His philosophy: "In order to innovate, you have to standardize the things that are indisputably the best practice. That allows you to focus on all the stuff that takes you by surprise." He's openly skeptical of AI hype — "Very many people believe that artificial intelligence is a magic dust that you're going to sprinkle over your business and all of a sudden everything is going to be perfect" — and insists Cleveland Clinic will never become "the Microsoft of health care." His stated motive is dual: better clinical outcomes, and cutting the 25–30% of US health-care costs that are administrative (roughly $5 billion of Cleveland Clinic's own spend) so savings can be redirected into care. He diagnoses two common failure modes for health systems chasing digital transformation: (1) letting enthusiastic internal staff build products no one uses, and (2) top-executive "meetings" with Microsoft/Google that produce five ideas and no ownership. His fix is Cleveland Clinic's actual strategy: find internal problems, pair them with narrow-focus AI partners (often small, scrappy companies that need Cleveland Clinic more than the reverse), never skip a lengthy pilot, and force clinicians to co-design with tech staff. He calls the resulting scattershot of internal experiments a "thousand flowers bloom" phenomenon, driven by entrepreneurial, impatient doctors and nurses — "people like Tomas Mihaljevic, circa 1999."

## Digital Twins

Cardiologist **Debbie Kwon** returned from maternity leave exhausted and worried she was missing things while reading stacks of EKGs and cardiac CT scans. She became convinced that cardiac MRI — used in just 1% of cases nationally despite being safer and more comprehensive than other tests — could be made scalable with AI. Using leverage from a competing Vanderbilt job offer, she got Cleveland Clinic to fund an 18-month deep dive, then a business plan for a new cross-departmental lab. She recruited **Chris Nguyen**, a biomedical engineering PhD who had built an AI cardiac-imaging lab at Mass General, overcoming a botched interview in which a jealous senior radiologist tried to sabotage the hire (a debacle that ironically got Kwon a $3 million Siemens Magnetom Cima.X research scanner as compensation). Nguyen leads the **Cardiovascular Innovation Research Center (CIRC)**, whose long-term (5–10 year) goal is a **digital twin** of every patient's heart: an AI-generated, continuously updated simulation built from real imaging and health data that persists across doctors and visits, letting clinicians test treatments in simulation before touching the patient — "Safer, cheaper, more effective medicine based on you, not just a dataset of people like you."

The near-term work: making cardiac MRI usable by more than the 12 (of 268) MRI technologists at Cleveland Clinic currently qualified to perform it. Nguyen's team rewrote the MRI's open-source pulse-sequence software to capture continuous video (a frame every 30 milliseconds over 20 minutes) instead of a technician manually timing single shots during breath-holds — producing 100x more data, organized only because AI (running on an Nvidia DGX system) can filter useful frames from motion-blurred ones in a tenth of a second. Starting in 2022, Nguyen and Kwon paid patients $100 to scan 200 hearts within a month, got a prototype a month later, but hit a "this is bad" moment with real (in vivo) motion artifacts; by late 2023 the model reached a "B-plus." A second AI model was then trained (via tedious, pixel-by-pixel "weekly image review council meetings") to do image segmentation — pre-labeling heart geometry so doctors could skip straight to diagnosis. The author witnessed a live demo at the Mellen Center: lead MRI technologist Angel Houston ("a badass sensei") ran a traditional scan requiring hundreds of manual clicks, then an automated scan needing roughly ten clicks of verification, finished in under half the time — with Houston stepping away mid-scan to get water. Nguyen envisions a further step where the MRI itself signals when it has enough data, shortening sessions further. Despite the results, adoption faces real resistance: some veteran doctors prefer the older, slower method. Kwon also showed a striking clinical finding — cardiac MRI reveals that women's hearts stretch less than men's during a valve leak, a difference invisible to echocardiograms, meaning women may currently be referred for valve surgery too late.

## Twenty-Dollar Burgers in a Haunted House

**Rohit Chandra**, a veteran Yahoo! engineer/technologist (one of the few people who outlasted seven Yahoo! CEOs between 2004–2017) with no health-care background, was recruited in 2022 as Cleveland Clinic's chief digital officer specifically because he came from outside medicine. His role, paired with Mihaljevic, was to be the cross-pollinating "impresario" for the thousand-flowers strategy — matching more Debbie Kwons with more Chris Nguyens. Meeting the author over $20 burgers at the InterContinental Hotel on Cleveland Clinic's campus, Chandra lays out the industry's dire economics: 2023 US health-care spending hit $4.9 trillion (up 7.5% year over year); per KFF, nearly 40% of hospitals operated at a loss and the median "winning" hospital's margin was just 2%, since Medicare/Medicaid and private insurers fix or squeeze reimbursement while costs (12.4% cumulative inflation 2021–2023) keep rising. Chandra likens the situation to being "the guy in a horror movie who knows the house is haunted but can't convince anyone else to flee" — one early meeting to discuss an AI/cost-saving idea took ten weeks to schedule, and the department head then asked what the meeting was even about. His conclusion: identifying AI opportunities is easy; identifying the right people is everything. Of Cleveland Clinic's 80,000 employees, he can name fewer than ten who will genuinely "live and die with this problem" rather than treat AI pilots as entertainment — his term for them becomes "samurai." His first named recruit: **Eric Boose**.

## The Farmer and the Samurai

**Eric Boose** grew up on a multigenerational Norwalk, Ohio produce farm, the first in his family to go into health care. A family physician since the early 2000s, he had an aptitude for electronic health records (EHRs) and rose to associate chief medical information officer, splitting time between practicing medicine and being a "Sherpa" for other doctors adopting new software. Chandra tapped him to lead Cleveland Clinic's 2024 pilot of **AI ambient scribe** software — apps that listen to doctor-patient visits (with consent), transcribe and filter the conversation via NLP, and auto-populate orders (labs, imaging, medications) and EHR-formatted notes, cutting the need for doctors to type during or after visits. The pilot tested five different scribe apps across 50 volunteer doctors each (250 total), and quality varied far more than expected: some vendors' output was clean, others needed heavy editing; apps struggled with accents and complex medical terminology, and had to be tuned to distinguish clinically relevant small talk (e.g., recent travel, relevant to diagnosis) from irrelevant chatter. Adoption itself was a bigger obstacle than the tech — many volunteer doctors never turned the app on or ghosted follow-up, and Mihaljevic confirmed he wouldn't mandate use until "we have a firm conviction that the mandate really translates into improvement of patient care." Boose's low-key, farmer-like persistence (chasing feedback, pushing vendors for days-fast fixes because they were small companies eager for a Cleveland Clinic reference) kept the pilot alive.

In 2025 Cleveland Clinic signed a five-year contract with **Ambience**, a San Francisco company, based on hard data: across 25,000 patient encounters, doctors chose to use Ambience's AI-generated notes 80% of the time; 67% of clinicians said it reduced cognitive burden; 32% said it increased face time with patients; and same-day chart closures (which speed billing) rose 7%. By the time of writing, roughly 4,000 providers were using it voluntarily, including a physician nearing retirement who said, "I think I can work for another year or two with this," and others who called it "life-changing." Boose, on the modest-sounding minutes saved per visit: "So are our margins."

## Hospitals and Hotels

**Rita Pappas**, a first-generation Lebanese American who trained as a nurse before switching careers at 31 to attend medical school, became a Cleveland Clinic pediatric hospitalist and pediatrics-wide process reformer (asthma care pathways, "family-centered rounds" that improved patient/nurse satisfaction) before being handed one of the hospital's biggest operational headaches: patient flow. Hospitals, she notes, are like hotels with beds, staff, and linens but no reliable sense of when "guests" arrive or leave — and Cleveland Clinic runs an unusually unpredictable version of this because it has one of the country's highest case mix indexes (CMI), treating very sick, often transferred patients needing longer, more complex stays. Cleveland Clinic had used Epic's **Grand Central** module since 2011 for admissions/discharges/transfers, but found it rigid and hard to customize; other vendors wanted to sell pre-built products rather than co-design. In October 2021 Cleveland Clinic ran a hackathon with **Palantir**, and by December 2021 had a working system — **Hospital 360** — a real-time dashboard integrating staffing platforms, physician notes, and Epic EHR data to forecast discharges (e.g., predicting 91 discharges by end of day and 132 more the next day) and expose bottlenecks like nursing shortages in real time.

Hospital 360 didn't speed anything up by itself, but it made root causes visible; a roughly 25-person nonmedical/nontechnical team still processes bed swaps, transfers, and discharges (previously done "short order cook–style," batched at day's end) but now works continuously. Measured gains: daily transfer volume up 10%, earlier same-day admissions, minutes shaved from each stage of a patient's surgical journey, and ER wait times cut by 90 minutes. The processing team initially feared job losses; Pappas told them "we need your eyes on what's happening — you're the final check," and while Cleveland Clinic isn't adding headcount as it grows, it hasn't eliminated the team either. Pappas is already scoping a "version 2.0" that would auto-trigger next steps (e.g., pushing a pending echo order to the top of a lab's queue). She also offers a pointed diagnosis of physician resistance to change: medical training conditions doctors toward individualistic, hyper-competitive achievement ("me, me, me, me, me") and, given the length and hierarchy of medical training, toward conservatism — meaning that for AI to become a true partner in medicine, medical education itself, and the kind of people who become doctors, may need to change.

## Sepsis

Checking back in with Chandra after three years on the job, he's self-critical ("I've done maybe three or four things") but names one clear win: bringing AI into **sepsis prediction**. Sepsis — the body's infection response spiraling into organ failure — kills about 350,000 Americans annually (more than breast cancer, prostate cancer, and opioid overdoses combined), yet nearly 40% of cases in a 2019 study weren't identified until critical, and 81% of Americans can't name its symptoms. Hospital costs for sepsis-related stays hit $52 billion in 2021. At Cleveland Clinic, sepsis was involved in two of every five patient deaths as of 2022. Chandra found two committed clinical partners: **Dr. James Morrison** (splitting time between ER and ICU, chairing the Sepsis Committee, focused on system-wide strategy across Cleveland, Florida, and overseas sites) and **Allie Tallman** (a navy brat/ER doctor's daughter who leads the **Sepsis Emergency Response Team (SERT)**, a few dozen nurse practitioners and PAs working the main-campus ICU). Tallman's personal stake: her healthy 74-year-old grandmother, misdiagnosed with a normal lumbar X-ray and sent home with muscle relaxers, was septic within days and dead within a week — "she didn't need to die that way." Their early, low-tech interventions included standardized sepsis order sets in Epic and a Mugatu ("Sepsis. So Hot Right Now.") meme campaign to keep frontline staff alert, since existing rule-based Epic sepsis alerts were so oversensitive they caused alert fatigue.

Cleveland Clinic partnered with **Bayesian Health**, founded by **Suchi Saria** (Johns Hopkins machine-learning/health-care lab director, who lost a nephew to sepsis and built the underlying prediction model in her academic lab). Saria initially hesitated to work with Cleveland Clinic, viewing large hospital systems as difficult and uncommitted, but was won over by Chandra's engineering background and persistence. The book includes an extended detour on **Epic**, the dominant EHR vendor founded by **Judith Faulkner** in 1979 (still CEO in her eighties, ~$5 billion in annual revenue, used by 21 of the top 22 US hospitals, only seven salespeople worldwide) — explaining why any AI vendor (like Bayesian) must be able to integrate with Epic without antagonizing it, since Epic can charge integration fees or deny access to outside tools.

The Bayesian pilot launched on Cleveland Clinic's main campus in May 2024. Early friction: the model over-generalized (e.g., mislabeling central nervous system infections as meningitis) and Tallman's core objection was that "we can't treat Main Campus with a blanket algorithm" — Cleveland Clinic's extreme case mix (heart center, digestive disease, surgical, neuro-cancer subspecialties) meant cardiac-related infections were routinely confused with sepsis by a model tuned elsewhere. The system used a three-level alert (level 1 = no concern, level 2 = moderate risk requiring monitoring, level 3 = immediate bedside attention), with nurse practitioners like **Dana Rubis** reviewing every alert against Epic notes and clinical judgment; doctor overrides fed back into Bayesian's training. At level three — the highest-stakes tier — the model had not yet reached 90% accuracy on Cleveland Clinic's main campus by the time of writing, a persistent frustration for Tallman even as she praised the tool's transparency (it explains its reasoning) and its preservation of clinician autonomy, echoing Morrison's framing that a good AI tool makes a doctor think "I kind of trust it, I'm going to go back there, I'm going to get some more of that" rather than removing them from the loop.

The pilot performed markedly better at **Fairview Hospital**, a smaller, 488-bed, lower-case-mix Cleveland Clinic community hospital, where standardized response protocols eliminated most of the "human friction" seen on the main campus — "There's no guesswork. A resident on day one can follow that same process just as much as James can." Overall, from 2021 to 2024, sepsis mortality across Cleveland Clinic fell 40%, though Morrison and Tallman are careful to note AI doesn't deserve sole credit — much of the improvement predates Bayesian, and some of it may reflect the Hawthorne effect (people behaving differently when observed). Mihaljevic's takeaway, and arguably the chapter's — and the whole Part's — thesis: "If you tell someone that even imperfect AI has contributed — even in a small way — to reducing sepsis mortality by 40 percent, you would have to call that success." AI, in his formulation, does not need to be perfect to be useful.
