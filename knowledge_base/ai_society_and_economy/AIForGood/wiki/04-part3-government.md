# Part 3: A Republic, If You Can Optimize It (Government)

Part 3 tests whether artificial intelligence can do what decades of reform commissions couldn't: make the federal government actually work. Tyrangiel builds the case through Operation Warp Speed's vaccine-logistics "moonshot" (Gus Perna, Deacon Maddox, and Palantir's Julie Bush and Aaron Jaffe), through Palantir's broader war with the Pentagon procurement establishment, through Eric Schmidt and Jennifer Pahlka's diagnosis of the "frozen middle" that resists modern software practices, through a small, almost accidental AI win in East Lansing's recycling program, through IRS commissioner Danny Werfel and CTO Kaschit Pandya's quiet, legally careful modernization of a sixty-year-old mainframe, and finally through DOGE's chaotic, results-free assault on the same bureaucracy. The throughline: AI can genuinely fix government dysfunction, but only when paired with someone who understands the human terrain — political cover, agile iteration, trust-building with career staff — and it fails, sometimes spectacularly, when technologists mistake speed and contempt for strategy.

## The General's Warning

Retired four-star general Gus Perna, who ran the logistics of Operation Warp Speed and left the army in 2021, opens Part 3 from his home in Alabama, now consulting for Palantir. Tyrangiel cites a bleak 2023 Gallup poll showing record-low public trust in institutions, plus concrete failures: the IRS answered only 35 percent of its calls during tax season, SNAP eligibility determinations had a 44 percent error rate, seven thousand pages of unemployment regulations stand between a jobless person and benefits, and Defense Department policy guidelines equal one hundred stacked copies of *War and Peace*. Perna insists AI could fix "everything" — organ donation, the IRS's staffing, army contracting rules — and pushes Tyrangiel to think bigger than the Department of Defense. But he cautions that Warp Speed's success depended on trusted subordinates who could translate his vision into execution against constant bureaucratic friction, without a pandemic's urgency to force cooperation next time. He points Tyrangiel toward his logistics chief, Corporal (later Colonel) Deacon Maddox.

## A Logistical Moonshot

Deacon Maddox, a career army logistician who'd worked with Perna since Fort Hood in 1998, became director of operations and planning for Operation Warp Speed in May 2020. Landing at HHS amid interagency turf wars (the CDC and FDA resented the Defense Department "playing in their sandbox"), Maddox needed a live, synchronized picture of an entirely new vaccine supply chain — data from manufacturers, the FDA, CDC, state agencies, and thousands of delivery trucks and pharmacies. Traditional defense contractors pitched slow, over-engineered solutions (a medical blockchain, hardware installed in every doctor's office). The fix came from Julie Bush, a Palantir "leverage" specialist (a Palantir-specific job title meaning she mapped who in government controlled spending and sold them what they needed) who had brokered Palantir's earliest federal deals and had a close relationship with Dr. Deborah Birx, the White House coronavirus response coordinator. Bush got Palantir a contract to build HHS Protect, which impressed Warp Speed's IT lead Brigadier General Robert Mikesh, who introduced her to Perna. Bush translated Perna's needs into a product vision: a constantly updated digital twin of the vaccine supply chain, made possible because Perna — unlike a private company — had the authority ("a hammer") to compel data-sharing from states, retailers, and distributors.

## Brief Moments for Exceptional Things

Tyrangiel explains what Palantir actually does: it "untangles hoses," standardizing an organization's disparate, messy data into a unified "ontology" (per chief architect Akshay Krishnaswamy) on top of which applications can be built — described as giving users from middle managers to four-star generals an "Iron Man suit." Palantir's battlefield use is cited via an anonymous 2024 US military official's remark that Ukraine's fight "is not much of a fight without [tablets] and Palantir." On Warp Speed, lead product engineer Aaron Jaffe (a Northwestern journalism grad) abandoned the contracted goal of a perfect end-to-end system once the FDA refused to share trial data, and instead applied agile-development principles — "embrace the chaos," ship partial visibility fast, iterate — to build a usable prototype in weeks rather than a comprehensive system in years. Deacon Maddox, empowered by Perna's political cover, approved the deviation from the original contract, which Maddox calls startlingly rare government behavior. Palantir engineers then spent weeks cleaning filthy incoming data (duplicate entries, mismatched formats, CSV dumps, undigitized rural providers) — work Jaffe calls mostly "human scut work with a dash of machine learning," while Palantir CEO Alex Karp insists AI was a genuine accelerant ("AI is software that works. And our stuff works."). The resulting platform, named Tiberius after Star Trek's Captain Kirk, let Perna track any vaccine vial from warehouse to arm, let states monitor their own inventory, and evolved rapidly — including a marketplace app (later converted to an international-donation app after Biden-administration concerns about fairness) letting states trade surplus doses. By mid-2021, as normal bureaucratic process reasserted itself, Jaffe felt the special, exceptional period closing: "you can feel that we are transitioning from exceptional to ordinary."

## Lululemon in the Iron Triangle

This chapter examines why Palantir is disliked by progressives and much of the military establishment. Cofounder Peter Thiel (a 2016 RNC speaker, Trump donor, and financier of the lawsuit that killed Gawker) explains much of the left's hostility; cofounder and CEO Alex Karp — born to a Black mother and Jewish father, severely dyslexic, and a Clinton/Biden/Harris supporter — draws separate resentment from the Washington "iron triangle" (Pentagon, Congress, lobbyists) for being loud, impatient, and openly patriotic about American exceptionalism while insisting Palantir must out-build competitors because it won't win contracts through insider relationships. Facing years of exclusion from defense contracts despite superior, cheaper technology, Palantir sued the Pentagon in 2016 for violating the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act (which bars new bloated procurement when off-the-shelf solutions exist) — a case that exposed the Defense Department still favoring a costlier, slower Raytheon/Northrop Grumman/Lockheed Martin consortium proposal. Palantir won decisively, opening the door to today's defense-tech boom (Palantir's market cap: $396 billion). Yet insiders still gripe that Palantir's Georgetown office sits near a Lululemon rather than the traditional contractor corridor near the Pentagon — a symbol of its outsider status even after victory. Karp frames the stakes broadly: friction-filled, low-output citizen interactions with government (immigration, health records, taxes) erode trust in the whole system.

## The Frozen Middle

*(Listed in the table of contents as "The Tragedy of the Frozen Middle"; the chapter heading itself reads "The Frozen Middle.")*

Invoking pediatrician John Gall's aphorism that "every complicated system that works evolved from a simple system that worked," Tyrangiel traces how government spending rules — designed for accountability — calcified into complexity that chokes anything genuinely new, like software. Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, reluctantly recruited by Defense Secretary Ashton Carter onto the Defense Innovation Board, concluded "you can't have AI in the government or the military until you solve the problem of software" — noting that ordinary commercial practices (rapid prototyping, user surveys, iterative deployment) are effectively illegal or forbidden by process in the federal government, including a mandated six-month review just to run a user survey. The Board's "Software Acquisition and Practices (SWAP) Study" made common-sense recommendations echoing fifteen prior military software reports dating to 1982, all ignored — the real obstacle being "the frozen middle," the career workforce with no incentive to change. Jennifer Pahlka, former deputy US CTO under Obama (crucial to the healthcare.gov rescue) and California's pandemic-era unemployment-system fixer, describes the dysfunction loop: rule-bound civil servants get punished either for following absurd process (bad software) or for deviating from it (political blowback), driving good people out of public service. Both Schmidt and Pahlka conclude government will underperform until it adopts industry software practices.

## An Emotional Nudge

A rare small-scale success story: Cliff Walls, five weeks into his job as East Lansing, Michigan's environmental sustainability manager, took a meeting with Prairie Robotics, a startup founded by two Saskatchewan technologists (CEO Sam Dietrich, CTO Stevan Mikha) with hands-on trash-hauling backgrounds. Their system mounted cameras and GPS on recycling trucks, using AI image recognition (trained on hundreds of thousands of images of contaminants like plastic bags, Styrofoam, and lawn clippings) to flag nonrecyclable items dumped in bins, then auto-generate mailed postcards to residents: a soft "educational nudge" or a sharper "emotional nudge" showing an actual photo of their contaminating item. Funding ($30,000 for two trucks) arrived almost by accident, redirected by the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) from a Recycling Partnership grant, sidestepping the normal budget-cycle gridlock. Across a 24-week, 7,000-household pilot (336,000 truck stops), the educational nudge cut contamination 11 percent and boosted participation 32 percent; the emotional nudge cut contamination 23 percent and increased bin-setout by 45 percent, with only two resident complaints and a 0.5 percent postcard error rate. Walls credits the program's survival to staying deliberately low-profile — "you have to make big AI policy things seem less big" — and Prairie Robotics has since expanded to nearly forty cities. Walls sees the same AI-image-recognition approach applying to pavement-condition surveys (PASER), tree clearance, fire hydrant visibility, and faded stop signs.

## AI Fight Club at the IRS

Rumors of a secretive "AI Fight Club" inside the notoriously antiquated IRS lead Tyrangiel to Commissioner Danny Werfel, who returned to run the IRS in 2022 after the Biden nomination (having previously served as acting commissioner in 2013 and led OMB's "reinventing government" efforts). Werfel describes the IRS's constraints: it can only make "five-degree turns," not 180-degree ones, given legal exposure and its "inherently governmental" designation, which bars outsourcing core functions like audit decisions (illustrated by his "you can't outsource the judge" courthouse analogy). Yet under this cover, the IRS was quietly using NLP for call routing, testing GPT-4 and Llama for code generation, using bespoke AI to flag tax evasion, and — most significantly — using AI to help translate its Kennedy-era Individual Master File (IMF) from 1960s COBOL and Assembly Language Code into modern languages, a ten-year modernization effort (begun 2014, 90 percent complete by November 2024) that had relied on manual "pair programming" between retiring COBOL engineers and modern developers before AI accelerated the process. CTO Kaschit Pandya frames the IRS's human-in-the-loop philosophy (a computer "advises," a human employee "makes the final decision") as both a legal necessity and a safeguard, citing a January 2023 Stanford/Michigan/Chicago/Treasury study finding Black taxpayers audited at 2.9–4.7 times the rate of non-Black taxpayers due to biased automated audit-selection algorithms targeting Earned Income Tax Credit claims — a finding Werfel confirmed and committed to correcting. Pandya, an Indian immigrant and self-described "modernization journey" evangelist, also describes the cultural challenge of moving thousands of IRS engineers off older tools (like SQL databases) toward AI-native platforms (like Databricks) without threatening their livelihoods. The chapter closes noting Trump's reelection the day after this reporting.

## Dilettantes and Vandals

DOGE (the Department of Government Efficiency), created by Trump's first-day executive order with Elon Musk as de facto leader, is portrayed as "policy by provocation" rather than genuine AI-driven reform. Despite elite technical talent — including Sahil Lavingia (early Pinterest employee, Gumroad founder) — DOGE produced no promised trillions in savings and little real software. Lavingia, assigned to the Department of Veterans Affairs, found no fraud but did find inefficiency (veterans still fax in health claims, which contractors like IBM and Booz Allen convert to digital text); he proposed a smartphone-photo-upload alternative using a Mistral AI document model that could save up to $200 million a year, but his superiors — including boss Justin Fulcher, who allegedly said DOGE could only hire Trump voters (a possible violation of federal antidiscrimination law) — told him DOGE wasn't interested in the "messy human work" of actually collaborating with VA staff and Congress to implement it. Lavingia was fired after 55 days following a media interview; he concluded DOGE was "a temp agency for software engineers to be deployed across the government to not build software." At the IRS, Werfel resigned on Inauguration Day; DOGE representatives Gavin Kliger (ex-Databricks, 25 years old) focused on pressuring employees to quit and mass firings, while Sam Corcos (a health-tech CEO) froze Pandya's modernization programs and restructured the org chart, wrongly equating IRS transaction volume with that of a midsize bank. Pandya stayed on through five interim commissioners and the loss of two thousand employees from his division, saying "someone needs to be here to pick up the pieces." Invoking technologist Anil Dash's line — "the purpose of a system is what it does" — Tyrangiel concludes DOGE's true output was performative destruction ("arrive, break things, leave"), a response to real government dysfunction that nonetheless failed to fix it, while Werfel's quieter, legally constrained IRS modernization showed a viable (if slower) alternative. The section, and Part 3, ends on the open question of whether Americans want government systems designed to work or systems designed to prove government can't.
