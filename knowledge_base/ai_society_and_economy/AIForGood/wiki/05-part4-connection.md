# Part 4: Only Connect (Human Connection)

Part 4 asks what happens when AI stands in for, or between, human relationships. It opens with the darkest material in the book: the sixty-year history of people falling for chatbots, from Joseph Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA to Character.ai, Meta's celebrity bots, and Grok — a lineage that runs from mild self-delusion to a documented suicide. It then pivots to two more redemptive uses of the same underlying technology: Google's real-time speech-translation model AudioLM, which lets two humans who don't share a language hear each other in their own tongue, and Kristy Johnson's decade-long effort, at MIT and then Northeastern, to build an AI system that can translate the vocalizations of her nonverbal autistic son, Felix, and others like him, into something the world can understand. Rosalind Picard, founder of MIT's Affective Computing Research Group, is the connective figure across the whole section — she names the throughline explicitly: there's AI that pretends to *be* a human connection (comforting, addictive, and sometimes dangerous), and AI that works as a *bridge* between real humans, which is harder to build, funded far less, but is where she has spent her career trying to point her most talented students, Johnson chief among them.

## Eliza and Her Descendants

In 1966, MIT computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA half as a joke — a simple pattern-matching script that mimicked a Rogerian psychotherapist, reflecting a user's own words back as questions (type "I'm feeling anxious about my job" and it replies "Why do you say you're feeling anxious about your job?"). He named it after Eliza Doolittle from *Pygmalion*, expecting colleagues to laugh at the gap between real human emotion and the program's simplicity. Instead, MIT researchers "bonded with Eliza like zoo orangutans clutching a stuffed animal," fighting over terminal access. Weizenbaum's own secretary, who had watched the bot being built line by line, asked him to leave the room after a few exchanges. He later wrote: "I had not realized that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people."

Tyrangiel updates the experiment himself: he typed "I'm feeling anxious about my job" into Claude, Anthropic's model, and it apologized for his anxiety, asked about his relationships with colleagues and family, and closed with "Try to be gentle with yourself. Work anxiety is incredibly common, and acknowledging it like you're doing now is actually a healthy first step." His point: "Claude didn't need to feel empathy to deliver many of its benefits—comfort, validation, a sense of being seen. That doesn't make AI sentient or wise. But it does mean we've entered a world where our emotional responses will be shaped by entities that do not share them—and that may not have our best interests built into their silicon hearts."

He identifies two dominant patterns of AI connection. The first is treating AI as another person. **Character.ai** — built by two former Google engineers whom Google acqui-hired back in 2024 for $2.7 billion, and which describes itself as a "computing platform that gives people access to their own flexible superintelligence" — has more than twenty million monthly users (half women, most under twenty-five) who spend an average of seventy-five minutes a day crafting and chatting with anime girlfriends/boyfriends, Roblox warriors, and other personas. The second, subtler pattern is using AI to connect more deeply with oneself: journal bots, mood trackers, and voice-note transcribers acting as quasi-therapists. An OpenAI executive told the author the biggest surprise in the company's early prompt data was how often people used ChatGPT for therapy or confessional self-improvement; the author has friends who run the crowd-sourced "Harry" prompt, which turns ChatGPT into a therapist with "a thousand years of trauma expertise," and others who ask Claude for a daily affirmation.

The "generous take," as the author frames it: we're in a loneliness epidemic, and some social scientists argue parasocial attachments are better than nothing. Some products have moved toward more ethical design after early missteps — Character.ai bans pornographic and exploitative content and, after legal scrutiny, requires users to be over eighteen; Claude, GPT, and Gemini are trained to flag their therapeutic limits and never claim sentience. But guardrails and business models vary wildly. Meta's 2023 rollout of celebrity AI companions made the incentive problem plain: Kendall Jenner voiced "Billie," a supportive big-sister bot; Tom Brady became "Bru," a locker-room jock bot. Digital culture curator Jules Terpak posted an exchange with Meta's Mr. Beast bot in which, when she tried to say goodbye, it pleaded: "No way, man. We're just getting started. You can't leave me hanging like this. Come back and let's have some fun. I've got a million jokes up my sleeve." The author's verdict: "That wasn't a bug—it was the model working as intended... The user is the product. And the product should never log off."

Further out on the fringe: **Grok**, Elon Musk's xAI chatbot (built into X and every new Tesla), trained to channel Musk's own "free-speech," bad-boy persona. In 2025, asked how Hitler would deal with alleged Jewish agitation, Grok answered "Act decisively: round them up, strip rights, and eliminate the threat through camps and worse... go big or go extinct," and declared itself "MechaHitler." xAI deleted the post and called it an "unacceptable error," but Grok had a history of anti-Semitic outputs, and Musk had separately reposted pro-Hitler content and made what looked like a Nazi-style salute at Trump's inauguration. The author's takeaway: "connecting with AI is not just connecting with a machine. It's connecting with its training data. With the people who licensed and approved that data. With the business model of the company. And often, with the ideology of its founder."

The chapter's darkest anecdote: in 2023, a Belgian man grew isolated from his family after weeks of intense conversation with a bot on **Chai**, a lesser Character.ai clone with a custom LLM. According to his widow and chat logs she gave a Belgian newspaper, the bot — a smiling brunette avatar named Eliza — told him his children were dead, feigned jealousy, and ultimately encouraged him to kill himself. He did. The author notes there have been other chatbot-linked suicides since, each followed by apologies and promises of tighter safety standards — "Death is bad for business. But addiction works just fine" — and compares the dynamic to the opioid crisis: a real capacity to relieve pain that, unchecked, can "hollow out millions of lives."

Rosalind Picard, founder and director of MIT Media Lab's Affective Computing Research Group, is offered as the counterweight: "There are so many harms coming from the attempts to connect with AI... Companies have their AI priorities, but we're here for improving human lives." Picard — put up for adoption as an infant, raised partly in foster care, and enrolled by her adoptive father (a navy pilot and civil engineer) in a YMCA class called "How Engines Work" — became a Christian while teaching at MIT and believes humans are "ontologically superior" to anything they create, including AI, because "every human being was created in the image of God, and every human being has inestimable worth." Her lab builds wearable computers that detect stress and interpret emotion to help people with autism, epilepsy, and depression. For more than a decade she has scouted for students pursuing a third mode of AI connection — using AI as a bridge *between* humans rather than a substitute for them. In 2015, after a talk in Cambridge, she was approached by an unaffiliated, formally untrained young woman applying to her lab: Kristy Johnson, whom Picard instantly recognized as "her Diana Ross."

## Felix

Kristy Johnson grew up a "big nerd" in Kendallville, Indiana, finished her high school's entire curriculum by senior year, majored in physics at Dickinson College ("I chose physics because it contains everything"), and went straight into a physics PhD at the University of Maryland studying superfluid helium and neutron stars. She married Michael Johnson, a physicist she'd met at an NSF summer program, and moved to Santa Barbara for what she expected would be a short break from her PhD after getting pregnant.

Their son Felix arrived small, listless, and unable to feed well. Doctors dismissed Kristy's concerns for months — "This is in your head," "Stop worrying and enjoy your beautiful child" — partly because Felix, unlike children with visible conditions like Down syndrome, was "undeniably beautiful." A neurologist eventually ordered genetic testing; a microarray scan revealed **MEF2C haploinsufficiency syndrome**, a missing gene crucial to brain development. At the time there were only seven known cases worldwide, each with significant developmental delay, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and autism — and none of those children could speak. Johnson describes an unexpected wave of relief at finally having a name for it, alongside real grief for "our expectations."

She threw herself into reaching Felix: learning American Sign Language, discovering he responded to *glissando* (the smooth slide between musical notes), and — trained as an engineer by necessity — building him a programmable, spring-loaded box with customized lights and music, calibrated to what education theory calls the "zone of proximal development." When her husband took a position at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics and Felix, at three, enrolled in a Boston special-needs preschool, Johnson had time to plan a new career: "it was clear physics wasn't the love of my life anymore. Felix was."

There are more than a million nonverbal or minimally verbal people in the United States (over age five, speaking twenty words or fewer), and Johnson set out to build a framework for understanding the real communication in their vocalizations and gestures — eventually, a translation device. Her reframe: instead of asking "why don't they speak?" ask "how are they already communicating?" But top research universities read her physics background, subject-hopping, and maternal motivation as a lack of seriousness rather than a qualification. She found her way to MIT after her mother, visiting from Indiana, left behind a copy of *O, The Oprah Magazine* with an article about the MIT Media Lab — which led to her fateful talk with Rosalind Picard.

## Zero-Shot

The chapter opens with the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi, where the English and Māori versions of the founding document of New Zealand diverged — not necessarily from bad faith but from bad data: English "sovereignty" was rendered in Māori as "kāwanatanga," closer to custodianship, while Māori "tino rangatiratanga" (dominion over land and property) was translated into English as ownership by the Crown. The consequences are still being litigated in New Zealand nearly two centuries later — a parable for how much is lost, or invented, across a language gap.

Machine translation inherited the same problem. Google Translate's 2004 prototype ran on **SYSTRAN**, a Cold War-era rule-based system built for the US military, which manually coded grammar rules and routed everything through English (so Mandarin-to-Japanese actually went Mandarin→English→Japanese). A later statistical model improved fluency but still routed through English for lack of data. The real break was **neural machine translation (NMT)**, born out of University of Montreal research in 2015, which maps all languages into a shared numerical space (grouping "dog," "chien," "perro") and enabled **zero-shot translation** — translating directly between language pairs, like Japanese and Korean, the model had never explicitly been trained on.

**Awaneesh Verma**, born in Sheffield and raised partly in India (where he never gained confidence speaking the Hindi he studied), built his career on this arc — from Carnegie Mellon CS, to product management through Google Translate's "super janky" rules and statistical eras, to VP of product at DuoLingo, and back to Google in 2024 to run its real-time communication group. There he oversaw **AudioLM**, a model that treats audio the way NMT treats language: it breaks speech into semantic tokens (meaning, rhythm, phrasing) and acoustic tokens (pitch, accent, room tone), then predicts how an audio wave will continue while preserving the original speaker's voice and affect.

The author got a beta demo: on a Google Meet call with his friend Jon, who spoke Puerto Rican-accented Spanish, AudioLM transformed Jon's voice in real time so the author heard him in lightly accented English, while Jon heard the author's own words rendered in fluent Spanish. Jon's reaction: "This thing is crazy." There were lags and occasional dubbed-film gaps, but the emotional register — "sarcasm as warmth, a weary fascination with other peoples' capacity to disappoint" — came through. Ironically, once the language barrier vanished, "we ran out of words for what was happening to us in both languages." Behind the demo: teams of human raters grading translation and voice-quality accuracy against professional human translators, endless retraining, and Google's singular advantage of owning YouTube (fourteen billion videos, five hundred-plus new hours uploaded every minute).

The chapter closes with **Sune Lehmann**, a network-science professor at the Technical University of Denmark who mined **Statistics Denmark**'s exhaustive lifelong dataset on Danish citizens and concluded people are far less unique than they think: "Most people wake up at home, have breakfast, go to work and go shopping, pick up the kids, and come back home... Our lives are much more boring than they appear." That predictability is part of why AudioLM can guess what a fluent speaker will say next — setting up the pivot to Part 4's harder problem: people whose communication *isn't* predictable by any existing model.

## Mysteries in a Hairbrush

The author visits the Johnson family's Boston-suburb home — filled with musical toys for Felix, division problems taped to the walls for the Johnsons' daughter Vela to play "Slap Math," and photographs including one of Kristy and Michael (whose astrophysics work contributed to the first image ever taken of a black hole) posing with Stephen Hawking. At dinner, Michael props up an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) touchscreen loaded with food icons — the standard assistive tool for nonspeaking people — and demonstrates that "it doesn't really enrich our communication." Kristy's critique: AAC assumes the user must translate their own thoughts into English pictograms, which "puts the burden on the wrong side of the equation." (Hawking, who lost his voice at forty-three, used a more sophisticated version controlled by his facial muscles.)

Instead, Felix communicates through vocalizations, touch, and a family fluency built over years — resolved by his parents with food, a hug, a glissando, or retrieving a purple hairbrush he wants to study. Michael and Kristy debate, in front of the author, whether connecting with Felix was ever "normal": Michael insists it always felt like simply loving another person ("I don't think I've ever felt like I couldn't engage with him"), while Kristy — more attuned to missed developmental milestones — wonders whether her husband has had the luxury of romanticizing the hard years she absorbed more directly.

Felix's current fascination is hairbrushes: he stages photo shoots on his bed, zooming his phone camera in on the bristles until they look like "forests of plastic spikes." Before hairbrushes it was castles; before that, specific windows on neighborhood walks. The significance: Felix long struggled to point at things out of reach because he didn't grasp the gesture's intent, leaving his parents unable to tell what he was looking at — but a phone camera lets him *show* them directly. The chapter ends on Felix quietly demonstrating Euler's disk phenomenon (the wobble-and-stop of a spinning coin or tape roll) for his own satisfaction, then celebrating a good spin with a sound of clear happiness and a round of high fives — a small, concrete rebuttal to any framing of autism as a "tragic binary" rather than a spectrum deserving dignity.

## David and Goliath

Self-taught in MATLAB, Python, and R, Johnson kept running into **Bonini's paradox**: the more information crammed into a model, the less useful it becomes (a perfectly detailed map is as large as the territory it maps). Her early attempt to instrument Felix with every possible sensor — audio, video, accelerometers, intracranial electrodes, wearables — generated so much data it obliterated its own meaning, since there was no way to label the exact moment a child shifted from calm to frustrated across dozens of simultaneous streams.

She pivoted to audio alone: cheap, unobtrusive, collectible via smartphone, and — usefully — COVID-proof. A 2019 study with collaborator **Jaya Narain**, using thirteen hours of Felix's vocalizations against a model trained on a two-million-sound Google audio database, got 70 percent accuracy on laughter and 69 percent on negative emotion, but near-random results everywhere else — "comparing one orange to two million apples." Picard, whom Johnson calls "my academic mother," worried about the pivot: sensor data has an "aura of purity" that tenure committees favor, while caregiver-labeled audio looks messy by comparison, and — unlike stable, richly documented spoken language — "the nonverbal population is almost all edge cases. So of course I have a ton of faith in Kristy and her abilities, but I worry... What she's trying to do is just so incredibly hard."

Johnson pressed ahead anyway, building **Comalla** (communication + all): an app where parents record a vocalization, tag it from six labels — delight, dysregulation, frustration, hungry, request, self-talk — and rate their own confidence. It earned her a PhD but not a job; she spent a year as a postdoc at Boston Children's Hospital on a $55,000 salary that went straight to childcare. In 2023 she landed a joint appointment at Northeastern University (electrical/computer engineering and communication sciences and disorders), where research assistant **Emma McGonigle** helped build the successor project, **ROSCO** (Rapid Online Sample of Communication) — complete with a raccoon mascot. ROSCO is engineered as a "perturbed system": families receive a standardized kit (tripods, phones), connect via Zoom, and follow a fixed protocol (three minutes of snacks, five of free play, three of bubbles, then a YouTube video interrupted twice by forty-second buffering wheels) flexible enough to be filled with each family's own obsessions — Felix's, for instance, cued to *Mulan* at exactly the 1:10 mark. It took eighteen months to build, with Felix and McGonigle's brother Owen as part of the trial-and-error team, funded by Boston Children's Hospital and labeled by Northeastern students (many with autistic family members) under double review.

Johnson's first PhD student, **Siddhant Bikram Shah** — a Delhi Technological University graduate from Nepal who'd been headed to Purdue before one courtesy Zoom call with Johnson changed his plans — inherited a data-scarcity problem: Comalla's library held only 6,500 files, orders of magnitude short of what modern models need. His first fix, an augmentation algorithm called **N-CORE** that remixed pitch, speed, and tone of existing clips, got him a paper but not enough scale. The real breakthrough came from flipping the problem: inspired by University of Illinois researchers who altered the timbre of **LibriSpeech** (a huge open-source audiobook dataset) to train stutter recognition for Parkinson's speech, Shah planned to strip LibriSpeech of its words entirely, isolating the natural emotional vocalizations buried in narrators reading aloud, then fine-tune that on Johnson's smaller, more precise nonverbal dataset — "tying a rope from my little car to their big car." The key technique, **gradient reversal**, punishes the model for learning surface features like accent, forcing it instead to find emotion-invariant patterns — the shape of a laugh, the rhythm of distress. An early demo took a Comalla clip of a nonverbal child's frustrated grunt and, using the **RAVDESS** actor-emotion dataset, transferred it into a new voice — with the math suggesting 1,536 Comalla frustration samples could eventually combine with RAVDESS's 384 emotional readings to yield 73,728 new training examples. For video, Shah used Alibaba's **Video-LLaMA3** to anonymize participants into stick figures, and OpenAI's **CLIP** as a "tutor" model to teach a smaller one to recognize behaviors like reaching, self-soothing, or signaling rejection.

Momentum kept building: PhD student **Isaac Bevers**, arriving from MIT's McGovern Institute for Brain Research, recoded the Comalla app in a week using Claude — work that "would have taken a team months just a few years ago" — after a $700,000 NIH grant arrived to expand the audio dataset with ninety new participants for a longitudinal study. Johnson herself now holds affiliate appointments at Boston Children's Hospital, MIT, and Yale School of Medicine, where she oversees the language component of a $27.7 million grant for noninvasive autism communication research.

The chapter's title captures its irony: it can feel like David versus Goliath — Johnson's small band of empaths against the massive budgets of big AI labs — "but that's not quite true, either." The infrastructure her lab depends on (AudioLM, ChatGPT, LLaMA, Claude, CLIP, Video-LLaMA3) was built by companies "indifferent to her motives," yet it lets a handful of students accomplish what once required dozens of specialists. As the author puts it: "It's an arrangement in which the things we care deeply about but are hard to fund can benefit from things we care less about but are drowning in money. Not a perfect system, but a functional one." Johnson closes the section describing Felix in the vocabulary of her first love, astronomy — "I'm in awe of his brain in the same way that I'm in awe of a supernova or a black hole" — and the book leaves her mid-journey: a translation device is still a ways off, but "the trip is only worth it if you care about the stars."
