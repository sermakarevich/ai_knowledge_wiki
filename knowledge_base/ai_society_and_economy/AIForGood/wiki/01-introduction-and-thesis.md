# Introduction and Thesis

The Introduction lays out how Josh Tyrangiel came to write the book and states its central argument: against the polarized, money-soaked debate between AI "doomers" and "accelerationists," a quieter "AI counterculture" of practical people is already using AI to fix broken systems in government, education, medicine, and human connection — and that work, not the hype or the doom, is the real story worth telling.

## The Perna video and the "AI awakening"

Tyrangiel's "AI awakening" starts late one night on YouTube, watching a lightly viewed talk by General Gustave Perna. Perna describes himself bluntly: "Forty years in the army... I was an average infantry officer. I was a great logistician." A month from retiring in May 2020, Perna got a Saturday-morning call from the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and arrived in Washington two days later to run Operation Warp Speed — "his arsenal consisted of three colonels and no plan."

What Perna says he needed most was "to see myself": real-time visibility into a boundaryless battlefield spanning state and federal agencies, drug companies, hospitals, pharmacies, manufacturers, truckers, and dry-ice makers, all standardized into data he could act on. Tyrangiel reduces the scale of the problem to a single material — plastic — noting Perna needed visibility into national plastic supply for syringes, needles, bags, and vials, or he risked having "hundreds of millions of vaccine doses and nothing to put them in."

Washington consultants paraded through his office for an hour each but couldn't even define the problem ("Excruciating," Perna says). Then he met Julie and Aaron from Palantir, the data-analytics company that "uses artificial intelligence to clean and visualize complex datasets." They told him, "Sir, we're going to give you all the data you need so that you can assess, determine risk, and make decisions rapidly." Perna hired them on the spot.

Within weeks he had his "God view" of the problem; a few months later, Operation Warp Speed delivered vaccines to all fifty states simultaneously, and Perna could rebut a panicked governor's claim of being shorted vaccines by pulling up the exact vial count on screen: "'Oh, no, general, that's not true.' Oh, yes. It is."

Watching this alone at night, Tyrangiel felt something he wasn't expecting — optimism — arriving, as he puts it, "on the back of a retired general talking about enterprise software." The leap he makes is explicit: if AI could help end a pandemic, it could remake the citizen's relationship with government — better chatbots for veterans benefits, student loans, SNAP, unemployment, social security, Medicare; a "warp speed" IRS that no longer makes taxpayers guess what they owe; smarter public health surveillance, infrastructure maintenance, and disaster response. "We have the technology. We've already used it."

## How the assignment began: the doomer/accelerationist Crusades

The backstory: in spring 2023, David Shipley, then editorial page editor at *The Washington Post*, called Tyrangiel after OpenAI's release of ChatGPT had turned AI coverage into "a kind of information hellscape." Shipley's idea was to send in "a tourist" — a columnist without daily deadlines — to make sense of the noise. Instead, Tyrangiel writes, "I'd landed in the Crusades."

He found the loudest AI voices split into two camps. **Accelerationists** argued we were on the cusp of an awesome future — AI already predicting viral outbreaks, soon delivering personalized knowledge assistants, mitigated climate change, cured diseases — provided we "move quickly" to stay ahead of Chinese competition and reap the benefits.

**Doomers** countered with scenarios from AI-manufactured superviruses to societal decay, job loss, authoritarian consolidation, and "the drain of all meaning," some casually trading a mathematical shorthand for extinction risk, "p(doom)," "as casually as they would their star signs."

Despite opposite conclusions, Tyrangiel notes both camps shared "brilliance, certainty, delight at being players in a turbulent drama. A hairball of motives."

He pauses to note that "AI" itself is a slippery term — a "constellation of overlapping techniques": predictive systems, classifiers (labeling images, sorting email, flagging tumors), optimizers (logistics, pricing, financial bets), generative systems (text, images, video, code), and the theoretical, further-off prospect of artificial general intelligence. What unites them "isn't sentience but speed."

## Following the money

Tyrangiel then turns to the financial stakes: the McKinsey Global Institute estimate of $4.4 trillion in annual corporate profits available from generative AI alone, and Morgan Stanley's estimate of $40 trillion more in operational efficiencies. His point isn't the size of the numbers but who captures them — unlike the Manhattan Project or the space race, "the pursuit of artificial intelligence in the United States is a private enterprise."

He traces the conflicts of interest on both sides: Google quietly dropped "Don't be evil" from its code of conduct in 2018; Meta (rebranded from Facebook amid a 2021 ethics crisis) never bothered much with pretense, with growth as "Zuck's true north" — and both companies were leading AI deployment while their executives were among the loudest accelerationists. Academic AI researchers, meanwhile, were drawing million-dollar checks from the same tech giants whose products they were supposed to evaluate dispassionately.

Doomers had their own entanglements. Tyrangiel singles out Elon Musk — OpenAI cofounder turned "leading dissident," who called AI humanity's "biggest existential threat," signed a letter demanding a six-month pause on systems more powerful than GPT-4, while simultaneously competing with former partner Sam Altman via his own AI startup, xAI. Even "minor-league doomers," he notes, found business booming, since "the more dramatic the concern, the more lucrative the engagement" — "In the mob they call this a protection racket."

He extends the doomers and accelerationists "a dollop of sympathy": AI is a seventy-year-old, ill-defined field, and its builders (largely non-native English speakers, often neurodivergent, "also almost all men") had little reservoir of public trust to draw on, especially given the same companies' record on social media.

The absurdity crystallizes for him in Salesforce's ad campaign starring Matthew McConaughey wandering AI-generated landscapes talking to a squirrel — "a perfect mirror of the AI moment": saturating hype detached from everyday life, with benefits accruing mostly "to people who were already very rich." As Tyrangiel puts it, "The whole thing made sense only if you were baked out of your mind."

## Danny Hillis and the pivot: "imagine the tech without the tech companies"

The turn in the Introduction comes via Danny Hillis — an early Arpanet user, pioneer of parallel processing that led to cloud computing, whom Tyrangiel places on computer science's "Mount Rushmore" in "the Teddy Roosevelt spot." When Tyrangiel, exasperated, asked Hillis what AI is actually good for, Hillis answered: "Try to imagine the tech without the tech companies."

Hillis — MIT-trained, a veteran of government consulting and Disney Imagineering, who once sold Google a knowledge graph only to watch it get used to target ads — knew that people like his younger self were quietly experimenting with AI "in ways no one was talking about," with more meaningful outcomes than another chatbot-calendar integration.

That reframing sent Tyrangiel toward Perna and Operation Warp Speed, and from there to "promising threads elsewhere — in other government agencies, in education, health care, human connection — where people were tinkering with AI to make the things that matter in the world work better." He names this loose group the **AI counterculture**, and says "It's stories from this AI counterculture that make up this book."

## Who the counterculture is, and the book's argument

Tyrangiel characterizes the people he profiles as, on the whole, "quiet, practical, with little vanity," often lacking prior software expertise, who kept working a stubborn problem past the point of comfort — "stubborn or desperate enough — or just cared with enough irrational force — to keep going."

He contrasts this with the "move fast and break things" ethos: "Progress is slow. Smashing is fast. You don't need to understand a thing to destroy it." The people in the book, he writes, are "brilliant, fanatically impatient" but don't reach for the hammer — they're frustrated with broken systems but don't believe people or institutions are garbage, "Just broken," and take on "the divine, ridiculous task of trying to fix what they love, using a technology they're only beginning to understand."

He's explicit that this isn't meant as a rebuttal to AI's dangers: "The downsides of AI are real — misuse, malfunction, the temptation to replace people instead of teaching them new skills. They're arriving in droves whether we like it or not." His argument is instead about the futility of the "defensive crouch": "they don't actually stop anything. They just ensure you get hit while looking the other way."

The alternative he endorses is to "get your hands dirty and make it work to preserve and improve the things you love" — which he frames not as naive optimism but "enlightened self-interest": "If we don't shape AI for good, it will be shaped by people who don't know or care about our problems. If we don't teach it what matters, someone else will teach it what's profitable."

He closes the Introduction with the book's operating premise: "The choice isn't between a world with AI and a world without it. That ship has sailed. The choice is between AI designed by people who think fixing things is worth the trouble, and AI designed by people who think breaking things is more efficient."

## Scope of the book

The Introduction sets up the four-part structure that follows, covering AI's use in education (Part 1, "I Believe the Children Are Our Future"), medicine (Part 2, "Physician, Heal Thyself"), government (Part 3, "A Republic, If You Can Optimize It" — including Perna's own fuller story, "The General's Warning" and "A Logistical Moonshot"), and human connection (Part 4, "Only Connect").
