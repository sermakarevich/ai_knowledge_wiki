# Part 1: I Believe the Children Are Our Future (Education)

Part 1 uses one company, Khan Academy, and its GPT-4-powered tutoring product Khanmigo, as a case study for what happens when a well-intentioned, well-resourced, mission-driven organization tries to bring generative AI into classrooms in real time — starting from a secret 2022 demo of GPT-4, through a chaotic public launch, into actual classrooms in Hobart, Indiana, and Newark, New Jersey. The throughline is that Khan Academy is the disciplined counterexample to an EdTech industry littered with expensive failures (LAUSD's "Ed," Summit Learning, Knewton, AltSchool), but even with Sal Khan's credibility, OpenAI's frontier model, and genuine care about students, the result is not a triumphant story of AI fixing education — it's messy, slow, expensive, occasionally dangerous, often disappointing, and ultimately dependent on human teachers who are willing to adapt. The author's recurring point is that the technology doesn't replace teaching; at best it clears space for teachers to be "more human."

## Ed's Dead

This chapter opens with the cautionary tale that frames everything else in Part 1: Ed, the Los Angeles Unified School District's AI chatbot, built by AllHere Education and launched in March 2024 by Superintendent Alberto Carvalho as "our nation's very first AI-powered learning-acceleration platform." Within months AllHere furloughed most of its staff, a whistleblower alleged mishandling of student data, the company filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy, and founder/CEO Joanna Smith-Griffin was arrested on securities fraud, wire fraud, and aggravated identity theft charges (accused of misusing investor funds for her wedding and a home down payment). The author calls it "the full Theranos" and situates Ed alongside other EdTech graveyard entries: Summit Learning (Mark Zuckerberg-backed, accused of screen addiction and data-collection overreach), Knewton (claimed predictive AI, couldn't predict its own collapse), and AltSchool (an ex-Google executive's $150 million experiment that reached only a few thousand students).

Against that backdrop, the chapter introduces Sal Khan, founder of the nonprofit Khan Academy (190 million registered users in 190+ countries, started in 2004 from videos Khan made to tutor his cousins). Khan turned down OpenAI cofounder Greg Brockman's invitation to try GPT-3 in early 2021, but in summer 2022 he and Khan Academy's chief learning officer, Kristen DiCerbo, signed NDAs and became among the first outsiders to see GPT-4, months before the public knew it existed. Khan describes being stunned when the model correctly answered and explained an AP biology question and generated well-written follow-up questions; DiCerbo was struck that GPT-4's answer on "learning styles" (a debunked pedagogical concept) matched exactly what she'd tell a teacher. Both wanted the model to guide students to answers rather than hand them out — the first lesson in system-prompting ("You are a tutor and I am a student. Do not give me the answer").

Khan brought the decision to a Khan Academy hackathon in Mountain View, splitting the organization between "this is a game changer" enthusiasm and serious alarm over GPT-4's flaws: it could be bullied into changing correct math answers, it hallucinated fake sources, and no one had yet probed its biases or potential for inappropriate conversations with a twelve-year-old. Khan ultimately decided Khan Academy should build the product rather than cede the space to less careful actors — "I think we're in a position to do it best... because we actually do care" — and proposed turning every identified risk into a feature (guardrails, moderation, anchoring on Khan Academy's own vetted content).

## How to Train Your Tutor (While Slowly Losing Your Mind)

A technical/explanatory interlude on how transformer-based LLMs actually work (the "GPT" in ChatGPT), including the scale of GPT-4's training (~10^25 FLOPs) and the core caveat that recurs throughout the book: the model "optimizes for fluency, not truth" and has no way to assess whether its own output is correct.

The chapter then covers the informal, high-speed nature of the OpenAI–Khan Academy collaboration: no contracts, no kickoff meetings, "just a Vegas-style corporate elopement." Khan Academy was one of several partners (alongside Duolingo, Stripe, Morgan Stanley) competing for OpenAI's limited attention during the GPT-4 launch push. Early friction included Khan Academy naively sending OpenAI its proprietary curriculum content assuming it would be used to retrain the model (OpenAI had already moved past that stage). The real work turned out not to be engineering but prompting — DiCerbo and Khan's team spent weeks as, in Khan's words, "pioneers in a new coding language: English," iterating system prompts (one of Khan's first: "Pretend you're a tutor... What would you say to help them figure it out without giving away the answer?"). The chapter stresses the probabilistic, non-deterministic nature of LLM outputs (the same prompt yields different results across runs) and the fact that GPT-4 was still being tuned by OpenAI for its own goals throughout this period, repeatedly breaking prompts that had been working.

## You'll Be Disappointed for a Long Time Until You're Not

This chapter follows the five-month sprint to Khanmigo's March 14, 2023 launch. OpenAI assigned "solution strategist" Jessica Shieh (ex-Deloitte/McKinsey, a lifelong Sal Khan fan who moved her wake-up time to 4 a.m. for the project) to push Khan Academy to "think bigger" than basic content-generation features. Shieh introduced "context stuffing" — loading the system prompt with detailed, per-student context (course, unit, misconceptions) since ChatGPT then had no persistent memory — while noting its costs: manual effort to write and test prompts across every grade/subject/state-standard combination, and real dollar costs (GPT-4 pricing at the time: 3¢/1,000 input tokens, 6¢/1,000 output tokens, multiplied across millions of tutoring sessions).

Then, on November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) to the public without warning Khan Academy — reaching a million users within five days and prompting districts including New York City to ban it over cheating fears. Khan was "annoyed" and constrained from commenting publicly by his NDA. Within about a week Khan Academy reframed the surprise release as a "blessing" (DiCerbo) because it surfaced the huge range of misuse cases (cheating, phishing scams, bomb-making questions, "what would Hitler do") that informed the safety work still ahead. A December on-site meeting introduced Khan Academy to formal red-teaming — testing the model with profanity, self-harm language, and sensitive history topics. Notable failure: ChatGPT, not trained on Khan Academy's vetted history materials, described the Trail of Tears as a "government-sponsored hike." The team also had to redesign how the bot handled coded expressions of suicidal ideation (e.g., students saying "unalive myself") after finding the model's default deflection ("Let's get back to math") was inadequate.

Jessica Shieh then ran an intensive five-day sprint with Khan Academy engineers to build the actual Khanmigo prototype — refining prompts iteratively, teaching the team to spot adversarial inputs, and coaching a "craftsmanship" process of drafting rough prompts and letting educators refine them. Shieh's own summary of the model's behavior over time: "the weird thing about the model is that you'll be disappointed for a long time until you're not." Separately, the team discovered GPT-4 could not be trusted to do reliable math (it would cave to a user insisting a wrong answer was right), so Khanmigo was engineered to use GPT-4 only for conversation/explanation while offloading actual calculations to Python. The launch nearly slipped — Shieh recounts still not having a model OpenAI was satisfied with 72 hours before the March 14, 2023 launch date.

## Get Buffington

Because of NDA secrecy, Khanmigo launched with essentially no users — none of Khan Academy's District schools had access on day one. Sal Khan pushed to loosen the NDA and pilot the product early with real schools. An initial test at Khan Academy's own Khan Lab School in Mountain View was judged non-representative (most students there have tech-industry parents). Vicki Zubovic, Khan Academy's chief external relations officer, argued instead for starting with Title I (low-income) schools already using Khan Academy Districts.

That led to Dr. Peggy Buffington, superintendent of schools in Hobart, Indiana (six schools, ~4,000 students), described as a larger-than-life local figure ("Be Excellent on Purpose!") who agreed to the pilot on a week's notice in February 2023, trusting Khan personally. A dozen Hobart High teachers used Khanmigo for lesson planning and student tutoring, kept deliberately low-profile to avoid friction with the district and Indiana's Department of Education. Early problems: Khanmigo's self-harm flagging system was miscalibrated (missing real distress or over-flagging sarcasm); students bullied/cursed at the bot; some tricked it into giving answers (one student rerouted a geometry question through Khanmigo's "historical figure" chat feature to get Pythagoras to explain his own theorem); others simply abandoned it for ChatGPT or Snapchat AI when it wouldn't comply. Despite the rough start, Buffington reported Khan Academy's developers fixed reported bugs almost immediately, and teachers began to see real value in Khanmigo's ability to let advanced students move ahead while giving struggling, embarrassed students private help — addressing what Buffington calls the classic problem of "teaching to the middle." Buffington's closing observation, and a recurring theme: "The biggest problem they gotta fix is kids don't know how to ask questions."

## Kids Say the Darndest Things

The author visits First Avenue School in Newark's North Ward (pre-K–8, ~1,100 students), a poorer and more racially diverse district than Hobart, guided by special assistant Alan Usherenko (a former Khan Academy devotee since 2009 who ran an unofficial after-school Khan Academy program) and principal Neysa Miranda (more skeptical of EdTech hype in general). Newark, despite the city's reputation for poverty and decline, had unexpectedly become the highest-engagement Khan Academy Districts user in the country.

This chapter is built around direct interviews with students, giving the book's most candid, unvarnished user feedback:
- **DJ and Noor** (4th graders): DJ describes Khanmigo helping him not "shut down" when he doesn't understand something, functioning almost as an emotional-regulation aid ("it gives me more confidence not to shut down"). Noor, whose mother doesn't speak much English, uses Khanmigo to translate/re-explain math problems into Arabic so her mother can help her.
- **Stella and Nyla** (8th graders, top students and debate champions): openly unimpressed. Stella argues Khanmigo "is not close to the same as an actual teacher," especially for visual learners who need someone to physically show steps. Nyla barely uses it except to check answers after finishing. Both complain Khanmigo's lack of persistent memory (wiped each session, for privacy) forces them to re-explain context every time, and that its phrasing of steps sometimes conflicts with what their teacher taught, creating confusion.

Teachers Leticia Colon and Jennifer Cook defend the tool as "part of a solution... not the only solution," but echo Buffington's exact concern from Hobart: most students are too passive and don't know how to ask good questions. The chapter closes on Usherenko and Miranda's mixed but hopeful assessment: Khanmigo is "promising, imperfect, improving," and "desperately needed humans to help it succeed."

## More Human Today

The concluding chapter surveys a year of iteration and mixed results. After a full school year (53 districts, 65,000 students), Khan Academy shipped visual/chart support for geometry and calculus, loosened overly strict language filters, added limited memory/personalization (DJ can now get pro-wrestling-themed word problems), and — to address the "kids don't know how to ask questions" problem — added speech-to-text input and "dynamic action bubbles" (AI-generated multiple-choice prompts students can just pick rather than compose). In one Newark school, teachers even had Khanmigo generate lists of good questions students should ask it, then printed and taped these as cheat sheets to desks — a moment the author flags as almost absurdly recursive ("Teaching using AI to help children use an AI that had been designed to help teach them").

Growth was strong in raw numbers — Khanmigo's second year saw 200 districts and roughly 680,000 students sign up, a 10x increase — but Sal Khan himself is candid about the limits: self-motivated "high flyers" benefit, but many disengaged students just repeat "I don't know" without real effort, and he states plainly there's "no simple answer for student engagement." A Newark student, Stella, is quoted making the same point more bluntly: "There comes a certain point where some kids just don't want to learn... there's nothing you can do to make them."

The chapter's more optimistic material comes from Khanmigo's roughly thirty teacher-facing tools (rubric generation, curriculum unpacking, exit tickets) and profiles of individual Hobart teachers who redesigned their own practice around it:
- **Alaina Richter** (4th-grade teacher), who stopped using Khanmigo as a forced drag through material and instead let students use it only to affirm mastery after she'd already taught a unit — turning practice time into what she called "Math Madness" and tripling scores and practice time.
- **Matt Atherton** (7th-grade English/language arts), who used Khanmigo's writing-coach feature to generate "awesome" vs. "not awesome" example sentences for students to critique, freeing him to focus on students' actual ideas rather than first-pass grammar notes.
- **Melissa Higgason** (high school physics/chemistry), whose "Designing the Ideal Airbag" stoichiometry lab — built by prompting Khanmigo for lab ideas using baking soda, vinegar, and Ziploc bags, then scaffolding it into a three-act structure — is the book's centerpiece success story. Freed from lecturing, Higgason spent the period moving between lab groups; her own verdict, which gives the chapter its title: "AI made this class more human today."

The chapter, and Part 1, end on the author's own synthesis: the fantasy of AI simply replacing teachers is false. What actually changes, when it changes at all, is "piecemeal and quiet," and depends entirely on teachers "willing to adapt, trusted to experiment" — the technology only clears space for that human work, it doesn't do it for them.
