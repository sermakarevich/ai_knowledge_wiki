# The AI economy: Interconnected

**Paper:** [The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback loops and speeds of change](/Users/sergii/Downloads/the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback-loops-and-speeds-of-change.pdf) — McKinsey Global Institute, September 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Think of the AI economy like an orchestra where the violin section plays at double speed, the brass section marches in slow motion, and the conductor only just got the sheet music. AI model capabilities are sprinting ahead exponentially while power grids, chip factories, and data centers are built brick by brick over years, and companies take even longer to rewrite how their people actually work. When one section falls behind, the whole performance stumbles — chips pile up with nowhere to plug them in, or powerful tools sit unused because no one redesigned the workflow. The report's advice is to stop tuning a single instrument and start conducting the whole orchestra, speeding up what lags and slowing down what is running dangerously ahead.

## TL;DR

The McKinsey Global Institute's September 2026 report argues that answering AI's big questions about jobs, investment returns, regulation, and company ROI requires a systems view of interconnected forces rather than technology analysis alone. Its central diagnosis is mismatched timelines: AI capabilities advance exponentially (reliably automatable task complexity doubling roughly every four months since 2023), physical infrastructure expands linearly over years, and organizational and institutional adaptation spans years and lags furthest, so easing one bottleneck exposes the next while adoption that outruns governance creates backlash risk. It organizes the system into Foundations (capabilities, R&D, physical resources, institutional stance), Transmission (adoption and adaptation), and Outcomes (productivity, innovation, demand, jobs, capital, business landscape, geopolitics, well-being), bound together by feedback loops in which better models drive adoption, investment, infrastructure, and still better models. The prescription is to manage at multiple speeds — accelerating lagging infrastructure, skills, and workflows while sometimes deliberately slowing deployment — and to look beyond efficiency toward new businesses, faster organizational learning, and public trust.

## Problem & Motivation

The report starts from four questions that cannot be answered from the technology in isolation, each belonging to a different stakeholder. Workers ask whether forecasts of mass job loss, including one extreme prediction of half of entry-level white-collar jobs disappearing within five years, are credible or scaremongering. Investors ask whether the enormous sums flowing into chips, power, and data centers will pay off or end in overcapacity and losses echoing the railway and dot-com booms. Policymakers ask how tightly to regulate without handing advantage to lighter-touch rivals on diverging American, European, and Chinese paths. And CEOs ask why rolling out the technology has not shown up in results. Each answer, the report argues, lives in the interactions: jobs depend on whether AI performs tasks as well and as cheaply as people and whether new tasks and demand emerge fast enough; investment pays off only if demand for AI services grows fast enough to fill the capacity being built; regulation must balance unmanaged risk against competitive disadvantage; and company returns depend as much on organizational adaptation as on the models themselves. The motivation is therefore to supply a general framework, grounded in prior research and client work and visualized in the Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 4 system maps, that connects capabilities, resources, institutions, businesses, markets, geopolitics, and people so leaders can see where hurdles and opportunities emerge as the pieces move at different speeds.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Mismatched timelines as the master bottleneck** — The report's organizing claim is that general-purpose technologies only pay off once complementary innovation, infrastructure, rules, capital, and willing organizations arrive, a gap that historically took decades to close (steam roughly a century to peak growth impact, electricity about forty years, computers and the internet about twenty-five). AI compresses the capability side into an exponential curve while construction and manufacturing timelines stay linear and company redesign stays uneven and slow, so constraints cascade: frontier chips and packaging bound progress in 2023, then electricity and grid connections took over, and the next constraints are expected in applications, skills, and workflows rather than hardware.

2. **Foundations–Transmission–Outcomes with feedback loops** — The system is divided into three analytical layers where Foundations (what AI can do and under what conditions) feed Transmission (whether and how people and organizations put capabilities to work) to produce Outcomes (productivity, innovation, demand, jobs, capital shifts, business landscape, geopolitics, well-being), with feedback running through every part. Better models encourage adoption, adoption attracts investment, investment expands infrastructure and R&D, and that speeds still better models, while shortages of power, weak governance, or public concern slow deployment. The categories deliberately overlap, since innovation and new demand are intertwined, and the framework is presented as an organizer of what matters most rather than a full inventory.

3. **Brains, brawn, rules, and nutrients** — Capabilities, physical resources, institutional stance, and R&D are cast in a memorable division of labor in which capabilities are the brains, physical resources the brawn, institutions the rules, and R&D the nutrients. R&D improves capabilities directly and stretches physical resources further through chip and data efficiency, while institutions shape every stage from export controls and safety rules through privacy, liability, and professional standards to the trust that determines whether deployment sticks. This framing explains why optimizing one piece in isolation, in the spirit of the von Bertalanffy, Forrester, and Ackoff sidebar lineage, fails when the binding constraint lies elsewhere.

4. **From attention to intention, and beyond original uses** — Commerce is described as shifting from the internet era's focus on attention toward AI's focus on intention, with suppliers better matching hidden or latent buyer needs. As with steam moving from mine pumps to shipping and railroads, and electricity moving from gas-light replacement to refrigeration and redesigned factories, AI's first uses of automating existing work are framed as rarely the most consequential, with hard-to-anticipate products, businesses, and business models expected to carry the largest long-run effects.

5. **Manage at multiple speeds** — Because exponential capability growth, slow infrastructure expansion, and multi-year productivity conversion coexist, the leadership doctrine is to watch the whole system and intervene at the speed each part requires. Sometimes that means accelerating what lags, such as power delivery, permitting, skills, or workflow redesign, and other times it means slowing down deployment that has outrun safeguards, governance, or public acceptance. Investor, policymaker, business, and individual playbooks all hang from this doctrine, from following bottlenecks to making policy responsive rather than settled in advance.

## Key Findings

Model capabilities are improving exponentially by several measures while the physical and organizational substrate lags markedly. The leading model succeeds half the time at tasks taking humans more than sixteen hours, the leading graduate-level science benchmark score reached 95 percent in 2026 against 39 percent roughly three years earlier, and reliably automatable task complexity has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 and every four months since 2023, aided by AI itself writing code, generating data, and running evaluations for the next generation. Embodied systems are gaining dexterity in navigation and manipulation but grow less reliable outside robot-designed settings. Against this, global data center capacity grew by 15 gigawatts in 2025 against roughly 18 gigawatts of demand growth, per-query compute, power, and water costs have fallen with efficiency yet total demand rises faster than efficiency or new supply can offset, and many facilities are pre-leased before opening. The 2023 packaging crunch, in which only TSMC could fuse Nvidia's H100 with memory at scale and buyers faced up to eleven months of lead time for a finished server, gave way to an electricity wall in which Microsoft held undeployable chips for lack of powered space and the median new American power plant took about five years from interconnection request to operation, up from under two in 2008, alongside copper and critical-mineral stress pointing toward a possible sizable mid-2030s shortfall, community opposition blocking or delaying at least 75 American projects worth some $130 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone, and shortages of trainable engineers.

Adoption is broad but shallow, governance is fragmented, and aggregate productivity has barely moved even as task-level effects look large. Nearly nine in ten organizations regularly use AI in at least one function, yet outside the 6 percent of high performers only about one-quarter redesigned workflows rather than inserting AI into existing ones, only about 30 percent reached level 3 or higher on a four-level governance maturity scale, only 22 percent of operations leaders report a fully implemented enterprise AI strategy, roughly one in three organizations lack a formal pre-deployment security check, and oversight defaults to IT or engineering while 70 percent of CEOs claim the greatest strategic influence. Trust is thin, with only 47 percent trusting companies to protect personal data, and cyber risk rises with the attack surface as models graduate from barely completing beginner tasks two years ago to autonomously executing multi-step test-network attacks taking professionals days. Average annual American nonfarm labor productivity from late 2022 through the first half of 2026 ran at 2.5 percent against a 2.1 percent long-run average, too soon to call structural, even as nearly one-third of American workers using AI saved three or more hours weekly, drug-discovery timelines shortened by as much as 80 percent with 1.5 times the drugs per cost, and 72 percent of American workers could see more than 15 percent of their working time reinvented by 2035. Capital follows the bottlenecks from chips and data centers toward electricity and grid, broadening from venture capital into public equity, private credit, and public debt with circular intra-ecosystem financing that blurs inside versus outside demand; seven leading companies spent a combined $756 billion on capital investment and R&D in 2025, McKinsey estimates $7 trillion in cumulative global data-center investment over 2025–2030 with over 40 percent in America, and American AI-related goods imports alone track about $750 billion in 2026. Geopolitics and sentiment split along familiar lines, with 55 percent of notable models since 2022 American-built against 23 percent Chinese, leverage exerted through American chip-export controls and Chinese rare-earth controls, personal queries rising to 73 percent of consumer ChatGPT use by mid-2025, and optimism concentrating in developing economies and the global south while advanced-economy publics, especially young Americans, stay wary over security, jobs, and privacy.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

For businesses the report urges looking beyond efficiency, since cost cuts that rivals can copy get competed away, and instead pairing AI with hard-to-replicate moats such as proprietary data, scale, customer relationships, and scarce physical assets inside models that deliver greater value to customers and suppliers. Leaders should spend freed resources building new products, businesses, and markets rather than stopping at automation, recognizing that a small number of standout firms historically drove disproportionate productivity growth through strategic moves into new arenas. Because capabilities improve faster than companies change, incumbents should exploit their scale advantage in customers, workers, and operations to test and gather feedback while building the capacity to adapt systems and workflows as fast as models improve, keep a live view of how AI could reshape advantage and profit pools in their industry, and earn support by delivering benefits people can see rather than merely explaining intentions. For investors the guidance is to follow the bottlenecks from compute toward electricity, infrastructure, and productive in-organization use, to expect different growth paths rather than assuming exponential capabilities mean exponential returns for every supplier or user, to look past raw adoption toward productivity-backed growth, new businesses, and legacy-constraint removal, and to watch for correlated exposures across chips, data centers, power, and software to the same AI-demand shock. For policymakers the agenda is to target each country or region's most binding constraint as it moves, to promote competition so rules do not entrench incumbents and access to skills, tools, and capital stays broad, to keep policy responsive through evidence-led adjustment and early self-regulation rather than settling every question in advance, to build trust by addressing jobs, privacy, and safety while expanding shared opportunity, and to foster investment by choosing what must be built at home versus accessed through partners, citing vehicles such as the United Kingdom's Sovereign AI Fund and the European Union's InvestAI. For individuals the counsel is to cultivate AI fluency and judgment about when to rely on human creativity, empathy, adaptability, and critical thinking, to weigh where shared personal information could end up before volunteering sensitive context, and to expand ambitions since capabilities that once required expertise, resources, or large teams are newly attemptable. Across all of this the report asks readers to track the framework's metrics, from benchmark scores and agent task lengths through capacity, interconnection queues, permitting times, pilot-to-scale shares, workflow-redesign rates, productivity and R&D outputs, model origins, trade flows, and trust surveys, since history suggests each technology wave's impact arrives faster and there may be less time to adjust than electricity or the internet allowed.

## Authors & Institutions

The report is authored by the McKinsey Global Institute, founded in 1990 as McKinsey's independent, fact-based economics research arm funded by McKinsey partners rather than commissioned work, organizing its agenda around productivity and prosperity, world resources, human potential, global connections, and future technologies and markets. The analysis draws on McKinsey client work and prior research alongside outside sources cited throughout, including METR for agent task-duration measures, the Stanford HAI 2026 AI Index and Epoch AI for investment and model counts, McKinsey's data-center supply model and state-of-AI surveys, Ipsos AI Monitor panels for trust, the UK AI Security Institute for cyber-capability evaluations, Data Center Watch for project delays, the International Energy Agency for copper, the American Medical Association and US Census Bureau for professional and worker use, the National Bureau of Economic Research for usage patterns, and UN and World Bank datasets for the e-commerce analogy. Geopolitically the narrative centers on American and Chinese ecosystems, with the United States narrowly leading frontier models and high-end compute and China leading open-weight models and industrial deployment, while institutional examples span American, European, and British policy vehicles.
