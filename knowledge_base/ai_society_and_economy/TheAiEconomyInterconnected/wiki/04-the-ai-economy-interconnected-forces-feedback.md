# The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback

**In one sentence:** AI works as an interconnected system in which foundations (capabilities, R&D, physical resources, institutional stance) drive adoption and adaptation, whose economic and social outcomes then feed back into further AI development.

## Key points

- Exhibit 4 frames AI as an interconnected system where a change in one part ripples through the rest, linking foundations, adoption/adaptation, and outcomes with feedback loops.
- AI foundations — capabilities, R&D, physical resources, and institutional stance — jointly determine what AI can do and how fast it can be built, with each shaping the others.
- Frontier AI R&D is mainly concentrated in a small group of companies in the United States and China, and progress is tied to investment, computing power, data, electricity, talent, and government policy.
- Physical resources for AI data centers (chips, copper wires, cooling, land, electricity, water) are reshaping trade and supply chains, and geographically concentrated resources such as rare earths can become geopolitical leverage.
- Adoption runs at different speeds: individuals experiment almost immediately while businesses move slowly and unevenly, with large firms aided by capital, small firms by agility, and midsize firms potentially struggling.
- Scaling AI successes attract further infrastructure and R&D investment, while failures expose governance, security, and skills weaknesses that shape the next round of regulation and organizational change.
- Economy-wide productivity gains require workflow redesign plus diffusion plus growth, because without growth, labor-productivity gains could cut employment and consumer spending — which accounts for more than half of global GDP — reducing returns on capital and inviting backlash.
- AI is already accelerating discovery, compressing parts of biomedical research from years into hours via protein-structure prediction, identifying new antibiotic candidates from vast molecular libraries, and speeding materials science.

## Foundations

AI's foundations — capabilities, R&D, physical resources, and institutional stance — determine what AI can do and how fast it can be built. Each shapes the others: R&D drives capabilities, capabilities require resources, and institutions influence how AI is developed and deployed.

**Covers:** Report p. 10, "Foundations" section

## AI capabilities

What AI can do extends beyond models and agents to embodied systems such as robots. Capabilities are the product of R&D, human expertise, data, computing resources, and the capital that funds their development. As capabilities advance, new uses become possible, organizations reconsider what can be automated, and governments reassess AI's risks. Whether the potential is realized depends on reliability, cost, public acceptance, and organizational readiness.

**Covers:** Report p. 10, "AI capabilities" section

## R&D

Research and development on frontier AI is mainly concentrated in a small group of companies in the United States and China. It improves model capabilities directly, and it makes physical resources go further by raising the efficiency of chips and data use. Progress is tied to private and public investment, computing power, data, electricity, talent, and government policy. Feedback mechanisms named:

- Profits can fund the next round of research, and expectations of future profits attract investment.
- Export controls can limit access to resources and affect what researchers work on or how they innovate.
- Breakthroughs can shift markets and geopolitical advantage.

**Covers:** Report pp. 10–11, "R&D" section

## Physical resources

The list of physical resources needed to build AI data centers and keep them running is long: chips, copper wires, cooling systems, land, electricity, and water are "just some of the required ingredients." Strong AI demand plus this wide range of inputs is reshaping global trade and supply chains, sometimes provoking local disputes over utility costs and the visual impact of vast new facilities. At the global level, some key resources are geographically concentrated and can become instruments of geopolitical leverage, "as China's restrictions on rare earth exports make clear."

**Covers:** Report p. 11, "Physical resources" section

## Institutional stance

Institutions — the rules, norms, and organizations that govern AI — shape the technology at every stage:

- At the outset, export controls and rules on data, infrastructure, and safety can influence what gets researched and built, where, and at what cost.
- During use, privacy and liability rules, professional standards, and company policies can shape how quickly and widely AI is adopted.
- As the technology spreads, its effects feed back into institutions, prompting new regulation and governance practices, influencing social norms, and building or eroding public trust.

Open questions listed: how much testing should be required before deployment, what incidents companies must disclose, and who is liable when the technology causes harm.

**Covers:** Report p. 11, "Institutional stance" section

## Transmission

"Think of technological capabilities, physical resources and institutional stance as the brains, brawn, and rules that shape AI's development. R&D supplies the nutrients." Together they determine what AI can do and at what cost; the next question is whether — and how — people and organizations put those capabilities to work.

**Covers:** Report p. 11, "Transmission" section (verbatim brains/brawn/rules/nutrients framing)

## Adoption and adaptation

Consumers, businesses, and governments are all adopting AI, but at different speeds. Individuals can pick up new tools right away, and millions experimenting with them almost immediately can help fuel innovation faster than institutional adoption often allows. Businesses move more slowly and often unevenly: redesigning workflows takes time, and workers may resist for fear of losing their jobs. Adoption is uneven by company size — greater financial resources may help large companies invest, smaller firms can benefit from greater agility, and midsize companies "with less of either advantage, may struggle to keep pace." Feedback loop: as organizations scale AI, successes attract further investment in infrastructure and R&D, while failures expose weaknesses in governance, security, and skills that shape the next round of regulation and organizational change.

**Covers:** Report p. 11, "Adoption and adaptation" section

## Outcomes

Putting AI to work changes economies, societies, and institutional stances. Some effects appear quickly, such as higher productivity in specific tasks; others emerge gradually as organizations redesign work, investment shifts, new products appear, and governments respond. As with earlier general-purpose technologies, some of AI's largest effects may come later, from innovations that are difficult to anticipate today. All outcomes feed back into AI's development and adoption.

**Covers:** Report p. 11, "Outcomes" section

## Productivity growth

AI has already made many individual tasks more efficient, but company- or economy-wide productivity requires more: companies must often redesign entire workflows, those changes must spread across many companies and industries, and growth must be part of the equation. Without growth, improvements in labor productivity could reduce employment and, in turn, consumer spending, "which accounts for more than half of global GDP." Lower spending could reduce returns on capital, discourage further investment, and generate backlash.

On timing, the chunk invokes the productivity paradox: transformative technologies "can seem ever-present and yet leave little trace in macroeconomic data." Verbatim quote: Robert Solow "famously captured the idea early in the digital era with his 1987 quip that 'you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.'" The paradox "resurfaced with the internet and is being debated again with AI."

**Covers:** Report pp. 11–12, "Productivity growth" section

## Innovation

AI is already speeding invention and discovery. Named examples: AI systems that predict protein structures have "compressed into hours parts of biomedical research that once took years," models screening vast molecular libraries "have identified new antibiotic candidates," and "[p]rogress in materials science is accelerating in much the same way."

**Covers:** Report p. 12, "Innovation" section (chunk ends mid-section)
