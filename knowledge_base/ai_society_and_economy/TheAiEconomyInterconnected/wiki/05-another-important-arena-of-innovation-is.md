# Innovation arenas: commerce, new demand, and beyond original uses

**In one sentence:** AI extends commerce from attention to intention, creates new demand through cheaper goods, higher incomes, and markets for delegated tasks, and — like steam and electricity before it — enables products and business models difficult to anticipate, while reshaping jobs, time use, capital flows, firm boundaries, geopolitics, and well-being.

## Key points

- AI shifts commerce from the internet era's focus on attention toward intention by helping suppliers understand buyer intent and match products to hidden or latent needs.
- Like steam power (mine pumps → shipping → railroads) and electricity (gas-light replacement → refrigeration, reorganized factories), AI is expected to enable entirely new products and business models.
- New demand arises from building the AI economy itself (models, robots, infrastructure, cybersecurity, change support), from delegating personal tasks (letters, trips, homework, finances, cleaning, cooking, yard work), from Jevons-paradox cheaper access, and from higher incomes plus Ford-style shorter hours and more leisure.
- About one-third of current US employment is in occupations enabled by earlier general-purpose technologies (truck drivers, mechanics, electricians, developers, cybersecurity analysts); whether AI creates more jobs than it destroys depends on new work plus fast-enough demand growth.
- AI reorganizes work around human-machine collaboration, affecting knowledge work (computing, accounting, law), rewarding judgment, curiosity, and conflict resolution, narrowing some junior–senior gaps while making the first career rung harder and potentially favoring higher-wage occupations.
- Capital has shifted from chips and data centers to electricity and grid, funded beyond venture capital by public equity, private credit, and public debt, with circular AI-company financing that obscures inside-vs-outside demand and spreads losses.
- McKinsey estimates $7 trillion cumulative global data-center investment 2025–2030 (over 40% in the US); 2026 US AI-related goods imports are on track for ~$750 billion — about as much as cars, oil and gas, and pharmaceuticals combined — while total private investment/GDP stays flat and overbuilding risk echoes railways and the internet.
- Public attitudes split geographically: ~70% of US respondents say AI will make personal information less secure, roughly half of Americans under 30 expect a negative societal effect, while developing/middle-income countries and an 80,000-user, 159-country Claude study (optimism concentrated in Africa, South America, much of Asia) are more positive.

## Commerce: from attention to intention

AI could help suppliers better understand buyer intent, improving matches between products and users and spurring new solutions for hidden or latent needs. The internet reduced search costs and connected global buyers and sellers via marketplaces; AI takes this further, shifting commerce "from the internet era's focus on attention toward a greater focus on intention."

## Beyond original uses

Technologies evolve beyond original uses: steam power began pumping mine water before transforming shipping and railroads; electricity was sold as gas-light replacement before powering homes and reorganizing factory production. Electricity also enabled refrigeration, transforming food production, transport, sale, and consumption. AI could similarly enable hard-to-anticipate products, businesses, and business models.

**Covers:** historical-analogy passage, p. 13

## New demand

| Channel | Mechanism / example |
|---|---|
| Building the AI economy | Demand for models, applications, robots, infrastructure, cybersecurity, organizational-change support |
| Delegated personal tasks | Drafting letters, planning trips, homework help, household finances; with robotics, cleaning, cooking, yard work |
| Cheaper access (Jevons paradox) | Greater efficiency increases total consumption rather than reducing it |
| Higher incomes + free time | Productivity gains give money and time to spend; precedent: US auto mass production → higher wages, shorter hours, five-day workweek; "Henry Ford explicitly argued that workers needed both the money and leisure to consume what industry produced — including automobiles." |

## Jobs

Most worried-over consequence, yet history shows major technological change creates new jobs while eliminating others — about one-third of US employment is in occupations that did not exist before earlier general-purpose technologies enabled them. Net outcome depends on new kinds of work plus enough new demand fast enough to offset automation losses.

Within jobs, processes reorganize around human-machine collaboration. Unlike earlier automation of physical labor, AI changes knowledge work (computing, accounting, law): routine tasks shift to AI, productivity rises, people move to complex tasks, and judgment, curiosity, and conflict resolution become more valuable — though the human-machine division keeps shifting. Entry-level workers may find the first rung harder to reach; lower-skill workers show evidence of narrowing the gap with experienced workers; shifts could favor higher-wage over lower-wage occupations with difficult transitions. Worker benefit versus threat shapes public support, adoption, and regulation.

**Covers:** Jobs subsection, pp. 13–14

## Time freed

Faster or fully taken-over tasks free time from paid and unpaid work, redirectable to more work, AI-created tasks, learning, entrepreneurship, unpaid activities, leisure/rest; efficiency can also raise demand for existing work, absorbing saved time. Some freed time is unwanted — fewer hours because less labor is needed, though workers prefer more. Ultimate use shapes output and demand; sustained growth requires income plus willingness to buy additional output.

**Covers:** Time freed subsection, p. 14

## Capital shifts

Bottleneck-driven: shortage of compute → chips and data centers; electricity/grid constraints → larger infrastructure. Financing broadened beyond venture capital to public equity, private credit, public debt. Circular financing — AI companies investing in or financing one another's growth — accelerates build-out but blurs inside-ecosystem versus outside demand and creates contagion interdependencies.

Scale: McKinsey estimates $7 trillion cumulative global data-center investment 2025–2030, much AI-driven, more than 40 percent in the US. In 2026 alone, US AI-related goods imports on track ~$750 billion — about as much as cars, oil and gas, and pharmaceuticals combined. Total private investment as share of GDP flat even as data-center investment soared; overbuilding risk parallels railway and internet booms (excess capacity, investor losses, ultimately substantial benefits).

**Covers:** Capital shifts subsection, p. 14

## Business landscape

AI changes company–customer–supplier relations: some firms in-source as AI cuts outside help and raises value of connected data/workflows/decisions; others depend more on suppliers controlling data, infrastructure, or customer access. AI lowers entry barriers for small firms/startups (coding, marketing, service, admin with fewer people/providers). Shifting boundaries turn suppliers/customers into competitors.

Access to the best model gives only temporary edge (leaders quickly overtaken/matched); AI commoditizes once-distinctive capabilities, shifting advantage to non-replicable choke points — critical workflows, customer relationships, administrative requirements such as medical-billing documentation — so choke-point holders may capture outsize value, and the best AI builders/users may not capture the greatest gains.

**Covers:** Business landscape subsection, pp. 14–15

## Geopolitics

US and China race, with balance-of-power stakes: US narrowly leads frontier models and high-end compute; China leads open-weight models and industrial AI deployment. Effective harnessing brings growth, influence, defense resources, and direct military enhancement. Control over chips, compute, models, standards becomes leverage, like oil/gas dependence; reliance on foreign-built/run AI risks data exposure, service/infrastructure disruption, and worst-case trade, cyber, or military conflict.

**Covers:** Geopolitics subsection, p. 15

## People, society, and well-being

AI changes how billions think: expertise once expensive (tutoring, translation, health/legal/financial navigation) becomes broadly reachable, but routine-thinking reliance may weaken reasoning habits, especially for children. Workers offload routine tasks toward judgment, creativity, problem-solving, human interaction — yet higher output in less time can raise cognitive demands, overload, and burnout risk.

Sentiment shapes regulation and use: advanced-economy publics are wary — in the US ~70 percent say AI will make personal information less secure, roughly half of adults under 30 expect a negative societal effect — while developing/middle-income countries expect economic and job-market improvement. Anthropic's 80,000+ Claude users across 159 countries show the same divide: Africa, South America, much of Asia more optimistic, seeing economic opportunity, versus Europe and the US.
