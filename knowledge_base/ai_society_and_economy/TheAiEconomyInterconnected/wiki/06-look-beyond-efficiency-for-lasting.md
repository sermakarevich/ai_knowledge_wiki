# Look Beyond Efficiency for Lasting Advantage
**In one sentence:** Lasting advantage from AI comes not from cutting costs alone — which rivals can copy and compete away — but from combining AI with hard-to-replicate moats, building new businesses, learning faster than capabilities evolve, and aligning business, investor, policy, and individual choices with the whole system's constraints.
## Key points
- Efficiency-only AI gains erode: competitors with the same technology achieve similar gains, driving down prices and erasing the initial advantage.
- Durable outperformance requires pairing AI with non-replicable moats — proprietary data, scale, strong customer relationships, scarce physical assets — in a model delivering greater value to customers and suppliers.
- First uses are rarely the most consequential: as with electricity replacing gas lighting before transforming industries, leaders should use freed-up resources to build new products, businesses, and markets.
- Economy-wide productivity growth has been driven by a small number of standout companies shifting strategy and entering new businesses, not by making existing businesses more efficient.
- Incumbents can out-learn startups if they exploit scale (more customers, workers, operations to test and gather feedback) by building capacity to adapt systems and workflows as fast as AI capabilities improve.
- Value creation fails under high societal resistance: people support change when they experience better products, services, or ways of working, and resist when jobs, livelihoods, and privacy feel at risk.
- Investor and policy outcomes hinge on system speeds: model capabilities can grow exponentially while physical infrastructure expands slowly and firms take years to convert AI into productivity and profits.

## Look beyond efficiency for lasting advantage
AI can reduce costs and make existing work more productive, but competitors with access to the same technology may achieve similar gains, driving down prices and eroding any initial advantage. Long-term outperformance requires combining AI with advantages rivals cannot easily replicate — proprietary data, scale, strong customer relationships, and access to scarce physical assets — in a business model that delivers greater value to both customers and suppliers.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Look beyond efficiency for lasting advantage"

## Build new things
> "The first uses of major new technologies are rarely the most consequential. Electricity began as a replacement for gas lighting before transforming industries and enabling entirely new forms of economic activity."

AI may likewise create new products, businesses, and markets difficult to anticipate today; automating existing work is only a first step, and leaders should use freed-up resources to build new sources of growth. A small number of standout companies have driven a disproportionate share of economy-wide productivity growth, largely through shifts in strategy and moves into new businesses that reshape industry segments, rather than by making existing businesses more efficient.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Build new things"

## Learn to learn faster
AI capabilities can improve exponentially, much faster than many companies can change technology, workflows, or organization. New businesses may have an edge by building around AI from the start without legacy systems, while established companies have a scale advantage — more customers, workers, and operations to test approaches and gather feedback — that only counts if they build capacity to adapt systems and ways of working quickly enough to keep pace.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Learn to learn faster"

## Give people a reason to support change
Whether employees, customers, and communities see AI as making lives better or worse shapes willingness to accept and use it; leaders must deliver benefits people can see and experience rather than simply explaining intentions. If people experience better products, services, or ways of working they may support change; if harms feel greater than benefits — jobs, livelihoods, and privacy at risk — resistance may mount. A systems view shows businesses may struggle to create value despite technological progress if societal resistance is high.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Give people a reason to support change"

## Develop a view of how AI could reshape your industry
AI could change who has an advantage, how companies compete, and where profits are made — lowering barriers to entry, shifting power among companies, customers, and suppliers, or changing the advantages of scale. Leaders should consider how these forces could play out in their industry and what they mean for strategy.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Develop a view of how AI could reshape your industry"

## Watch the whole system
AI's trajectory depends on more than technology improvement: changes in adoption, infrastructure, investment, regulation, and economic outcomes can reinforce or constrain one another, and the report's framework identifies metrics for tracking these changes across the AI system.

**Covers:** report pp. 16, section "Watch the whole system"

## For investors
- **Follow the bottlenecks:** constraints create investment opportunities by generating demand for solutions; when computing power was scarce, opportunities emerged around chipmakers and frontier-model companies, and as constraints shift toward electricity, infrastructure, and productive use inside organizations, new opportunities may emerge there.
- **Expect different growth paths:** exponential growth in capabilities or usage will not translate into exponential growth for every AI-using or supplying business; model capabilities improve rapidly while physical infrastructure expands much more slowly and companies may take years to translate AI into productivity and profits.
- **Look past adoption:** a company can adopt AI at grand scale without changing long-term prospects; signs of lasting advantage are productivity gains supporting growth (not cost reduction alone), new products/businesses/markets emerging, overcoming legacy technology and organizational constraints, and shifting resources toward new opportunities.
- **Look for correlated risks:** AI-system investments may be highly correlated across industries — e.g. chips, data centers, power, and AI software could all fall on the same AI-demand or investment slowdown — so investors should assess how diversified exposures really are.

**Covers:** report pp. 16–17, section "For investors"

## For policymakers
- **Address system constraints:** what holds AI back differs by country/region and changes over time — electricity, digital infrastructure, permitting, skills, institutional capacity — so identify the most binding constraints and move policy with them.
- **Promote competition:** AI may lower the cost of creating new products and services; rules should not unnecessarily favor incumbents or hinder entrants, and access to skills, tools, and capital to build with AI should be broadened.
- **Make policy responsive:** capabilities and applications can evolve faster than laws; not every use or risk can be anticipated and some predicted risks may never materialize, so adjust rules as evidence changes rather than settling every question in advance, starting with industry self-regulation and staying close to businesses, workers, researchers, and the public.
- **Build trust and confidence:** realizing decades of AI-driven growth and living-standard gains may depend on public acceptance; address legitimate jobs, privacy, and safety concerns, make the case for what AI could make possible, and expand access to skills, technology, and business-building resources so people can share in opportunities.
- **Foster investment:** decide which AI capabilities must be developed at home versus accessed through partners, and strengthen computing, energy, research, and talent via public investment, incentives, and partnerships without replicating the entire ecosystem — e.g. the United Kingdom's Sovereign AI Fund and the European Union's InvestAI initiative to mobilize public and private AI-infrastructure investment.

**Covers:** report pp. 17, section "For policymakers"

## For individuals
- **Cultivate AI fluency:** as AI grows in work and daily life, learn to use it and when to rely on human judgment; creativity, empathy, adaptability, and critical thinking may become more valuable as the people–AI division of labor shifts, requiring continuous learning.
- **Be thoughtful about what you share:** personal information as context can make AI tools more useful but creates risks — before sharing sensitive financial, health, or personal information, consider where it could end up, who might access it, and how it could be used.
- **Expand your ambitions:** AI gives individuals capabilities that once required specialized expertise, substantial resources, or large teams, changing what is realistically attemptable — learning a new field, creating a product or service, starting a business, or pursuing a previously out-of-reach idea.

**Covers:** report pp. 17, section "For individuals"

## Conclusion
History suggests each technology wave's economic impact arrives faster: steam power took about a century from commercialization to peak growth impact, electricity about 40 years, and computers and the internet about 25 — and it is too early to know whether AI compresses that timeline further, adding urgency since businesses, workers, and governments may have less time to adjust. AI's trajectory will not be set by technology alone — models improve, costs fall, new uses emerge — but by how capabilities interact with physical resources, institutions, organizations, markets, and people moving at different speeds, where one constraint can slow the rest and progress feeds back into the system; the task is not to predict a single AI future but to understand interactions, mismatches, and how choices alter outcomes.

**Covers:** report pp. 18, section "Conclusion"
