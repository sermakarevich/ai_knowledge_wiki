# Data center construction itself is now
**In one sentence:** Data-center construction and physical inputs cannot keep pace with fast-rising AI demand while capabilities advance far faster than organizational reorganization, governance, and security can absorb, so AI must be managed as an interdependent system rather than isolated parts.
## Key points
- Data-center construction is a bottleneck, especially in the United States, which accounts for nearly half of global installed data-center capacity; per-query compute, power, and water costs have fallen with efficiency, but total demand is rising faster than efficiency gains or new supply can offset, facilities are expanding rapidly yet cannot keep pace, and many are pre-leased before opening.
- Community opposition blocked or delayed at least 75 US data-center projects worth some $130 billion in Q1 2026 alone — matching all of 2025 in three months, per Data Center Watch — over electricity prices and large unsightly facilities, causing permitting delays and freezes, including New York's July signing of the first-ever statewide permitting freeze on large data centers.
- Copper and other critical minerals for chips and wiring show stress, new mines and processing plants take years to permit and build, the world could face a sizable copper shortfall by the mid-2030s unless plans accelerate (IEA), AI competes with many industries for those materials, and developers also report shortages of engineers and other workers who take time to train.
- Reliably automatable task complexity — measured by how long the same tasks take a human expert — has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 and roughly every four months since 2023, partly because AI writes code, generates data, and runs evaluations used to train the next generation; technologies available today could in theory automate more than half of current work hours in the US, Europe, and Latin America.
- Tools exist but reorganization does not: in a McKinsey survey of 1,719 respondents across 97 countries (May–June 2026), nearly nine in ten said their organizations regularly use AI in at least one function, yet outside the 6 percent who are AI high performers, only about one-quarter reported redesigning workflows around AI rather than inserting it into existing ones.
- Governance, strategy, and security lag: only about 30 percent of ~500 organizations reached level 3 or higher on a four-level maturity scale (Dec 2025–Jan 2026 survey), only 22 percent of operations leaders report a fully developed and implemented enterprise AI strategy though more than half name strategy as the biggest driver of returns, responsibility is fragmented across IT/legal/data/HR/risk/strategy with oversight defaulting to IT or engineering while 70 percent of CEOs claim greatest influence, and roughly one in three organizations still have no formal pre-deployment AI security-risk check.
- Cyber risk grows with adoption: more AI systems mean more attack surface, AI is a more capable hacker tool (in April 2026 the UK AISI found Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview could autonomously execute multi-step attacks on test networks taking human professionals days, versus best models barely completing beginner-level cyber tasks two years earlier), AI-generated phishing/fraud is harder to spot at unmatched scale, and a single large AI-enabled cyberattack or governance failure causing major financial or physical harm could trigger backlash and stricter regulation slowing adoption beyond those directly involved.
- Countervailing opportunities and the systems frame: a leap in model architecture or an adjacent field like quantum computing could loosen several constraints at once — as the transistor simultaneously advanced computing, communications, and electronics — and AI breakthroughs in energy or biotechnology could unlock growth beyond AI itself; Section 2 divides the system into Foundations (what AI can do/conditions), Transmission (how people and organizations put capabilities to work), and Outcomes (effects), with feedback loops (better models → adoption → investment → infrastructure/R&D → better models; shortages/weak governance/concern slowing deployment).

## Community backlash and permitting freezes
**Covers:** Report p. 8, "In some places, local communities are pushing back" paragraph

In some places, local communities are pushing back. Concerns about rising electricity prices and large, unsightly facilities have triggered backlash, leading to permitting delays and, in some cases, construction freezes. ## Other physical constraints: minerals and workers
**Covers:** Report p. 8, "Other potential constraints are emerging" paragraph

Supplies of copper and other critical minerals needed for chips and wiring are showing signs of stress, but new mines and processing plants take years to permit and build. Unless current plans are accelerated, the world could face a sizable copper shortfall by the mid-2030s, according to the International Energy Agency. AI will be competing with many other industries for those materials. And physical resources are not the only constraint: Data center developers also report shortages of engineers and other workers, who take time to train.

## Capability pace vs. reorganization lag
**Covers:** Report p. 8, "AI's capabilities, meanwhile" and "So, the tools are here" paragraphs

The complexity of tasks that AI can reliably handle — measured by how long the same tasks take a human expert — has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019, and roughly every four months since 2023. This is partly because AI increasingly contributes to the research that improves it; existing models can now write the code, generate the data, and run the evaluations used to train the next generation of models. Technologies available today could, in theory, automate more than half of current work hours in the United States, Europe, and Latin America.

So, the tools are here. But the reorganization is not. In a McKinsey survey of 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, conducted in May and June 2026, nearly nine in ten said their organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function. Yet outside a small group of AI high performers, representing 6 percent of the sample, only about one-quarter reported redesigning workflows around AI rather than inserting AI into existing ones.

## Risks: governance, fragmentation, and cyberattack
**Covers:** Report pp. 8–9, "Risks" through "slows adoption well beyond the companies directly involved"

Governance is also lagging. A separate McKinsey survey of about 500 organizations on AI trust and governance, conducted in December 2025 and January 2026, found that only about 30 percent had reached level 3 or higher on a four-level maturity scale. Strategy remains underdeveloped as well. Only 22 percent of operations leaders report a fully developed and implemented enterprise AI strategy, even though more than half name strategy as the single biggest driver of AI returns.

The problem usually stems from fragmentation. Responsibility for AI is split across IT, legal, data, HR, risk, and strategy, with no single owner. Day-to-day, oversight often defaults to the teams closest to the technology — IT or engineering — simply because no one else has claimed it. At the same time, 70 percent of global CEOs say they have the greatest influence on their company's AI strategy. The gap between who actually runs AI and who is nominally accountable for it creates its own risk. Often, no one is positioned to answer basic questions about autonomous AI systems — which actions they may take, which decisions need human approval, who is accountable when something goes wrong.

Similarly, roughly one in three organizations still have no formal process to check AI systems for security risks before deploying them. Weak links may include smaller players, such as local governments, which may lack the resources for advanced security but remain connected to larger systems, creating vulnerabilities that extend beyond their own networks.

As adoption increases, so does the risk of cyberattack. More AI systems in more places give criminals more surface area to exploit. The technology is also becoming a more capable tool for hackers. In April 2026, the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) found that Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview could autonomously execute multi-step attacks on test networks that would take human professionals days of work. Just two years earlier, the best available models in AISI's evaluations could barely complete beginner-level cyber tasks. The risks extend beyond businesses and governments to individuals. AI-generated phishing and fraud are harder to spot because AI can produce personalized, convincing messages at a scale no human scammer could match.

Just as with a large governance failure — that causes or allows major financial or physical harm — a single large AI-enabled cyberattack could trigger public backlash and stricter regulation that slows adoption well beyond the companies directly involved.

## Opportunities: breakthroughs that shift timelines
**Covers:** Report p. 9, "Opportunities" paragraph

Major breakthroughs could also change several of these timelines at once. A leap in model architecture — or in an adjacent field like quantum computing — could loosen several of AI's constraints at the same time, the way the invention of the transistor simultaneously advanced computing, communications, and electronics. The benefits could flow the other way, too. AI-driven breakthroughs in fields like energy or biotechnology could unlock innovation and growth well beyond the AI industry itself.

## AI as a system: layers and feedback loops
**Covers:** Report p. 9, "2. AI as a system: How the pieces fit together"

Many of today's hardest challenges and most important developments must be understood as complex systems. This is the case for AI. Its effects come from the interaction of many interdependent parts, not from any one part alone. Capability, R&D, physical resources, institutions, and adoption each shape the others (see sidebar, "Why a systems view matters").

Returning to the framework introduced in Exhibit 1, the system is divided into three analytical layers (Exhibit 4): Foundations shape what AI can do and the conditions under which it develops; Transmission is how people and organizations put AI's capabilities to work; and Outcomes are the effects that follow. The framework organizes the factors thought to matter most; it is not a full inventory, and the categories overlap. Innovation, for example, is intertwined with new demand.

Feedback runs through every part: "Better models can encourage adoption; adoption attracts investment; investment expands infrastructure and R&D; which in turn speed up development of yet better models. Conversely, shortages of power, weak governance, or public concern can slow deployment. The system is in motion, with feedback running through every part of it."

## Sidebar: Why a systems view matters
**Covers:** Report p. 9, sidebar with notes 1–3

Systems thinking is probably as old as philosophy itself, but it was applied directly to business and technology in the 20th century by people studying how organizations actually behave. Biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy spent decades arguing that breaking a phenomenon into isolated parts misses essential properties that surface only when the parts are observed together, consolidated in his 1968 book General System Theory. The engineer Jay Forrester, in the 1950s, studied why factories lurched between hiring booms and mass layoffs even when customer demand barely moved (Industrial Dynamics, MIT Press, 1961): orders took time to travel from retailer to distributor to factory, so by the time a factory ramped up hiring the signal was stale and it overshot, then cut hard to correct, repeating the cycle — swings caused by the system itself (information delay), not the macroeconomy. Management theorist Russell Ackoff illustrated the idea with a car story ("Towards a system of systems concepts," Management Science, July 1971, Vol. 17, No. 11; 1994 Deming Institute presentation): take the world's best engine, transmission, brakes, and so on from every car in production and put those "best" parts together — the result is not the best car, it may not run at all, because the parts were never designed to work together. The lesson applied to AI: "Optimizing one piece in isolation — faster chips, more capital, a new rule — will not improve outcomes if the real constraint lies elsewhere."
