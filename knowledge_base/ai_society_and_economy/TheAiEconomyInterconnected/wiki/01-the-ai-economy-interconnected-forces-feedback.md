# The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback loops and speeds of change

**In one sentence:** Answering AI's big questions requires seeing the whole interconnected system, because AI capabilities, physical infrastructure, and organizational adaptation move at vastly different speeds and fall out of sync, creating bottlenecks and risks that leaders must manage at multiple speeds.

## Key points
- Less than four years after ChatGPT's launch, generative AI is already changing how people think and work, yet where it goes next is deeply uncertain.
- The report maps connections between AI and physical resources, institutions, businesses, markets, geopolitics, and people, all of which shape AI and change in response to it.
- Risks and bottlenecks arise because AI capabilities advance exponentially by some measures while data centers, power grids, chip factories, and mines progress more linearly, and business redesign spans years.
- As one constraint eases, others can be exposed: progress may slow when infrastructure or organizations cannot keep up, while risks grow when adoption outruns safeguards, governance, or public acceptance.
- The management prescription is to manage at multiple speeds — sometimes accelerating what lags, other times slowing down — guided by a systems view.
- Whether jobs disappear depends on whether AI can do job tasks as well and as cheaply as people and whether new tasks emerge fast enough to replace taken-over ones.
- Investment pays off only if demand for AI services grows fast enough to fill the computing capacity being built (chips, power, data centers), echoing railway and dot-com overcapacity risks.
- Realizing a general-purpose technology's full potential historically takes decades, requiring complementary innovation, infrastructure, rules, capital, and willing people and organizations.

## Report identity and framing

McKinsey Global Institute report, September 2026, titled "The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback loops and speeds of change." Its premise:

> "AI is shaped by forces far beyond the technology itself, and its effects reach just as widely. Mapping how the pieces interact helps us see what could influence its course and where hurdles and opportunities may emerge."

MGI context stated in chunk: established 1990; mission to provide a fact base for economic/business decision-making; five research themes (productivity and prosperity; resources of the world; human potential; global connections; technologies and markets of the future); independent, fact-based, funded by McKinsey partners, not commissioned.

## At a glance

Verbatim core claims:

> "Answering big questions about AI means seeing the whole picture, not just focusing on the technology. To help do that, we mapped the connections between AI and physical resources, institutions, businesses, markets, geopolitics, and people—all of which shape AI and change in response to it."

> "Risks and bottlenecks arise because different parts of the AI system are moving at vastly different paces. AI capabilities are advancing exponentially by some measures. Progress in building data centers, power grids, chip factories, and mines is more linear. Redesigning how a business and its people work spans years. As one constraint eases, others can be exposed."

> "In the AI era, leaders must therefore learn to manage at multiple speeds. Progress may slow when infrastructure or organizations cannot keep up, while risks can grow when adoption outruns safeguards, governance, or public acceptance. Sometimes the answer is to accelerate what is lagging; other times it may be to slow down. A systems view can help us navigate these choices."

Opening uncertainty framing: "Will it improve our lives or take our jobs? Lead to unprecedented growth or upheaval? Who will benefit, and who might lose?"

## Introduction — four unanswerable-in-isolation questions

Four stakeholder questions the chunk poses:

| Stakeholder | Question in chunk |
|---|---|
| Workers | Whether predictions of mass job losses are credible or a scare story; one widely reported, admittedly extreme forecast says AI could eliminate as many as half of entry-level white-collar jobs within five years |
| Investors | Whether enormous infrastructure sums will pay off — or leave excess capacity and losses as after railway and dot-com booms |
| Policymakers | How tightly to regulate risks without disadvantaging their economies; China, the EU, and the US follow different paths |
| CEOs/CTOs | "We have rolled out the technology, so why aren't we seeing it in our results?" |

Cross-domain answers given:

- Jobs: depends on whether AI does tasks as well/cheaply as people, and whether new tasks emerge fast enough.
- Investment: pays off only if AI-services demand grows fast enough to fill built computing capacity (chips, power, data centers).
- Regulation trade-off: "Move too slowly and risks may go unmanaged; move too quickly and lighter-touch rivals may pull ahead."
- Company ROI: depends on technology, economics, and whether the organization adapts to capture full value.

Framework claim: drawing on prior research and client work, the paper proposes a general framework connecting separately-considered factors; paper begins with out-of-sync components and bottlenecks/risks, then assembles them into a single system. Chapter 1 covers speeds of change and falling out of sync, oriented by Exhibit 1 (online/digital PDF clickable; print pp. 19–24).
