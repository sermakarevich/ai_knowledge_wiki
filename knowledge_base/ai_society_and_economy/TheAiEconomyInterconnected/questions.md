# Retrieval Practice: The AI economy: Interconnected

### Q1. What speed mismatch does the report place at the center of AI risks and bottlenecks, and what management prescription follows from it?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > AI capabilities advance exponentially by some measures while data-center, power-grid, chip-factory, and mine buildouts progress more linearly and business redesign spans years, so easing one constraint exposes the next. Progress stalls when infrastructure or organizations cannot keep up, while risks grow when adoption outruns safeguards, governance, or public acceptance, so leaders must manage at multiple speeds. See [[wiki/01-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|The AI economy: Interconnected forces]].

### Q2. How did electrification and the internet illustrate the delayed payoff of general-purpose technologies, and how does AI repeat the pattern?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > Electrification first needed enough power generation, then grid expansion, with big productivity gains only after factories were redesigned around the electric motor; the internet likewise needed fiber buildout, new business models, and company reorganization. AI repeats this with model capabilities doubling every few months while physical infrastructure builds linearly over years and company change is uneven and slower. See [[wiki/02-in-the-early-decades-of-electrification|General-purpose technologies outpace systems]].

### Q3. What were the 2023 chip-packaging crunch and the electricity wall that followed it?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > By 2023, a year after ChatGPT launched, demand for AI chips outgrew packaging capacity: only TSMC could fuse Nvidia's H100 with memory at scale, leaving buyers facing up to 11 months of lead time for a finished server. As chip bottlenecks eased, electricity and grid connections became the binding constraint, with Microsoft holding undeployable chips for lack of powered data-center space and the median new US power plant taking about five years from interconnection request to operation. See [[wiki/02-in-the-early-decades-of-electrification|General-purpose technologies outpace systems]].

### Q4. What facts describe the data-center construction bottleneck and the community backlash against it?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > The US holds nearly half of global installed data-center capacity, yet construction expands rapidly without keeping pace: per-query costs fall with efficiency while total demand rises faster, and many facilities are pre-leased before opening. In Q1 2026 alone, community opposition over electricity prices and unsightly facilities blocked or delayed at least 75 US projects worth some $130 billion, matching all of 2025, followed by New York's first-ever statewide permitting freeze on large data centers in July. See [[wiki/03-data-center-construction-itself-is-now|Data center construction bottleneck]].

### Q5. How do the report's Foundations, Transmission, and Outcomes layers fit together, including the brains/brawn/rules/nutrients framing?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > Foundations (capabilities, R&D, physical resources, institutional stance) determine what AI can do and under what conditions; Transmission is whether and how people and organizations put capabilities to work through adoption and adaptation; Outcomes are the productivity, innovation, demand, jobs, capital, geopolitical, and well-being effects that then feed back into foundations. Capabilities are the brains, physical resources the brawn, institutions the rules, and R&D the nutrients that improve models and stretch physical resources further. See [[wiki/04-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|Interconnected forces and feedback]].

### Q6. What evidence shows that AI adoption is broad but shallow, and that governance and strategy lag behind it?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > In a May–June 2026 McKinsey survey of 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, nearly nine in ten organizations regularly used AI in at least one function, yet outside the 6 percent of high performers only about one-quarter redesigned workflows rather than inserting AI into existing ones. Only about 30 percent of roughly 500 organizations reached level 3 or higher on a four-level governance maturity scale, only 22 percent of operations leaders report a fully implemented enterprise AI strategy, and roughly one in three lack a formal pre-deployment security check. See [[wiki/03-data-center-construction-itself-is-now|Data center construction bottleneck]].

### Q7. How does AI shift commerce and create new demand, and what historical analogies does the report use?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > AI shifts commerce from the internet era's focus on attention toward intention by matching suppliers to hidden or latent buyer needs, paralleling steam moving from mine pumps to shipping and railroads and electricity moving from gas-light replacement to refrigeration and redesigned factories. New demand comes from building the AI economy itself, delegating personal tasks, Jevons-paradox cheaper access raising total consumption, and higher incomes plus Ford-style shorter hours funding more spending and leisure. See [[wiki/05-another-important-arena-of-innovation-is|Innovation arenas and new demand]].

### Q8. Why does lasting advantage come from looking beyond efficiency, and what replaces efficiency as the moat?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > Efficiency-only gains erode because rivals with the same technology achieve similar gains and compete prices down, and economy-wide productivity growth has historically come from a few standout firms entering new businesses rather than making existing ones more efficient. Durable advantage pairs AI with hard-to-replicate moats such as proprietary data, scale, customer relationships, and scarce physical assets, while incumbents exploit scale to test and learn faster than capabilities evolve. See [[wiki/06-look-beyond-efficiency-for-lasting|Look beyond efficiency]].

### Q9. What headline figures anchor the Foundations exhibits for capabilities, R&D, and physical resources?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > The leading model succeeds half the time at tasks taking humans more than 16 hours, with the top graduate-level science score reaching 95 percent in 2026 versus 39 percent about three years earlier; global private AI investment hit $290 billion in 2025, almost five times the 2019 level; and global data-center capacity grew 15 gigawatts in 2025 against roughly 18 gigawatts of demand growth. Efficiency cuts resources per task but cheaper AI can raise total demand, and physical capacity expands on slow linear construction timelines. See [[wiki/07-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|Foundations exhibits]].

### Q10. What do the 47%, 89% vs 46%, and 2.5% figures say about trust, adoption depth, and productivity so far?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > Only 47 percent of respondents trust companies using AI to protect personal data, showing institutions changing far more slowly than capabilities improve. In 2026, 89 percent of organizations used AI in at least one function but only 46 percent of those had moved beyond pilots, while over 80 percent of US physicians used AI professionally, up from 38 percent in 2023. Average US nonfarm labor productivity grew 2.5 percent annually from late 2022 to mid-2026 versus a 2.1 percent long-run average, too soon to call structural. See [[wiki/08-47-89-vs-46-2-5-less|Trust, adoption, and productivity exhibits]].

### Q11. What do the time-saved, $756B investment, and one-third build-vs-buy figures describe?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > Nearly one-third of US workers who used AI at work saved three or more hours in the prior week, with another 31 percent saving one to two hours, and how that freed time is used shapes growth and well-being. Seven leading AI companies spent a combined $756 billion on capital investment plus R&D in 2025, twice the 2022 level, funded across public markets, private credit, governments, and venture capital toward chips, data centers, and power. About one-third of organizations used agentic coding tools to build rather than buy software, consistent with models alone conferring no lasting edge versus data, relationships, and integration. See [[wiki/09-30-756b-1-3-nearly-one-third-of|Time saved, investment scale, competing beyond models]].

### Q12. How does the report characterize geopolitics, changing personal use, risks, and feedback loops?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > The US produced 55 percent of notable models since 2022 versus 23 percent for China, with leverage exerted through US chip-export controls and Chinese critical-mineral controls and no single measure of leadership. Personal queries rose to 73 percent of consumer ChatGPT use by June 2025, and AI affect thinking, learning, and relating with both creative gains and overload or burnout risks. Risks are potential harms distinct from bottlenecks, while economic gains can fund more R&D and infrastructure and people's experiences can shift business behavior and regulation in either direction. See [[wiki/10-geopolitics-people-society-and-risks-ai|Geopolitics, people, and risks]].

### Q15. Given tight capital, grid queues, and low workflow redesign, where should a midsize firm place its next AI bet to earn a durable return?

> [!tip]- Answer
> > It should concentrate scarce funds on one bottleneck-adjacent workflow where it holds proprietary data or customer access, pairing the deployment with a full workflow redesign and a visible customer or employee benefit rather than spreading pilots thinly. That focused redesign plus moat-backed new offering is more likely to convert AI use into productivity-backed growth and public acceptance than efficiency-only automation or model-chasing. See [[wiki/06-look-beyond-efficiency-for-lasting|Look beyond efficiency]].
