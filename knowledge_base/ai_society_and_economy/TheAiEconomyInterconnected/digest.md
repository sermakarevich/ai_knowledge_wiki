# The AI economy: Interconnected — Digest

## 1. [[wiki/01-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback loops and speeds of change]]

**In one sentence:** Answering AI's big questions requires seeing the whole interconnected system, because AI capabilities, physical infrastructure, and organizational adaptation move at vastly different speeds and fall out of sync, creating bottlenecks and risks that leaders must manage at multiple speeds.

**Key points:**

- Less than four years after ChatGPT's launch, generative AI is already changing how people think and work, yet where it goes next is deeply uncertain.
- The report maps connections between AI and physical resources, institutions, businesses, markets, geopolitics, and people, all of which shape AI and change in response to it.
- Risks and bottlenecks arise because AI capabilities advance exponentially by some measures while data centers, power grids, chip factories, and mines progress more linearly, and business redesign spans years.
- As one constraint eases, others can be exposed: progress may slow when infrastructure or organizations cannot keep up, while risks grow when adoption outruns safeguards, governance, or public acceptance.
- The management prescription is to manage at multiple speeds — sometimes accelerating what lags, other times slowing down — guided by a systems view.
- Whether jobs disappear depends on whether AI can do job tasks as well and as cheaply as people and whether new tasks emerge fast enough to replace taken-over ones.
- Investment pays off only if demand for AI services grows fast enough to fill the computing capacity being built (chips, power, data centers), echoing railway and dot-com overcapacity risks.
- Realizing a general-purpose technology's full potential historically takes decades, requiring complementary innovation, infrastructure, rules, capital, and willing people and organizations.

## 2. [[wiki/02-in-the-early-decades-of-electrification|General-purpose technologies outpace the systems needed to realize their potential]]

**In one sentence:** Like electrification and the internet before it, AI's big productivity gains are delayed because model capabilities improve exponentially while physical infrastructure builds linearly and companies reorganize unevenly, so out-of-step parts create bottlenecks such as the 2023 chip-packaging crunch and the electricity/grid-connection wall.

**Key points:**

- Electrification's big productivity gains arrived only after factories were physically redesigned around the electric motor, following an earlier sequence of first generating enough power, then expanding the grid.
- The internet followed the same staged pattern: first fiber-optic networks were built, then new business models were needed, and finally companies reorganized around the technology.
- AI model capabilities are improving exponentially, doubling every few months by some recent measures, while physical infrastructure builds out more linearly over years and company-level change is uneven and slower.
- When AI's parts move out of step, bottlenecks slow advancement or deployment (e.g., 2023 demand for AI chips outpacing manufacturing) and mismatches create risks when governance or cybersecurity lags powerful new tools.
- By 2023, a year after ChatGPT's launch, demand for AI chips outgrew packaging capacity: buyers faced lead times of up to 11 months for a finished H100-based server because only TSMC could fuse Nvidia's H100 with memory at the required scale.
- Early generative-AI hurdles shifted from frontier chips and computing power to electricity supply and grid connections, with the next constraints expected in applications, workforce skills, and organizational workflows rather than hardware.
- A single data center can consume as much power as a small city, and when local grids cannot absorb that demand, new power lines and substations — with their own permits, land, and queues — must be built before the facility turns on.
- Microsoft's Azure was short on operating capacity for several quarters due to electricity: on an October 2025 earnings call its CFO said the company had AI chips it could not deploy for lack of powered, ready-to-occupy data center space; the median new US power plant now takes about five years from interconnection request to commercial operation, up from less than two years in 2008.

## 3. [[wiki/03-data-center-construction-itself-is-now|Data center construction itself is now]]

**In one sentence:** Data-center construction and physical inputs cannot keep pace with fast-rising AI demand while capabilities advance far faster than organizational reorganization, governance, and security can absorb, so AI must be managed as an interdependent system rather than isolated parts.

**Key points:**

- Data-center construction is a bottleneck, especially in the United States, which accounts for nearly half of global installed data-center capacity; per-query compute, power, and water costs have fallen with efficiency, but total demand is rising faster than efficiency gains or new supply can offset, facilities are expanding rapidly yet cannot keep pace, and many are pre-leased before opening.
- Community opposition blocked or delayed at least 75 US data-center projects worth some $130 billion in Q1 2026 alone — matching all of 2025 in three months, per Data Center Watch — over electricity prices and large unsightly facilities, causing permitting delays and freezes, including New York's July signing of the first-ever statewide permitting freeze on large data centers.
- Copper and other critical minerals for chips and wiring show stress, new mines and processing plants take years to permit and build, the world could face a sizable copper shortfall by the mid-2030s unless plans accelerate (IEA), AI competes with many industries for those materials, and developers also report shortages of engineers and other workers who take time to train.
- Reliably automatable task complexity — measured by how long the same tasks take a human expert — has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 and roughly every four months since 2023, partly because AI writes code, generates data, and runs evaluations used to train the next generation; technologies available today could in theory automate more than half of current work hours in the US, Europe, and Latin America.
- Tools exist but reorganization does not: in a McKinsey survey of 1,719 respondents across 97 countries (May–June 2026), nearly nine in ten said their organizations regularly use AI in at least one function, yet outside the 6 percent who are AI high performers, only about one-quarter reported redesigning workflows around AI rather than inserting it into existing ones.
- Governance, strategy, and security lag: only about 30 percent of ~500 organizations reached level 3 or higher on a four-level maturity scale (Dec 2025–Jan 2026 survey), only 22 percent of operations leaders report a fully developed and implemented enterprise AI strategy though more than half name strategy as the biggest driver of returns, responsibility is fragmented across IT/legal/data/HR/risk/strategy with oversight defaulting to IT or engineering while 70 percent of CEOs claim greatest influence, and roughly one in three organizations still have no formal pre-deployment AI security-risk check.
- Cyber risk grows with adoption: more AI systems mean more attack surface, AI is a more capable hacker tool (in April 2026 the UK AISI found Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview could autonomously execute multi-step attacks on test networks taking human professionals days, versus best models barely completing beginner-level cyber tasks two years earlier), AI-generated phishing/fraud is harder to spot at unmatched scale, and a single large AI-enabled cyberattack or governance failure causing major financial or physical harm could trigger backlash and stricter regulation slowing adoption beyond those directly involved.
- Countervailing opportunities and the systems frame: a leap in model architecture or an adjacent field like quantum computing could loosen several constraints at once — as the transistor simultaneously advanced computing, communications, and electronics — and AI breakthroughs in energy or biotechnology could unlock growth beyond AI itself; Section 2 divides the system into Foundations (what AI can do/conditions), Transmission (how people and organizations put capabilities to work), and Outcomes (effects), with feedback loops (better models → adoption → investment → infrastructure/R&D → better models; shortages/weak governance/concern slowing deployment).

## 4. [[wiki/04-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback]]

**In one sentence:** AI works as an interconnected system in which foundations (capabilities, R&D, physical resources, institutional stance) drive adoption and adaptation, whose economic and social outcomes then feed back into further AI development.

**Key points:**

- Exhibit 4 frames AI as an interconnected system where a change in one part ripples through the rest, linking foundations, adoption/adaptation, and outcomes with feedback loops.
- AI foundations — capabilities, R&D, physical resources, and institutional stance — jointly determine what AI can do and how fast it can be built, with each shaping the others.
- Frontier AI R&D is mainly concentrated in a small group of companies in the United States and China, and progress is tied to investment, computing power, data, electricity, talent, and government policy.
- Physical resources for AI data centers (chips, copper wires, cooling, land, electricity, water) are reshaping trade and supply chains, and geographically concentrated resources such as rare earths can become geopolitical leverage.
- Adoption runs at different speeds: individuals experiment almost immediately while businesses move slowly and unevenly, with large firms aided by capital, small firms by agility, and midsize firms potentially struggling.
- Scaling AI successes attract further infrastructure and R&D investment, while failures expose governance, security, and skills weaknesses that shape the next round of regulation and organizational change.
- Economy-wide productivity gains require workflow redesign plus diffusion plus growth, because without growth, labor-productivity gains could cut employment and consumer spending — which accounts for more than half of global GDP — reducing returns on capital and inviting backlash.
- AI is already accelerating discovery, compressing parts of biomedical research from years into hours via protein-structure prediction, identifying new antibiotic candidates from vast molecular libraries, and speeding materials science.

## 5. [[wiki/05-another-important-arena-of-innovation-is|Innovation arenas: commerce, new demand, and beyond original uses]]

**In one sentence:** AI extends commerce from attention to intention, creates new demand through cheaper goods, higher incomes, and markets for delegated tasks, and — like steam and electricity before it — enables products and business models difficult to anticipate, while reshaping jobs, time use, capital flows, firm boundaries, geopolitics, and well-being.

**Key points:**

- AI shifts commerce from the internet era's focus on attention toward intention by helping suppliers understand buyer intent and match products to hidden or latent needs.
- Like steam power (mine pumps → shipping → railroads) and electricity (gas-light replacement → refrigeration, reorganized factories), AI is expected to enable entirely new products and business models.
- New demand arises from building the AI economy itself (models, robots, infrastructure, cybersecurity, change support), from delegating personal tasks (letters, trips, homework, finances, cleaning, cooking, yard work), from Jevons-paradox cheaper access, and from higher incomes plus Ford-style shorter hours and more leisure.
- About one-third of current US employment is in occupations enabled by earlier general-purpose technologies (truck drivers, mechanics, electricians, developers, cybersecurity analysts); whether AI creates more jobs than it destroys depends on new work plus fast-enough demand growth.
- AI reorganizes work around human-machine collaboration, affecting knowledge work (computing, accounting, law), rewarding judgment, curiosity, and conflict resolution, narrowing some junior–senior gaps while making the first career rung harder and potentially favoring higher-wage occupations.
- Capital has shifted from chips and data centers to electricity and grid, funded beyond venture capital by public equity, private credit, and public debt, with circular AI-company financing that obscures inside-vs-outside demand and spreads losses.
- McKinsey estimates $7 trillion cumulative global data-center investment 2025–2030 (over 40% in the US); 2026 US AI-related goods imports are on track for ~$750 billion — about as much as cars, oil and gas, and pharmaceuticals combined — while total private investment/GDP stays flat and overbuilding risk echoes railways and the internet.
- Public attitudes split geographically: ~70% of US respondents say AI will make personal information less secure, roughly half of Americans under 30 expect a negative societal effect, while developing/middle-income countries and an 80,000-user, 159-country Claude study (optimism concentrated in Africa, South America, much of Asia) are more positive.

## 6. [[wiki/06-look-beyond-efficiency-for-lasting|Look Beyond Efficiency for Lasting Advantage]]

**In one sentence:** Lasting advantage from AI comes not from cutting costs alone — which rivals can copy and compete away — but from combining AI with hard-to-replicate moats, building new businesses, learning faster than capabilities evolve, and aligning business, investor, policy, and individual choices with the whole system's constraints.

**Key points:**

- Efficiency-only AI gains erode: competitors with the same technology achieve similar gains, driving down prices and erasing the initial advantage.
- Durable outperformance requires pairing AI with non-replicable moats — proprietary data, scale, strong customer relationships, scarce physical assets — in a model delivering greater value to customers and suppliers.
- First uses are rarely the most consequential: as with electricity replacing gas lighting before transforming industries, leaders should use freed-up resources to build new products, businesses, and markets.
- Economy-wide productivity growth has been driven by a small number of standout companies shifting strategy and entering new businesses, not by making existing businesses more efficient.
- Incumbents can out-learn startups if they exploit scale (more customers, workers, operations to test and gather feedback) by building capacity to adapt systems and workflows as fast as AI capabilities improve.
- Value creation fails under high societal resistance: people support change when they experience better products, services, or ways of working, and resist when jobs, livelihoods, and privacy feel at risk.
- Investor and policy outcomes hinge on system speeds: model capabilities can grow exponentially while physical infrastructure expands slowly and firms take years to convert AI into productivity and profits.

## 7. [[wiki/07-the-ai-economy-interconnected-forces-feedback|The AI economy: Interconnected forces, feedback loops and speeds of change — Foundations]]

**In one sentence:** AI capabilities are advancing exponentially while the R&D that feeds them is broad-based, physical capacity expands only linearly and lags demand, and institutions, adoption, and productivity dynamics mediate how fast any of this turns into economic outcomes.

**Key points:**

- The leading model succeeds half the time at tasks that take humans more than 16 hours, and the leading graduate-level science benchmark score (GPQA Diamond) reached 95% in 2026, up from 39% roughly three years earlier — with task complexity AI can reliably complete doubling since 2019 (exponential trajectory).
- Embodied AI (e.g., robots) is gaining dexterity and ability to navigate and manipulate objects, but performance becomes less reliable moving from robot-designed settings into varied real-world environments; LLMs are improving at harder problems and longer tasks.
- Global private AI investment was $290B in 2025 — almost 5 times the 2019 level — and AI-advancing R&D extends well beyond models into computing, energy, cooling, materials, and other deployment-enabling technologies, advancing incrementally with occasional large-jump breakthroughs.
- Physical capacity lags capabilities: global data center capacity grew by 15 gigawatts in 2025 against roughly 18 gigawatts of demand growth, and the required resource list (chips, electricity, water, land, cooling systems, minerals) expands on slow construction/manufacturing timelines (linear trajectory).
- Greater efficiency cuts resources per AI task but cheaper AI can encourage more use and raise total demand.
- Institutions are defined as the rules, norms, and organizations guiding how AI is developed and used; adoption is the use of AI while adaptation is the changes people and organizations make to integrate it into how they work.
- AI's productivity gains may take time to spread from individual tasks to companies and economies (section header; body cut off at chunk end).

## 8. [[wiki/08-47-89-vs-46-2-5-less|47% trust, 89% vs 46% adoption, 2.5% productivity — and downstream economic exhibits]]

**In one sentence:** Institutions and public trust lag fast individual AI adoption (47% trust; 89% of organizations use AI but only 46% move beyond pilots), aggregate productivity gains remain modest so far (2.5% annual growth since late 2022), while task-level exhibits point to large future effects on innovation, demand, jobs, and time use.

**Key points:**

- Fewer than half of survey respondents (47%) trust companies that use AI to protect their personal data (Ipsos AI Monitor 2026).
- In 2026, 89% of organizations used AI in at least one business function, but of these only 46% had progressed beyond pilots (McKinsey "state of AI in 2026").
- Individual professional adoption is faster than organizational scaling: in early 2026 more than 80% of US physicians (81% of 1,692 surveyed, Jan 15–Feb 2, 2026) reported using AI professionally, more than twice the 38% share in 2023.
- Average annual US nonfarm labor productivity growth between late 2022 (ChatGPT launch) and the first half of 2026 was 2.5%, slightly above the 2.1% long-run average since 1947, but too soon to tell whether structural or normal volatility.
- AI has shortened drug-discovery timelines by as much as 80%, and pharmaceutical companies using AI could bring 1.5 times as many new drugs to market for the same cost.
- The report cites ~$30T in 2024 e-commerce sales as an example of the enormous new-demand market an earlier general-purpose technology (the internet) enabled.
- By 2035, 72% of US workers could see AI reinvent how they spend more than 15% of their working time; jobs are more likely to change than be replaced outright in the near future.
- Mechanism repeated across exhibits: institutions change more slowly and unevenly than physical infrastructure can be built and far more slowly than AI capabilities improve, while broad productivity gains require workflow redesign spreading across firms and industries.

## 9. [[wiki/09-30-756b-1-3-nearly-one-third-of|Time saved, $756B investment scale, and competing beyond models]]

**In one sentence:** AI is saving workers measurable hours, drawing massive investment into chips-to-power capacity whose payoff depends on returns, and pushing competitive advantage from models themselves toward data, relationships, and integration as about one-third of firms build rather than buy software.

**Key points:**

- Nearly one-third of US workers who used AI at work in the previous week said it saved them three or more hours; an additional 31% reported saving one to two hours.
- How freed-up time is used — more paid work, learning, entrepreneurship, leisure, or other activities — will help determine AI's effects on economic growth and well-being.
- Combined capital investment and R&D spending by seven leading AI-related companies was $756B in 2025, twice as much as in 2022.
- Funding comes from public markets, private credit, governments, and venture capital, flowing toward parts of the AI system needing capacity: chips, data centers, and electricity generation.
- The McKinsey Global Institute-classified AI foundation added about $500 billion in revenue from 2022 to 2025, and delivered a return on invested capital about 1.5 times that of other industries between 2022 and 2024.
- About one-third of survey respondents report their organization used agentic coding tools to build its own software instead of purchasing it.
- Best AI models alone may not confer a lasting edge because the technology is widely available and leaders may not stay ahead; firms may compete on proprietary data, customer relationships, and integration effectiveness.
- AI may reshape competition by changing company-customer-supplier relationships, reducing some scale advantages, and requiring governance structures to manage risks as use expands.

## 10. [[wiki/10-geopolitics-people-society-and-risks-ai|Geopolitics, people and society, and risks AI]]

**In one sentence:** AI is becoming a source of geopolitical power led by the US in notable models (55% vs 23% for China) amid chip and mineral controls, reshaping how people think and relate (personal queries 73% of consumer ChatGPT use in June 2025), creating system-wide risks distinct from bottlenecks, with feedback loops that can accelerate or slow change.

**Key points:**

- Over half of notable AI models released since 2022 were developed in the United States (55%), compared with 23% in China (Epoch AI; McKinsey Global Institute analysis).
- The US and China compete with different strengths — the US may lead in frontier models while China may be ahead in manufacturing deployment — and use policy leverage: US restrictions on advanced-chip exports vs Chinese controls on critical-mineral exports.
- In June 2025, personal queries made up nearly three-quarters (73%) of consumer ChatGPT interactions, compared with about half in June 2024 (Aaron Chatterji et al., NBER working paper 34255, September 2025).
- AI could change how people think, learn, work, and relate, spurring creativity and enjoyment but also contributing to cognitive overload and burnout via work requiring more oversight, judgment, and context-switching.
- People's experiences with AI shape their views of the technology, influencing how businesses use it and governments regulate it.
- In the report's framework, risks are potential harms, distinct from bottlenecks that slow AI development and adoption; they arise from resources AI requires, models themselves, how people and organizations use them, and broader changes from community pressure to errors, cyberattacks, and fraud.
- The system does not move in one direction: economic gains can fund more R&D and infrastructure (accelerating progress), while people's AI experiences can change business behavior and government policy (speeding or slowing adoption).

## The argument in five moves

1. AI cannot be judged on models alone: capabilities, physical infrastructure, institutions, and organizational adaptation move at different speeds, so bottlenecks and risks emerge wherever the parts fall out of sync.
2. History shows the delay is normal: like electrification and the internet, AI needs complementary grids, factories, business models, and workflows built over years and decades before the big productivity gains arrive.
3. The current lags are physical and organizational at once: chips, power, grid connections, minerals, and construction capacity constrain supply while firms adopt AI widely but rarely redesign workflows, and governance, strategy, and security trail behind, raising cyber and backlash risks.
4. Foundations, transmission, and outcomes form feedback loops: R&D, capabilities, resources, and rules determine what AI can do; uneven adoption puts it to work; productivity, innovation, new demand, jobs, time use, capital shifts, firm boundaries, geopolitics, and well-being feed back into further investment, regulation, and development.
5. The payoff depends on growth and acceptance: economy-wide gains require workflow redesign plus diffusion plus new demand, funded capacity must be filled, and societies must experience visible benefits rather than job, privacy, and safety harms.
6. Advantage therefore goes beyond efficiency: durable winners pair AI with hard-to-copy moats, build new products and markets, learn faster than capabilities evolve, follow bottlenecks without over-concentrating correlated bets, and track the whole system instead of predicting a single AI future.
