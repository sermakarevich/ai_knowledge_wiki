# Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI -- In Plain Language

## What is this about?
Imagine economists built a "what-if machine" for the years 2026 to 2030.

You tell it how fast AI learns office-type work -- writing emails, making software, running an online shop -- and it tells you what happens to paychecks, jobs, and the whole economy.

It does not predict the future. It tells three consistent stories so we can compare guesses side by side.

Think of it like a flight simulator for the economy: you move a few levers, then watch what happens to growth and jobs.

The main levers are how much office-type work AI can do,
how widely it is used,
and whether it replaces workers or helps them.

A small difference early can look tiny in 2027
and huge by 2030, because use builds on use.

## Why does it matter?
Right now, forecasts about AI and jobs are all over the place.

Some say "almost nothing happens." Others say "millions lose work." They use different math, so you cannot compare them.

That is confusing for workers, bosses, and policymakers. Everyone is arguing from a different map.

This paper puts different assumptions into one shared machine.
Same town, same rules, only the AI settings change.

Without a shared machine, one expert means "tasks AI could do"
and another means "tasks actually used at work",
so their numbers never line up.

If it works, we can ask better questions:
how fast to retrain people,
how to plan hiring,
and what safety net we might need.

It also shows timing matters. Almost nothing separates the stories until after 2027, then they split fast.

## How does it work?
Picture a small town with two kinds of workers:
bakers who work with knowledge and paperwork,
and electricians who work with their hands and tools.

Only the bakers face the robots directly.
Electricians feel it second-hand, when bakers apply for their jobs.

Now robots arrive that can learn some bakery tasks.
Some bakers get a helper robot.
Others find the robot does their task alone.
Those bakers must retrain as electricians.

Here is what the machine does, step by step:

1. Count how much bakery work robots can do. This is capability.
Mild means little, wild means almost half of office-type work.
2. Count how much is actually used in real shops. This is diffusion.
A smart robot in a box changes nothing until shops turn it on.
3. Measure how much faster or cheaper each robot task becomes.
This is the productivity gain.
Some tasks get a little faster, others more than twice as fast.
4. Split use into replacement versus help.
Replacement is automation. Help is augmentation.
Replacement hurts jobs more than help.
5. Count whether new bakery jobs appear. This is reinstatement.
If one new task appears for every two automated ones, pain is smaller.
If none appear, pain is larger.
6. Let pay and hiring adjust slowly.
Pay does not drop instantly,
and a baker cannot become an electrician overnight.
That delay creates unemployment.
7. Add up the town result:
total income, average pay,
share going to workers versus machine owners,
and how many are out of work.

Run the same town three times:

- Mild story: robots touch 4% of all work.
Total income is 1.6% above no-AI by 2030.
Unemployment stays near normal.
Think ten extra months of normal growth spread over four years.
- Busy story: robots touch 12% of all work, about 20% of office-type work.
Total income is 8.3% above no-AI, growing 5.4% per year.
That growth is faster than the 1990s dot-com boom.
Office employment falls about 4%,
office unemployment rises from 2.9% to 4.5%.
- Wild story: robots touch 30% of all work, about half of office-type work.
Total income is 32.4% above no-AI and grows 15.4% per year.
At that speed incomes double about every five years.
But office employment falls 21.5%
and almost one in five office-type workers is unemployed.

In August 2026 the authors asked 10,980 US adults what they expect. The middle answer lands near the busy story: income up about 8.6%, office employment down about 4.2%.

People expect fast learning but limited use at work, and about eight months to find a new occupation after displacement.

## Where can this be used?
- Career planning: if you do office-type work, think about skills that transfer to hands-on or people-facing jobs, and how long retraining takes.
- Company workforce planning: decide when to use AI to assist staff versus replace tasks, and how much retraining budget to set aside.
- Policy: plan unemployment insurance, retraining programs, and job-matching help before displacement peaks after 2027.
- Investing: faster growth often means higher returns to capital, but also a bigger shift from workers to owners. Labor share can fall from 60% toward 45% in the wild story.
- Personal finance: average pay rises but office pay stalls or falls while other pay rises a lot, so where you work matters more.
- Public discussion: anyone can try the online scenario explorer, move the levers, and see paths for income, pay, and unemployment through 2030.

## Conclusions & takeaways
- The same engine gives very different futures. Small changes in AI spread make a huge difference by 2030.
- Growth and job pain can rise together. The town can get much richer while bakers still struggle to find work.
- Who gains depends on frictions. If machines are hard to build fast, or pay moves slowly, workers feel more pain and owners gain more.
- There is a pay-versus-jobs trade-off. If pay stays high, unemployment rises more. If pay falls fast, more stay employed but earn less.
- The public already expects the busy story, not the mild one. Views vary widely, but the middle guess is substantial change.
- Remember in a month: these are scenarios, not predictions. No probabilities, horizon stops at 2030, only two job groups, one type of capital, no crashes or politics, and only a small boost from faster science by 2030.

## Jargon decoder
The paper uses economics words for simple ideas. Here is what they mean:

| Term | What it means in plain words |
| --- | --- |
| GDP | Total income of the town or country in a year. Bigger GDP means more stuff and services produced. |
| Labor share | Slice of total income that goes to workers as pay. If it falls from 60% to 56%, owners of machines take more. |
| TFP | Extra output you cannot explain by more workers or more machines. Think better recipes, not more cooks or ovens. |
| Cognitive occupations | Office-type jobs: managers, professionals, sales, office staff. The only jobs AI touches directly here. |
| Automation vs augmentation | Automation means AI does the task alone. Augmentation means AI helps a person do it faster. |
| Displacement | When AI takes tasks that used to support jobs, so fewer such jobs are needed unless new tasks appear. |
| Search-matching / frictions | Finding a new job takes time, like a baker retraining as an electrician. That delay shows up as unemployment. |
| Sticky wages | Pay moves slowly. Bosses often cut jobs first instead of cutting pay right away. |
| Re-employment time | How many months a displaced worker needs to find work in a new occupation. Survey middle answer is about eight months. |
| Task reinstatement | New tasks created to replace automated ones. Half means one new task for every two automated. Zero means none. |
| Semi-endogenous growth | Idea that more research money speeds up new ideas, but slowly, because science is still held back by physical work. |
| Scenario explorer | Public website where you move levers for capability, use, gains, and automation, and see charts for income, pay, and jobs. |
