# Introduction and Framework
**In one sentence:** The paper builds a task-based framework that maps different assumptions about AI capabilities in 2026-2030 into comparable paths for GDP, wages, labor share, reallocation, and unemployment, illustrated with modest, substantial, and extreme scenarios plus a US survey.

## Key points
- Published estimates of AI's impact span orders of magnitude and use incomparable frameworks, so the paper offers an integrated model to compare assumptions rather than making predictions or assigning probabilities.
- The model converts share of tasks affected, diffusion of use, productivity gain per task, and automation-versus-augmentation share into paths for productivity, growth, wages, labor share, reallocation, and unemployment through 2030.
- AI directly affects only cognitive occupations (management, professional, sales, office), not other occupations (e.g. construction, electricians); adoption raises productivity and capital demand but displaces cognitive workers facing switching frictions, producing unemployment.
- Modest change adds <0.5 pp to GDP growth by 2030 and +0.1 pp unemployment; substantial leaves GDP 8.3% above no-AI; extreme has AI doing almost half of today's cognitive work, 15% yearly GDP growth, labor share 60% to 45%, nearly one in five cognitive workers unemployed.
- AI-accelerated innovation adds little by 2030 (labor productivity well under 1% via this channel) because research stays bottlenecked by physical tasks in the semi-endogenous setup, so it is likely a lower bound.
- Median answers from 10,980 US adults imply near-substantial outcomes — GDP +8% and cognitive employment -4% by 2030 — while almost all scenario divergence occurs after 2027.

---
## Why the paper exists
AI is likely to reshape the US and global economies in the coming decade.
But how, and by how much, is extraordinarily uncertain.
Published productivity estimates span orders of magnitude.
Employment forecasts range from negligible to catastrophic.
Many estimates use differing frameworks, so they are hard to compare.
The paper offers a common structure for comparing possibilities for 2026-2030.

## Contribution: scenarios, not predictions
The contribution is a simple, integrated framework plus a scenario explorer.
The explorer is at anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios.
It maps a small parameter set into economic paths:
fraction of tasks affected, diffusion, gain per task, automate versus augment.
Outputs are productivity, growth, wages, labor share, reallocation, unemployment.
The scenarios are explicitly not predictions.
No probabilities are attached; the goal is comparability.

## Model core logic in brief
The framework is a standard task-based model of automation and augmentation.
Workers sit in cognitive occupations or in all other occupations.
Cognitive means management, professional, sales, and office jobs.
Other means jobs not directly exposed, e.g. construction workers, electricians.
AI adoption raises productivity but lowers cognitive labor demand and wages.
Some cognitive workers must then find jobs in other occupations.
Frictions make switching hard — e.g. software engineer to electrician.
That can sustain higher unemployment.
AI also raises capital demand, lifting its return and income share.
Faster discovery of ideas is a second, relatively small growth channel.

## Three scenarios preview
Modest, substantial, and extreme change span published predictions.
Modest: GDP in 2030 is 1.6% above no-AI, slightly above Acemoglu (2025).
It matches the order of OECD figures in Filippucci, Gal, and Schief (2024, 2025).
Substantial: GDP is 8.3% above no-AI by 2030.
It matches 2023 financial and consulting forecasts.
Benchmarks: ~1.5 pp annual labor-productivity growth in Briggs and Kodnani (2023).
Also McKinsey Global Institute (2023) and Baily, Brynjolfsson, and Korinek (2023).
Also Aghion and Bunel (2024) and Penn Wharton Budget Model (Arnon and Smetters, 2025).
Extreme: GDP is 32% above no-AI by 2030, far beyond the above.
Benchmarks are AGI-growth literature: Korinek (2023) and Korinek and Suh (2024).
Plus Davidson (2023) compute take-off, Erdil et al. (2025), AI 2027 (Kokotajlo et al., 2025).
In the extreme, AI does almost half of today's cognitive work.
GDP growth reaches 15% per year.
In every scenario, employment shifts cognitive to other occupations.
Cognitive wages grow more slowly than without AI; other wages grow faster.
Extreme gaps: cognitive wage 11.5% below no-AI path, other wage 34% above.
Unemployment depends on numbers affected and ease of switching.
Almost all divergence comes after 2027.
Labor share falls as wages lag output; extreme: 60% today to 45% in 2030.

## Innovation channel is small by 2030
The growth speedup from AI in innovation is surprisingly minor.
This holds even in the extreme scenario.
The model follows semi-endogenous growth in Jones (1995).
Automating research raises research inputs to knowledge production.
But research stays bottlenecked by physical tasks.
Labor productivity rises well under 1% via this channel in all scenarios.
Measured TFP rises by less.
The model omits research-automation feedbacks relevant by 2030.
It cannot generate Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2019) singularities.
Nor the explosive path of Davidson, Halperin, Houlden, and Korinek (2026).
So these innovation effects are likely a lower bound.

## Survey preview
The authors also ran a representative survey of US adults.
Sample: 10,980 respondents.
They asked when AI can do eight cognitive tasks of rising difficulty.
They asked how widely AI is used and how large gains are.
They asked whether workers are automated or augmented.
They asked how long a displaced worker needs to find a new job.
Median answers fed through the model land near substantial change.
That means GDP up 8% and cognitive employment down 4% by 2030.

## Stakes, caveats, and roadmap
Labor-market data to date show relatively muted AI effects.
This holds even as measured AI usage grew rapidly.
Evidence on starting wages in exposed jobs is mixed (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025; Azar et al., 2025).
Two readings fit: truly modest change, or the start of an extreme path.
Modest effects need only small institutional adjustment.
Large displacement with much higher output may require new resource mechanisms.
Options span retraining to new insurance and income support.
Caveats: the model omits catastrophic risks, politics, cycles, and finance shocks.
It has no demand feedback beyond slow real-wage adjustment.
It values no household production outside GDP (Nguyen et al., 2026).
Workers differ only by status and two occupation groups, with no tenure loss (Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan, 1993; Huckfeldt, 2022; Braxton and Taska, 2023).
Capital is one good on a fixed-elasticity schedule; only cognitive tasks covered, so analysis stops at 2030.
Literature spans task models (Zeira, 1998; Autor, Levy, and Murnane, 2003; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019), capital supply (Moll, Rachel, and Restrepo, 2022), growth (Romer, 1990; Jones, 1995; Bloom et al., 2020), matching (Diamond, 1982; Mortensen and Pissarides, 1994; den Haan, Ramey, and Watson, 2000; Blanchard and Gali, 2007; Michaillat, 2012), and task bundles (Autor and Thompson, 2025; Fukui, Nakamura, and Steinsson, 2026).
Roadmap: Section 2 model, Section 3 calibration plus scenarios plus survey, Section 4 results, Section 5 conclusions; Appendix A simulation, B survey, C innovation derivations.
