# Results: The Three Scenarios

**In one sentence:** By 2030 AI adds 1.6% to GDP in the modest scenario, 8.3% in the substantial scenario, and 32.4% in the extreme scenario — with the labor-market pain concentrated on cognitive (knowledge-work) occupations.

## Key points

- **Modest scenario GDP:** GDP is 1.6% above the no-AI path in 2030 (index 114.5 vs 112.7 with 2024 = 100), growing at 2.4%/yr vs 2.0%/yr without AI — about ten extra months of normal growth over four years.
- **Modest labor effects are tiny:** average wage +0.7% (cognitive +0.4%, other +1.1%), labor share 60.0 → 59.4%, cognitive employment −0.5% since mid-2026, overall unemployment 3.9% vs 3.8% normal.
- **Substantial scenario GDP:** GDP is 8.3% above the no-AI path in 2030 (index 122.1), growing at 5.4%/yr — faster than the 4.7% peak of the 1990s dot-com boom.
- **Substantial distribution split:** average wage +2.1% hides cognitive wages −0.3% vs other occupations +5.9%; labor share falls 60 → 56% (capital 40 → 44%), a 4-point shift in four years ≈ the whole US labor-share decline since 1980.
- **Substantial jobs:** cognitive employment −3.9% since mid-2026 (other occupations +4.6%); cognitive unemployment rises 2.9 → 4.5% (over 50% higher), overall unemployment 4.6%.
- **Extreme scenario is transformative:** GDP +32.4% above no-AI (40% above mid-2026), growth 15.4%/yr (incomes double every ~5 years); labor share collapses 60 → 45.2%, capital share 40 → 54.8%, return to capital 6.5 → 8.3%.
- **Extreme labor pain + survey check:** cognitive employment −21.5%, cognitive unemployment 17.9%, overall 11.9%; cognitive wages −11.5% vs no-AI (flat in absolute terms) while other wages +33.6%; the median survey respondent implies outcomes near substantial (GDP +8.6%, cognitive employment −4.2%, unemployment ~4.6%).

### The two technology channels (Table 3, panel D)

Output rises through two channels: higher measured total factor productivity (TFP) on tasks AI touches, and a larger stock of ideas (At) that compounds growth. In 2030 measured TFP is +0.7% above no-AI in modest, +3.1% in substantial, and +13.4% in extreme; TFP growth runs at 1.2, 2.3, and 7.3%/yr respectively against 1.0%/yr without AI. The ideas-stock channel is small by 2030 — the stock is only +0.07%, +0.20%, and +0.61% above no-AI, growing at 1.69, 1.76, and 2.02%/yr vs 1.67% — so nearly all of the 2030 GDP gap comes from the direct productivity and automation channels, not from accumulated new ideas. That ordering matters: ideas compound later, which is one reason the GDP paths in Figure 2 keep steepening through 2030 instead of flattening.

## 4.1 The modest change scenario

AI is a small technology; the economy stays on a "normal" path with changes at the margins. Inputs: only 4% of economy-wide tasks impacted by 2030 (capability m2030 = 0.20, diffusion d2030 = 0.20); +35% productivity on impacted tasks (a2030 = 0.30); half automated, half augmented (ψ2030 = 0.50); one new cognitive task per two automated (ρ = 0.5).

Outcomes: GDP +1.6% above no-AI, growth 2.4%/yr; average wage +0.7% (cognitive +0.4%); return to capital 6.6% vs 6.5%; capital stock +2.3%; labor share 60.0 → 59.4%; cognitive employment −0.5% since mid-2026; unemployment 3.9% vs 3.8% normal — only small labor-market effects.

Modest at a glance (2030):
- GDP level: +1.6% above no-AI (index 114.5 vs 112.7)
- GDP growth: 2.4%/yr vs 2.0%/yr no-AI
- Average / cognitive / other wages: +0.7 / +0.4 / +1.1%
- Labor / capital share: 59.4 / 40.6 (from 60 / 40)
- Cognitive employment: −0.5% since mid-2026
- Unemployment (cognitive / all): 2.9 / 3.9% (norms 2.9 / 3.8%)
- Measured TFP: +0.7% above no-AI, growing 1.2%/yr

## 4.2 The substantial change scenario

AI is pivotal — bigger than the internet — with large macro effects on GDP and on cognitive occupations. Inputs: 12% of economy tasks (≈20% of cognitive workers' 2025 tasks) impacted (m2030 = 0.30, d2030 = 0.40); +57% productivity on impacted tasks (a2030 = 0.45); 75% automated, 25% augmented (ψ2030 = 0.75); one new task per four automated (ρ = 0.25).

Outcomes: GDP +8.3% above no-AI, growth 5.4%/yr (vs 2% no-AI; dot-com peak was 4.7% in 1999). Average wage +2.1% — far below GDP — because cognitive wages slip −0.3% while other wages rise +5.9%. Labor share 60 → 56%, capital 40 → 44%: 4 points in four years, nearly the entire post-1980 US labor-share decline (Karabarbounis and Neiman, 2014). Cognitive employment −3.9% since mid-2026; other-occupation employment +4.6%; slow reallocation lifts cognitive unemployment 2.9 → 4.5%.

Substantial at a glance (2030):
- GDP level: +8.3% above no-AI (index 122.1)
- GDP growth: 5.4%/yr vs 2.0% no-AI (dot-com peak: 4.7% in 1999)
- Average / cognitive / other wages: +2.1 / −0.3 / +5.9%
- Labor / capital share: 56.1 / 43.9 (from 60 / 40)
- Capital stock: +13.8%; return to capital: 7.0% (from 6.5%)
- Cognitive employment: −3.9% since mid-2026; other employment +4.6%
- Unemployment (cognitive / all): 4.5 / 4.6% (norms 2.9 / 3.8%)
- Labor income: +1.4% above no-AI; capital income: +18.9%
- Measured TFP: +3.1% above no-AI, growing 2.3%/yr

Reading the paths: the average-wage panel fans upward after 2028 (0.7 / 2.1 / 9.7), while the cognitive-wage panel fans downward — modest stays just above zero at +0.4, substantial dips just below at −0.3, extreme plunges to −11.5. The return-to-capital panel rises in staircase order (6.6 / 7.0 / 8.3 over a 6.5 no-AI baseline), mirroring capital-stock gaps of +2.3 / +13.8 / +56.3%. The labor-share panel is the mirror image: modest clings to 59.4 near the 60.0 dashed line, substantial sags to 56.1, extreme dives to 45.2. The mechanics are identical in every scenario — output up, cognitive employment down, other employment up — what differs is size and speed.

## 4.3 The extreme change scenario

AI transforms the economy beyond any historical event in size and speed. Inputs: 30% of economy tasks (≈half of cognitive workers' 2025 tasks) impacted (m2030 = 0.50, d2030 = 0.60); productivity more than doubles on affected tasks (a2030 = 0.80, exp(0.8) ≈ 2.2); 90% automated, 10% augmented (ψ2030 = 0.90); no reinstatement (ρ = 0).

Outcomes: GDP 40% above mid-2026, +32.4% above no-AI; growth 15.4%/yr — at that rate incomes double every 5 years vs every 35 years in the last century of US history. Average wage +9.7% masks cognitive −11.5% (slightly below mid-2026 in absolute terms, given ~2%/yr no-AI wage growth) vs other occupations +33.6%. Labor share 60 → 45%, capital 40 → 55%: 15% of GDP shifts from labor to capital; return to capital 6.5 → 8.3% (+28%). Cognitive employment −21.5%; cognitive unemployment 17.9% (above any postwar US rate); economy-wide 11.9% (vs <10% after the financial crisis, ~8% in Covid-19).

Extreme at a glance (2030):
- GDP level: +32.4% above no-AI (index 149.3; +40% vs mid-2026)
- GDP growth: 15.4%/yr (doubling every ~5 years at that rate)
- Average / cognitive / other wages: +9.7 / −11.5 / +33.6%
- Labor / capital share: 45.2 / 54.8 (from 60 / 40)
- Capital stock: +56.3%; return to capital: 8.3% (from 6.5%, +28%)
- Cognitive employment: −21.5% since mid-2026
- Unemployment (cognitive / all): 17.9 / 11.9%
- Cognitive wage bill: −31.0%; capital income: +81.4%
- Total labor income: +0.5% (share falls by 1/4, GDP rises by 1/3)
- Measured TFP: +13.4% above no-AI, growing 7.3%/yr

**Who gains and who loses:** GDP is a third larger but cognitive workers earn less with more unemployment. Total labor income ≈ its no-AI level (0.45 × 1.32 ≈ 0.60); all GDP gains accrue to capital (+81% above no-AI). The cognitive wage bill is −31% (wages −11.5% on −21.5% fewer jobs); other occupations' wage bill rises by nearly the same amount. Gains are ~3× the cognitive loss, so compensation is arithmetically possible: a ~9%-of-GDP transfer (≈ Social Security + Medicare combined) would hold cognitive income at no-AI and leave the rest >20% ahead — but transfers of that scale for a technology shock have no precedent (cf. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson, 2013, on Chinese import competition).

The middle panel (cognitive unemployment) starts all lines at the 2.9% normal level; modest never leaves it (2.9), substantial climbs to 4.5, extreme erupts to 17.9. Because reallocation takes time, unemployment absorbs the displacement first — the paper stresses the 2030 snapshot is mid-transition, not a resting point.

## 4.4 The scenarios implied by survey responses

Each respondent's five answers (capability, use, automation, speed-up, re-employment time) are mapped to model parameters and run through the model (reinstatement, posting speed, wage rigidity, capital elasticity held at substantial values: ρ = 0.25, θH = 0.25, ξ = 0.5, ε = 3; 3,259 full respondents, weighted).

The median American expects capable AI (six of eight tasks by 2030) but limited diffusion: used on two-fifths of what it could do, augmenting half the time, +~55% productivity on impacted tasks. Median model outcomes sit near substantial: GDP +8.6% above no-AI, unemployment ~4.6% for both cognitive and all workers.

What the median respondent's answers map to: capability m2030 = 0.44 (six of eight tasks, between substantial's 0.3 and extreme's 0.5); diffusion d2030 = 0.40 (exactly the substantial value); automation share 0.47 (the modest value of 0.50); productivity gain ≈ 0.44 in logs or about one-third faster per task; re-employment in ~8 months (slower than substantial assumes, faster than extreme). Dispersion is wide: ~30% expect no time saving on a suited task while 49% expect the time at least halved, so the inter-quartile GDP range runs from +3% to +19% above no-AI. The paper's point is that the public as a whole is already pricing something like the substantial scenario, not the modest one.
