# SwarmAgentic — Plain-language explainer

## What is this about?

SwarmAgentic is a framework that builds a whole team of Artificial Intelligence (AI) assistants from scratch, starting from nothing more than a short task description and a scoring rule. Instead of a person hand-designing who does what and in which order, the system invents the roles (for example a travel planner, a budget checker, a quality reviewer), writes each role's instructions, arranges them into a workflow, and then keeps improving both the roles and the workflow on its own. The search method is Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), an algorithm inspired by flocks of birds, reinterpreted so that a Large Language Model (LLM) reads failures and rewrites the team in words rather than nudging numbers. Each candidate team is treated as one particle flying through a space of possible designs, guided by its own best version so far and by the best team anyone in the group has found.

## Why does it matter?

Most existing systems for automatically building AI assistants still need heavy human help: someone supplies starter agents, fixed templates, or a frozen way for agents to talk to each other. The paper scores seven earlier frameworks on three autonomy properties and finds that none of them invents agents from scratch while also improving both agent behavior and teamwork by itself. That manual scaffolding becomes expensive whenever the task changes, and it breaks down on open-ended problems like planning a full trip under many conflicting constraints, where nobody knows the right team shape in advance. SwarmAgentic is presented as the only compared framework satisfying all three autonomy properties, and its headline result is a 261.8% relative gain over Automated Design of Agentic Systems (ADAS) on the TravelPlanner benchmark, which suggests that removing the hand-built scaffolding genuinely unlocks better plans rather than just sounding elegant.

## How does it work?

The process runs in a loop with a small group of candidate teams, five particles refined over ten iterations in the main configuration. It begins with diverse starting teams created at different randomness levels, so some stay close to familiar patterns while others try unusual structures, and each team is scored by the task's objective function. Before every change, a language model diagnoses what is actually wrong, sorting problems into missing or redundant agents, vague instructions, and missing, redundant, or badly connected workflow steps. Then three guidance signals are combined: lessons from past failures so the same bad fix is not repeated, lessons from the particle's own personal best, and lessons from the swarm's global best, with the global and personal guidance forced to quote exact phrases from the better team so improvements stay grounded. Finally those plans are compiled into real edits using shared operations, namely adding, modifying, or deleting roles, and adding, deleting, reordering, or repairing the inputs and outputs of workflow steps, and the loop repeats until the iteration budget runs out and the best team overall is returned.

A concrete travel-planning example in the paper shows how each signal earns its keep: global-best guidance adds a Quality Assurance Specialist who reviews the whole plan, personal-best guidance inserts a cross-verification step where an Accommodation Coordinator double-checks budget, minimum stays, and room counts before the plan moves on, and failure-driven adjustment hardens the reviewer's checklist with an explicit budget-compliance item after earlier cost violations. The discovered teams are real runnable programs, such as an eight-step five-role TravelPlanner system ending in a Travel Plan Integrator, and the paper contrasts this with the ADAS workflow, which has no dedicated accommodation specialist and therefore often violates room-type, minimum-stay, occupancy, or pet rules even when each part looks locally sensible.

## Where can this be used?

The paper tests the idea on six settings that span planning, coordination, writing, and reasoning: long-horizon travel planning under hard constraints, trip planning, meeting planning, and calendar scheduling from the Natural Plan suite, open-ended Creative Writing from unordered key points, and Multilingual Grade School Math (MGSM) as a structured reasoning check. Reported scores lead the baselines in every column, including perfect delivery rates on TravelPlanner and strong results on meeting planning, calendar scheduling, and MGSM, and a system discovered with a small cheap model still performs best when run on stronger models from other providers. That pattern suggests the natural home for this approach is any task where the right division of labor is unknown in advance and constraints interact globally, such as itinerary building, scheduling with conflicts, or multi-stage writing pipelines, while the MGSM result is used to argue that full autonomy does not sacrifice performance on more structured problems either.

## Conclusions and takeaways

The central claim is that jointly inventing agents and their collaboration from scratch, guided by explicit failure memory plus personal-best and global-best examples, beats pipelines that start from hand-written seeds or frozen interaction patterns. The ablation study backs this up: on a Creative Writing sample the full configuration scores 8.8, while removing workflow restructuring drops it to 6.7, removing agent adaptation drops it to 7.3, and removing failure memory drops it to 8.4, so every piece carries weight and the workflow restructuring matters most. The stated limits are that the system starts with no domain templates that could speed it up on routine problems, that model hallucinations can propagate through the optimization loop, and that everything is text-only with no vision or physical action. Taken together, the takeaway is a practical one: if you can describe the job and how to score it, a swarm of candidate teams can discover a specialized, interpretable workflow that a fixed template would likely miss.

## Jargon decoder

- **Agentic system:** a team of AI assistants, each with a role and instructions, that pass work along a workflow to solve a task together.
- **From-scratch generation:** inventing all roles and workflows from the task description, with no starter agents or task-specific templates supplied by a person.
- **Self-optimizing functionality:** the system rewriting an individual agent's own instructions and responsibilities from feedback, without manual edits.
- **Self-optimizing collaboration:** the system restructuring teamwork itself, meaning step order, dependencies, added or removed steps, and how information flows between agents.
- **Particle:** one candidate team in the swarm; its position is the current design and its velocity is the planned set of text edits for the next round.
- **Personal best and global best:** the best design this particle has ever seen versus the best design any particle in the swarm has ever seen, both used as examples to learn from.
- **Failure-aware velocity update:** the step that merges three inputs, namely what failed before and must be avoided, what the personal best teaches, and what the global best teaches, into one edit plan.
- **Position update:** the step that turns the edit plan into a concrete new team using Add, Delete, Modify, and Reorder operations on roles and steps.
- **Delivery rate, commonsense and hard-constraint pass rates, final rate:** TravelPlanner scores measuring whether a plan was produced at all, whether it respects everyday plausibility rules, whether it respects strict user requirements, and whether it passes everything end to end.
- **MODEL SWARMS:** a related but different method that blends the internal weights of existing models into one opaque model, rather than building an inspectable team of agents the way SwarmAgentic does.
