# Stellar Colosseum: A Many-Agent Harness for Long-Horizon Research in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science

**Paper:** [Stellar Colosseum: A Many-Agent Harness for Long-Horizon Research in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15983) — arXiv:2609.15983v2 [cs.AI], 15 Sep 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine trying to prove a hard math theorem as sending an expedition into unknown mountains rather than a single sprint. Stellar Colosseum first sends out many scout teams to try different routes and deliberately sends skeptics to poke holes in each route, and only when one route looks solid enough does it allow the team to split the climb into linked base camps that can be built in parallel. If one camp fails it is rebuilt locally without tearing down the whole mountain, and if the final assembled climb has a fatal crack, expert inspectors point exactly at the broken section so it can be patched or the expedition can pick a new route while keeping all maps and warnings from the failed attempt.

## TL;DR

Stellar Colosseum is a model-agnostic, many-agent harness for allocating inference across long-horizon mathematics and theoretical computer science research. It organizes work at two levels: a stage-level pipeline of strategy exploration, readiness gating, proof decomposition into a dependency graph, parallel subproblem solving, and global verification with revision or re-exploration, and a within-stage procedure that generates populations of candidates, attacks each with targeted falsification, and synthesizes random overlapping subsets through a tree-structured aggregation that preserves critiques alongside proposals. Shared research state carries the current strategy, sectioned plan, completed sections, verifier findings, the full latest draft plus feedback, and a curated knowledge directory across rounds. Demonstrated with Gemini 3.1 Pro (plus Gemini 3.7 Flash on TCS-Bench), the workflow contributed to several new results on open problems from FOCS and JMLR papers, reached 71.0% on TCS-Bench via cross-model selection, solved 218 of 222 Codeforces problems in a proof-oriented pipeline with execution feedback, and was integrated into Google Antigravity's Teamwork framework as the Long Proof pattern.

## Problem & Motivation

Language models can produce plausible short proofs but remain unreliable on long-horizon research where progress depends on a sequence of uncertain, interdependent decisions. Real research starts with literature search and small examples, tries several representations and conjectures before finding lemmas worth proving, and even a promising approach can fail when a key lemma turns out false or a later step exposes a missing assumption. As a proof grows, definitions and assumptions must stay consistent across dependent lemmas, a change in one part can alter what later sections must establish, and a failed approach may still yield a useful restriction, counterexample, or alternative formulation. The paper frames this as a decision problem of what to develop, what to repair, and when to change direction using evidence gathered along the way. More inference-time compute helps by exploring and checking more paths, but agreement among candidates can hide a shared error, so a flat vote over final answers gives little guidance for proofs. Colosseum is designed around four challenges that follow from this: strategic uncertainty about which route to pursue, distributed technical difficulty spread over interdependent bottlenecks, long outputs with error accumulation across sections, and the need to record failure and partial progress as specific claims and objections rather than generic failure judgments.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Stage-level research pipeline with exploration, gating, and repair loops.** The workflow runs through strategy exploration, a readiness gate, proof decomposition, parallel subproblem solving, and global verification, with explicit loops for local retry, revision and re-verification, and re-exploration. Exploration develops and challenges candidate routes until one is concrete enough to decompose, decomposition turns it into a sectioned skeleton with a dependency graph, eligible sections are solved in parallel, and a rejected assembled proof either enters revision (replacing section bodies or modifying the outline before reassembly) or returns to exploration when the strategy no longer supports a credible path.

2. **Readiness gate as an explicit decompose-or-continue decision.** Rather than decomposing the first plausible idea, a dedicated gate audits whether a strategy card is concrete enough to support a proof plan, checking that the central reduction and mechanism are stable, unresolved claims are precise enough to assign to sections, and no unresolved bridge is likely to change the target or architecture. A route with an unresolved fatal bridge can only proceed under strict stability conditions with every remaining obligation assigned a concrete proof or verification path, while a major obligation that could force a route change or has no clear repair path sends the strategy back for further exploration.

3. **Dependency-aware decomposition with local parallel solving.** The decomposer produces a numbered, sectioned LaTeX skeleton plus a directed acyclic graph of subproblems in topological order, where document order controls exposition and the graph controls the order work can proceed. A subproblem becomes eligible once its dependencies complete, independent sections are solved in parallel with relevant completed sections in context, and each section faces a local reviewer before commit so a failure triggers a local retry with the failed section and review as extra input while preserving completed work elsewhere.

4. **Adversarial generate–falsify–aggregate inference reused in every stage.** A difficult stage is never entrusted to a single continuation; instead the harness generates a population of diverse candidates (strategies, skeletons, section bodies, verifier assessments, or revisions) under typed schemas, subjects each to adversarial reviewers hunting for counterexamples, invalid implications, circularity, theorem misuse, and target mismatch, and then reduces the candidate–critique bundles through an overlapping random-sample tree. Each aggregation node draws a random subset with overlap across groups, merges compatible components while retaining competing branches and unresolved objections, and the root returns a synthesized artifact plus any surviving objections, so critiques stay attached to the proposals they address.

5. **Global verification that rejects on a single concrete defect.** The global verifier reads the original problem plus the completed document as one argument with local reviews as audit context, catching cross-section failures such as wrong-assumption dependency use, notation drift, omitted cases, and silently inherited conditional claims. It runs multiple independent reviews merged through tree aggregation that preserves distinct substantive objections rather than taking a majority vote, so one concrete fatal defect suffices to reject, and its feedback localizes every material defect to the arising section and claim to make revision actionable.

6. **Two-form shared research knowledge across rounds.** Information stays available within a round (strategy, plan, completed bodies, verifier findings) and across rounds through the full latest draft plus its verifier feedback passed directly into the next attempt, alongside a curated knowledge directory that preserves reusable findings aggregation might otherwise discard. A curator maintains theorems and lemmas, failed approaches with precise failure points, references with needed hypotheses, and observations with evidence and implications, with each entry retaining its source and caveats and updated across rounds.

## Key Findings

Runs of the workflow contributed five new research results detailed in companion papers, spanning stronger coresets for l_p subspace approximation when p > 2 (improving the epsilon dependence from epsilon^{-p} to epsilon^{-2} at the same sampling rule by keeping truncation in the sampling probabilities), a conditional sparsity lower bound establishing the condition-number barrier in sparse least squares under the randomized exact-volume Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis, a near-tight dimension lower bound for maximum inner-product and Chamfer embeddings that nearly closes the gap between 1/epsilon and 1/epsilon^2 in the exponent, a single-stage Hadamard quantization scheme using pairwise-independent dithers that removes the residual-stage payload and cuts the leading constant by about 5.93x, and a near-optimal prefix-matrix factorization lower bound of Omega(log^{3/2} n / (log log n)^{3/2}). Two long-horizon case studies demonstrate sustained coherent behavior: 46-page and 75-page revisable proof drafts with explicit dependency structure for even-m constructions in Knuth's cycles problem, and a 22-page independent rediscovery draft of the Erdos unit-distance breakthrough (u(n) >= n^{1+epsilon_0}) developed over 15 exploration rounds with internet disabled, arriving at the same central architecture of unramified towers and relative unit groups as the original solution. On breadth evaluation, TCS-Bench's 300 research-level theorem-proving tasks from FOCS, STOC, and SODA papers were each tackled with one Colosseum proof per base model, and critique-based cross-model selection between Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3.7 Flash runs reached 71.0% accuracy (213 solved), well above direct Gemini 3.1 Pro at 30.3%, Gemini 3.1 DeepThink at 52.0%, each individual Colosseum run at 54.0% and 55.0%, and GPT-5.6 Pro (max) at 68.0%, with the critique signal separating grader-labeled correct and incorrect proofs at AUC 0.896 against an oracle best-of-two upper bound of 77.3%. In competitive programming, the proof-oriented workflow augmented with implementation and execution feedback recorded 218 accepted solutions on a 222-problem Codeforces suite.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The paper notes that current runs fix population widths, aggregation fan-in, and sample counts up front even though the value of an additional sample or round varies across stages and subproblems, so fixed allocation can be inefficient. It proposes a future adaptive controller that reuses signals the workflow already produces, including strategy diversity, unresolved objections, repeated local failures, and agreement among independently generated candidates, to expand uncertain branches, grant additional retries to unstable sections, and stop stages whose outputs have stabilized, with compute-matched evaluation needed to distinguish better allocation from simply using more inference. A second direction is to use the harness's structured research trajectories — candidate strategies, falsifier critiques, aggregation decisions, dependency graphs, intermediate drafts, and revision histories from runs with externally validated outcomes — as post-training data where intermediate states supervise strategy selection, decomposition, objection handling, and revision while rejected routes and verifier feedback supply negative and corrective signals, distilling inference-time orchestration into the base model. The main challenge flagged is credit assignment, since a successful final result does not reveal which intermediate strategies, critiques, or revisions caused progress, though the harness branching structure may help by comparing candidates sharing the same context but leading to different downstream outcomes.
