# Retrieval Practice: ARES: An Automated Evaluation Framework for Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems

Answer from memory before opening any answer. Run sessions with `kb show summary/quiz`.

### Q1. What are the three dimensions ARES scores a RAG system on, and why does it use three separate judge models instead of one combined score?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Context relevance (is the retrieved passage relevant to the query?), answer faithfulness (is the answer grounded in the retrieved passage, or hallucinated?), and answer relevance (does the answer address the query?). Three separate judges are used because each targets a distinct pipeline failure mode — a retrieval problem, a generation-grounding problem, and a generation-relevance problem look different and need different fixes, so collapsing them into one score would hide which stage actually broke. See [[wiki/01-introduction-and-related-work|Introduction & Related Work]].

### Q2. Why does the paper single out RAGAS and EXAM as the two most relevant prior systems, and what specific limitation does each have that ARES avoids?

> [!tip]- Answer
> EXAM requires every query to come bundled with a set of associated sub-questions to grade against — a data-preparation burden ARES doesn't impose. RAGAS relies on a small set of fixed, hand-written heuristic prompts that offer little adaptability to new corpora and, in ARES's own head-to-head evaluation, substantially underperform ARES's fine-tuned judges. See [[wiki/01-introduction-and-related-work|Introduction & Related Work]].

### Q3. Walk through how ARES generates negative examples for judge fine-tuning — what are "weak" and "strong" negatives, and why does it need both?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Weak negatives are easy-to-distinguish mismatches: unrelated in-domain passages for context relevance, or answers sampled from other passages for faithfulness/relevance. Strong negatives are harder: passages from the same source document as the gold passage (or BM25 top-10 similar passages when none exist), and contradicted answers generated by prompting FLAN-T5 XXL. Both are needed in equal numbers to positives so the judge learns to reject both obviously wrong and subtly wrong candidates, rather than only spotting easy negatives. See [[wiki/02-ares-method|The ARES Method]].

### Q4. Why doesn't ARES just report the raw average score from its fine-tuned judges, and why doesn't it just rely on the small human-preference validation set instead?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Raw judge averages come entirely from unlabeled data scored by a synthetically-trained model, so they may be systematically inaccurate in ways invisible without ground truth. Relying only on the small human-preference set instead would require expensive per-system manual labeling of every RAG system's outputs, which doesn't scale. PPI resolves this tension by using the small labeled set to learn a rectifier function that corrects the judge's predictions across the much larger unlabeled pool, giving both accuracy and confidence bounds cheaply. See [[wiki/02-ares-method|The ARES Method]].

### Q5. Which two benchmark families are used in the main experiments, and why does the paper exclude answer faithfulness from that evaluation?

> [!tip]- Answer
> KILT (Natural Questions, HotpotQA, FEVER, Wizards of Wikipedia) and SuperGLUE (MultiRC, ReCoRD), all with open-domain retrieval versions constructed for the experiments. Answer faithfulness is excluded there because neither benchmark family has human-annotated hallucinated answers to test against — faithfulness is instead evaluated separately on real attribution datasets (AIS) in Section 5.2. See [[wiki/03-experimental-setup|Experimental Setup]].

### Q6. A team wants to compare two RAG configurations that differ only slightly in accuracy. What metric does ARES use to judge whether it can reliably tell them apart, and what does a value above 0.9 mean?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Kendall's τ (tau), the rank correlation coefficient between the correct ranking of systems and ARES's predicted ranking, computed from concordant vs. discordant pairwise comparisons. A τ above 0.9 is treated as successful — meaning ARES's ranking of RAG configurations closely matches the true ranking, which matters because developers typically make pairwise comparisons (this retriever vs. that one, this chunking strategy vs. that one) rather than reading absolute scores. See [[wiki/03-experimental-setup|Experimental Setup]].

### Q7. On the AIS attribution benchmark, how close does ARES get to the ground-truth hallucination rate, and with how much labeled data?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Within about 2.5 percentage points of the ground-truth average hallucination rate (e.g. predicted 0.478 vs. actual 0.458 on WoW, and 0.835 vs. 0.859 on CNN/DM), using only 200 human-preference datapoints for PPI. This is notable because it shows the PPI-correction approach transfers from the synthetic KILT/SuperGLUE setting to a real attribution/hallucination-detection task. See [[wiki/04-results-and-analysis|Results & Analysis]].

### Q8. The paper reports that a judge fine-tuned on Natural Questions loses a lot of raw accuracy when applied to a very different domain (e.g. cross-lingual or text-to-code), yet still sometimes yields a usable Kendall's τ after PPI. Explain that apparent contradiction.

> [!tip]- Answer
> Raw judge accuracy measures how often the classifier's individual predictions are correct, which can collapse when the domain shift is drastic (e.g. NQ→FEVER answer relevance dropping to 28.4% raw accuracy). PPI, however, doesn't need every prediction to be correct — it learns a rectifier function from the small labeled validation set that statistically corrects for the judge's systematic error pattern, and that correction can still preserve a useful *relative ranking* (0.89 Kendall's τ in that example) even when point-wise accuracy is poor. Ranking accuracy and raw classification accuracy are measuring different things. See [[wiki/04-results-and-analysis|Results & Analysis]] and [[wiki/05-appendix-details|Appendix Details]].

### Q9. What hyperparameters and stopping rule does ARES use to fine-tune its DeBERTa-v3-Large judges, and why might the "three consecutive epochs with no improvement" stopping rule matter more than a fixed epoch count?

> [!tip]- Answer
> Cross-entropy loss, Adam optimizer, a single linear classification head on the `[CLS]` token with 0.1 dropout, a linear warmup/decay learning-rate schedule, learning rate 5e-6, and batch size 32; training stops after three consecutive epochs without loss improvement on the human-preference validation set. A patience-based stopping rule (rather than a fixed epoch count) matters because the "right" amount of fine-tuning likely differs by domain and dataset size — stopping on a plateau avoids both underfitting a harder domain and overfitting an easier one with a one-size-fits-all epoch budget. See [[wiki/05-appendix-details|Appendix Details]].

### Q10. GPT-4-generated labels are proposed as a substitute for human labels in the appendix study. What's the tradeoff, and when would you actually accept it?

> [!tip]- Answer
> GPT-4 labels cut annotation cost dramatically — from hundreds of human annotations down to fewer than ten few-shot prompts — at the cost of decreasing Kendall's τ by roughly 0.05–0.30 in most settings compared to human labels. This tradeoff is acceptable when you need a fast, cheap first-pass evaluation or are iterating quickly and can tolerate a somewhat noisier ranking signal, but risky when you need the tightest possible confidence intervals or are making a high-stakes go/no-go call between RAG configurations, where the accuracy loss could flip a close comparison. See [[wiki/05-appendix-details|Appendix Details]].

### Q11. ARES validates itself on datasets with roughly 150-300 human-preference datapoints and reports strong Kendall's τ. Is that validation-set size actually adequate evidence that ARES generalizes, or is this a case where the evaluation itself deserves scrutiny?

> [!tip]- Answer
> It's suggestive but not fully conclusive. The paper does show the drop-off explicitly (τ falls to 0.44 at 25-50 labels, and 100-150 is described as a rough minimum), which is honest self-reporting of a limitation rather than a hidden one. But the "150-300 is enough" claim rests on KILT/SuperGLUE and one attribution benchmark (AIS) — all English, all drawn from a similar Wikipedia/news register — so it doesn't establish that 150 points is enough for, say, a highly specialized legal or medical corpus where few-shot synthetic generation quality (which the whole pipeline depends on) may be much lower. Treat the ~150-datapoint claim as validated for benchmark-style, general-domain text, not as a universal minimum. See [[critical_thinking|Critical Analysis]].
