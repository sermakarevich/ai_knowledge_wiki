Abstract
Knowledge graphs provide structured and reliable information
for many real-world applications, motivating increasing interest
in combining large language models (LLMs) with graph-based re-
trieval to improve factual grounding. Recent Graph-based Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) methods therefore introduce
iterative interaction between LLMs and knowledge graphs to en-
hance reasoning capability. However, existing approaches typically
depend on manually designed guidance and interact with knowl-
edge graphs through a limited set of predefined tools, which sub-
stantially constrains graph exploration. To address these limitations,
we propose GraphScout, a training-centric agentic graph reasoning
framework equipped with more flexible graph exploration tools.
GraphScout enables models to autonomously interact with knowl-
edge graphs to synthesize structured training data which are then
used to post-train LLMs, thereby internalizing agentic graph reason-
ing ability without laborious manual annotation or task curation.
Extensive experiments across five knowledge-graph domains show
that a small model (e.g., Qwen3-4B) augmented with GraphScout
outperforms baseline methods built on leading LLMs (e.g., Qwen-
Max) by an average of 16.7% while requiring significantly fewer in-
ference tokens. Moreover, GraphScout exhibits robust cross-domain
transfer performance. Our code will be made publicly available 1.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) [1, 15, 47, 53] have achieved remark-
able success in question answering and complex reasoning, but they
remain prone to hallucinations and lack reliable access to domain-
specific or up-to-date knowledge. Retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) [42] addresses these limitations by grounding model outputs
in external knowledge at inference time. While effective for unstruc-
tured text, conventional RAG pipelines struggle with structured
and relational data, such as knowledge graphs, where multi-hop
dependencies and structural constraints are essential. This has mo-
tivated a growing interest in graph-based retrieval and reasoning
(GraphRAG) [11, 19, 37], which seeks to exploit graph structure for
relational inference and information aggregation.
The first class of GraphRAG methods consists of passive retrieval-
driven approaches. These methods [23, 54] are typically built upon
the standard RAG framework. They first employ static retrieval
strategies to select a set of nodes from the knowledge graph that
are relevant to the given query, and then expand their neighboring
subgraphs according to predefined rules, such as a fixed hop count.
The resulting structured information is subsequently linearized into
textual form and provided to LLMs for downstream reasoning.
The second class comprises active traversal-based approaches.
These methods [24, 36, 43, 55] equip LLMs with a set of basic
graph interaction tools, such as node querying and relation ex-
pansion, and leverage carefully designed prompting schemes to
enable multi-round interactions with the knowledge graph during
the reasoning process. Active traversal-based approaches enable
dynamic graph exploration, enhancing retrieval–reasoning coordi-
nation. Recent methods [13, 27, 33, 34] further structure this process
through explicit traversal planning, schema-aware validation, or

Despite recent progress in GraphRAG, existing methods still suf-
fer from notable limitations in flexibility and graph exploration. On
the one hand, most current approaches rely on manually designed
and limited sets of graph interaction primitives, which constrains
their expressiveness and flexibility. For example, when performing
multi-hop neighbor queries along known path types, models often
have to invoke basic neighbor expansion operations iteratively,
resulting in prolonged interaction sequences and low efficiency.
On the other hand, LLMs lack intrinsic priors for structured graph
exploration, causing these methods to primarily depend on exter-
nal mechanisms, such as workflow constraints, to enhance graph
exploration. As a result, they struggle to achieve a fundamental
improvement in the LLM’s intrinsic graph reasoning and explo-
ration capabilities. Empirical results reported in prior work (e.g.,
GraphCoT [24] in Figure 1) also reflect these limitations to some
extent. Even with more flexible graph exploration tools, these issues
remain unresolved. For example, PolyG [34] adopts a Cypher-based
interface to enable more general graph operations, yet its perfor-
mance (in Figure 1) only marginally surpasses that of GraphCoT
due to its limited intrinsic graph reasoning and exploration abilities.
These observations suggest that current GraphRAG research
calls for a shift in methodological perspective. Rather than continu-
ally stacking increasingly complex prompt engineering or workflow
control mechanisms, a more promising direction is to equip LLMs
with more flexible and expressive graph interaction tools, while
fundamentally enhancing their intrinsic graph reasoning and explo-
ration abilities. Nevertheless, enhancing such intrinsic capabilities
typically requires targeted post-training, which in turn relies on
diverse, high-quality annotated graph reasoning trajectories that
are expensive to manually construct and difficult to scale in practice,
forming a major bottleneck.
To address the above challenges, we propose GraphScout, a
training-centric framework designed to equip LLM with intrinsic
exploration ability for agentic graph reasoning. GraphScout con-
sists of three core components. First, GraphScout provides a set
of Agentic Graph Exploration Tools, including a Code Inter-
preter and a Node Retriever, which enable LLMs to interact with the
knowledge graph in a highly flexible and programmable manner.
Second, the Graph Quizzer module leverages a strong LLM as a
senior scout to freely explore the graph environment, automatically
synthesizing diverse and high-quality graph query–answer pairs,
along with their corresponding evidence clues that serve as super-
vision signals for subsequent training. Finally, the Graph Solver
module optimizes small-parameter LLM as a junior scout under a
multi-turn interaction paradigm, enabling it to progressively ac-
quire agentic graph reasoning ability. Experiments show that LLMs
trained with GraphScout consistently outperform baselines built on
substantially larger backbone LLMs on multiple GraphRAG bench-
marks by an average margin of 16.7%, while requiring an order
of magnitude fewer inference tokens. Moreover, models trained
on a single-domain knowledge graph transfer robustly to unseen
domains, demonstrating stable cross-domain performance.

Related Work
2.1
LLM for Graph Reasoning
Inspired by the recent success of large language models (LLMs) in
natural language processing, increasing attention has been paid
to applying LLMs to graph-related tasks [23]. Existing studies can
be broadly categorized according to the role LLMs play in graph
reasoning. One line of work treats LLMs as feature extractors [6,
7, 10, 30, 56], where textual information associated with graph
nodes or edges is encoded by LLMs and then fed into graph neural
networks (GNNs) [28, 48, 52]. These methods typically adopt an
LLM-GNN cascaded architecture, leveraging LLMs for semantic
representation and GNNs for structural modeling. Another line of
research employs LLMs as final predictors [25]. In this paradigm,
graph structures are either serialized into textual sequences and
processed directly by LLMs [4, 50], or combined with GNNs that are
specifically trained or fine-tuned to provide structural supervision
for LLM-based reasoning [2, 5, 44]. Such approaches have been

2.2
Augmenting LLMs with Knowledge Graph
Despite their strong reasoning capabilities [1, 47, 53], LLMs suffer
from limitations such as hallucinations [39, 46] and outdated knowl-
edge, motivating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [3, 14, 26].
While effective for unstructured text, conventional RAG strug-
gles with relational knowledge [29, 40]. To address this gap, re-
cent work augments LLMs with knowledge graphs, giving rise to
GraphRAG [11]. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized
into two categories. The first follows a passive retrieval-driven
paradigm, retrieving relevant nodes or subgraphs and linearizing
them into textual representations for LLM consumption [4, 11, 18].
The second line of work adopts an active traversal-based paradigm,
enabling LLMs to iteratively interact with knowledge graphs by
selecting traversal operations step by step [24, 43]. Representative
methods include GraphCoT [24], which aligns chain-of-thought
reasoning with stepwise graph traversal. More recent approaches
further extend this paradigm through structured planning or agent
collaboration [13, 27, 33, 34], improving robustness and accuracy
in complex graph reasoning. PolyG [34] generates Cypher queries
conditioned on the question structure, while GraphCounselor [13]
leverages multi-agent coordination for planning and verification.
Despite these advances, such methods still rely on manually de-
signed interaction schemes and external control mechanisms, rather
than learning intrinsic graph reasoning and exploration capabilities.
3
GraphScout
As illustrated in Figure 2, GraphScout overcomes the limitations
of predefined tools by integrating a code interpreter and a node re-
triever as Agentic Graph Exploration Tools for both Graph Quizzer
and Graph Solver. Graph Quizzer is designed to explore high-quality
graph question-answer pairs for LLM post-training, employing a
strong LLM as a senior scout to construct diverse graph problems
along with their corresponding traversal clues. Graph Solver is pro-
posed to endow LLMs with intrinsic exploration by post-training
a small-parameter LLM as a junior scout, formulating graph rea-
soning as learning a multi-turn decision policy for agentic graph
interaction. Training is primarily driven by answer rewards, with
auxiliary clue-based rewards to guide exploration under sparse
feedback. Consequently, the overall GraphScout framework com-
prehensively explores diverse graph reasoning problems through
the Graph Quizzer and efficiently addresses intrinsic graph explo-
ration challenges via the Graph Solver.
