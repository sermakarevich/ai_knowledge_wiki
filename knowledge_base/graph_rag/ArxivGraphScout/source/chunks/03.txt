Baselines. We compare our GraphScout approach with several
baselines: BaseLLM, TextRAG [14], GraphRAG [54], Cypher [34],
GraphCoT [24], PolyG [34], and GraphCounselor [13]. To ensure a
comprehensive and fair evaluation of these baselines, we instantiate
each method with multiple state-of-the-art LLMs, including GPT-
4o [1], GLM-4.6 [15], Qwen-Max [53], and DeepSeek-Chat [32].
Detailed settings are provided in the Appendix A.4.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate model performance using both
model-based and automatic metrics. Specifically, we employ Qwen-
Score, an LLM-based metric using Qwen-Max as a judge to assess
answer correctness by comparing generated outputs with ground-
truth answers, and report the proportion of responses judged as
correct. Besides, we report the F1 score, which measures token-level
overlap between predicted and ground-truth answers.
Models and Training Settings. We conduct experiments using
two model variants: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B. For
the Graph Quizzer stage, we use DeepSeek-Chat to explore the
knowledge graph and synthesize sufficient training data. To pre-
vent test-set leakage, we ensure that all clue nodes in the training
set are disjoint from any nodes appearing in the test set questions.
During training, we adopt the verl [41] framework and employ its
implementation of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [16]
for reinforcement learning, training the models for 400 optimiza-
tion steps. Detailed training hyperparameters and configurations
are provided in the Appendix A.1.

Accuracy Across Question Difficulty Levels. To better
understand how GraphScout behaves under different reasoning
requirements, we further break down the GRBENCH results by

Easy. GraphScout-4B consistently achieves strong performance
across all five domains on easy questions, often outperforming
the strongest graph-based baselines. This suggests that the model
learns to reliably ground answers on graph information for common
single-hop or shallow retrieval queries.
Medium. The advantage of GraphScout becomes even more
evident on medium difficulty. Across domains, GraphScout-4B out-
performs the baselines by a substantial margin, indicating strong
capability in multi-turn exploration and multi-hop information
aggregation. These results align with our motivation that learn-
ing intrinsic exploration ability (rather than relying on prompting
heuristics) is particularly beneficial when the question requires
iterative tool use and compositional reasoning.
Hard. Hard questions are challenging for all methods. Many
hard instances in this dataset emphasize recommendation-style
reasoning and external world knowledge, placing less emphasis on
structured graph traversal. Consequently, the benefits of stronger
graph exploration ability are less pronounced, and GraphScout-4B
shows limited gains on this split.

5
Discussion
To position GraphScout, we contrast two representative GraphRAG
settings: Document-Centric, which constructs graphs on-the-
fly from unstructured text corpora (representative examples in-
clude HippoRAG [19], HyperGraphRAG [37], and Graph-R1 [36]),
and Native-KG Reasoning, which reasons over pre-existing cu-
rated knowledge graphs (representative examples include Graph-
CoT [24], GraphCounselor [13], and our proposed GraphScout).
These settings differ in several respects. First, they assume different
graph sources: document-centric methods induce graphs from text,
whereas Native KG methods operate on persistent curated graphs.
Second, their main bottlenecks are different. Document-centric
methods are often bottlenecked by graph construction quality, in-
formation loss during text-to-graph conversion, and effective multi-
hop retrieval over noisy induced structures. In contrast, Native KG
reasoning must jointly satisfy topological constraints and semantic
grounding constraints. Third, their technical goals are also different:
document-centric methods mainly improve retrieval and evidence
connectivity across textual silos, whereas native KG methods tar-
get accurate multi-hop reasoning over explicit relational structure.
Since these settings involve different inputs, failure modes, and
evaluation priorities, we focus our empirical comparisons on base-
lines under the Native KG reasoning setting, which is the setting
directly addressed by GraphScout.
6
Conclusion
This paper presents GraphScout, a framework that enhances LLMs’
agentic graph reasoning with intrinsic exploration ability. Graph-
Scout consists of three core components: Agentic Graph Exploration
Tools, which equip LLMs with more flexible and programmable
graph interaction interface; Graph Quizzer, which explores diverse
and high-quality problem sets on graphs while recording traversal
clues; and Graph Solver, which post-trains LLMs to acquire intrin-
sic graph exploration ability. Extensive experiments against state-
of-the-art GraphRAG methods demonstrate that small-parameter
LLMs equipped with GraphScout significantly outperforms substan-
tially larger LLMs by an average of 16.7%. Moreover, GraphScout
exhibits promising cross-domain generalization capability, indicat-
ing that LLMs can develop generalized graph reasoning ability even
with training on a single graph. Future work will investigate LLMs
bootstrapping via iterative self-questioning and self-answering in
an iterative enhancement loop, while addressing challenges such
as training instability and self-bias amplification.
