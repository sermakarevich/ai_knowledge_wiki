# Experience with GitHub Copilot for Developer

**Paper:** [Experience with GitHub Copilot for Developer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13282v1) — arXiv, January 2025

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine giving every developer an eager junior pair-programmer who types ahead of them, great at boilerplate and unit tests but clueless about your company's secret recipes. Zoominfo rolled this assistant out to more than 400 engineers in careful stages, like taste-testing a new dish with five chefs before serving it to the whole restaurant, and then counted how often the chefs actually kept the assistant's suggestions. Roughly one in three suggestions and one in five suggested lines survived, which added up to hundreds of thousands of lines of shipped code and about a fifth of coding time saved, with nearly three-quarters of developers happy. The catch is that the assistant still needs a senior looking over its shoulder, since it stumbles on company-specific logic and its quality can wobble from suggestion to suggestion.

## TL;DR

This paper reports Zoominfo's systematic four-phase evaluation and deployment of GitHub Copilot across 400+ geographically dispersed developers, from a 5-engineer pilot in July 2023 through a 126-engineer trial to full rollout starting September 2023. Adopting GitHub's recommended acceptance-rate metric, the authors measure about 6,500 suggestions and 15,000 suggested lines per day over 26 days in November–December 2024, finding 33% suggestion acceptance and 20% line acceptance with a slight upward trend and weekend upticks, in line with figures reported by GitHub and Google. The top four languages (TypeScript, Java, Python, JavaScript) cover close to 80% of suggestions and sustain acceptance near 30%, while HTML, CSS, JSON, and SQL show notably smaller rates, and JetBrains dominates volume over VS Code with similar suggestion rates but lower line acceptance. Developer satisfaction reaches 72% with roughly 20% time savings concentrated in boilerplate, unit tests, naming, and documentation, tempered by observed limits around domain-specific logic and inconsistent code quality requiring extra scrutiny.

## Problem & Motivation

The paper starts from a strategic belief stated verbatim in the wiki: the speed at which a company turns ideas into customer outcomes is strongly correlated with competitive advantage, and giving developers the best tooling for writing, testing, and reviewing code is fundamental to that speed. GitHub Copilot launched in 2021 promising faster development through AI-generated suggestions, yet the authors argue there remains limited empirical evidence about its evaluation, deployment, and effectiveness in medium- to large-scale enterprise environments. Zoominfo offers itself as that test case: a Go-To-Market Intelligence Platform with hundreds of millions of business contacts and company profiles, billions of daily events, close to 4,000 employees with a quarter in engineering and 400 active developers, thousands of repositories mostly on self-hosted GitHub Enterprise Server plus GitLab, and demanding scale goals around real-time serving, sub-second search, 99.9% uptime, and strict security and privacy.

To fill the evidence gap the study poses five research questions for a medium-scale enterprise setting: how Copilot should be evaluated to reach a production deployment decision, what acceptance rates look like across programming languages, what drives developer satisfaction with AI-assisted coding, how effective Copilot is at improving productivity, and what observed and potential limitations emerge. Productivity itself is framed classically as output per input, where developer input is mostly time and output is features that ultimately become customer value, measured both quantitatively through objective pipeline signals such as DORA metrics and qualitatively through subjective developer satisfaction surveys. The paper's promised contribution is therefore both methodological, a phased evaluation-to-deployment playbook with governance and compliance built in, and empirical, reporting acceptance rates, satisfaction, time savings, and lessons learned at a scale other enterprises can compare against.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Four-phase evaluation-to-rollout playbook** — After an ad hoc assessment shortly after Copilot's release generated internal excitement, the team ran a disciplined July–September 2023 sequence: a one-week Phase 1 pilot with five engineers scoring 8.8/10 overall experience and 8.6/10 productivity improvement, a Phase 2 recruitment of 126 engineers stratified across specializations, experience, geography, and stacks with security training, written compliance acknowledgments, and survey commitments, a two-week Phase 3 controlled trial with 72 survey respondents reporting 8.0/10 satisfaction and 7.6/10 productivity improvement with no reported pull-request quality decline, and a Phase 4 paced rollout from September 7, 2023 via a ServiceNow Workflow for license provisioning and compliance tracking.

2. **Acceptance rate as the success metric** — Arguing that productivity impact is hard to measure after short-term use, the authors adopt GitHub's finding that acceptance rate of shown suggestions is a better predictor of perceived productivity than alternatives. They define it per developer prompt as accepted over shown suggestions, counting even partial accepts at full credit and excluding lines shown from the denominator, averaging across developers and tracking it separately for suggestions and for lines alongside DORA metrics and satisfaction scores.

3. **Production-scale acceptance breakdown by language and editor** — Beyond a headline number, the study reports daily volume and rates over a 26-day window plus per-language tables for the top dozen languages and per-editor comparison of JetBrains versus VS Code. This reveals that each suggestion averages 2.8 lines, that suggestion acceptance runs about 1.5 times the line rate, that the top four languages drive the vast majority of suggestions and acceptances, that Go leads on rate but with small volume and unusually long suggestions, and that editors diverge on line acceptance and lines per suggestion for unknown reasons.

4. **Projected benefit taxonomy validated in practice** — The paper enumerates expected gains before measurement: automated generation of snippets and whole functions for routine patterns, pseudo-code-review trained on billions of lines to catch bugs and enforce best practices, explanation and commenting assistance that aids collaboration, reduced learning curves for new languages and frameworks, and overall gains in time efficiency, quality, onboarding, and development-cycle speed. The trial and production phases then test these against utility rankings where unit-test generation and boilerplate score highest, documentation and pattern recognition score moderate to high, and variable naming scores modestly.

5. **Enterprise governance and phased-evidence framing** — The contribution includes the compliance scaffolding rarely detailed in tool papers: prerequisite security code-review training, acknowledgments covering generative-AI usage, AI governance, data governance, ethics, and classification, unique participant identifiers, mandatory review, validation, testing, documentation, deployment, and security controls. By pairing this with mixed quantitative acceptance data and qualitative survey evidence, the paper positions its 33%/20%/72% figures as enterprise-comparable benchmarks while noting alignment with GitHub and other-company results as a future research question in itself.

## Key Findings

Over the November 11 to December 9, 2024 measurement window the team observes close to 6,500 suggestions and 15,000 lines suggested per day, with standard deviations near half the means driven by weekday-weekend swings rather than day-to-day noise, weekday volumes running above average and weekend volumes well below it across Israel Friday–Saturday and US/India Saturday–Sunday schedules. Developers accept about one-third of suggestions and one-fifth of suggested lines, with the suggestion rate roughly 1.5 times the line rate, slight upward slopes in both trend lines, and the curious pattern that weekend acceptance rates usually rise even as volume falls. At a few tens of suggestions per developer per day this compounds quickly to about 75,000 accepted lines in the reporting period alone and hundreds of thousands of Copilot-generated lines in the codebase, matching rates reported by GitHub and by another company using a different AI pair programmer.

Language and editor cuts add texture without overturning the headline. The top four languages account for close to 80% of suggestions, 75% of suggested lines, and close to 85% of acceptances, holding above 30% suggestion acceptance, while per-language suggestion rates span roughly 14% to 32% with HTML, CSS, JSON, and SQL interestingly weaker than general-purpose languages for unknown reasons. JetBrains sees substantially more usage than VS Code, suggestion acceptance sits near 30% in both, but VS Code's line acceptance runs about 50% higher with fewer lines per suggestion, again without an established explanation. On the human side, satisfaction reaches 72% overall with strong marks for daily-task support, especially boilerplate and unit tests, plus meaningful variable names, documentation, and comments, alongside roughly 20% time savings and good-to-excellent standards alignment that nevertheless required minimal to moderate edits and extra oversight on domain-specific or highly innovative tasks.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The observed limitations center on missing domain-specific logic and inconsistent code quality, which the authors say directly erode time savings because generated code demands additional scrutiny before integration. The phased trial already surfaced warning signs in this direction: most pilot participants needed to modify suggestions, trial respondents flagged uneven performance on specialized work, and the conclusion stresses that security implications need careful handling even amid overall success. To manage this the paper's implicit recommendation is to keep the guardrails it used, namely security training, policy acknowledgment, thorough code review, functionality validation and testing, documentation standards, and paced license distribution with utilization tracking.

Beyond what was observed, the authors envision eight risks they explicitly note have not yet materialized: inadvertent exposure of sensitive or proprietary information learned from public repositories, intellectual-property infringement from resembling copyrighted snippets, security vulnerabilities in generated code, telemetry data-privacy concerns, regulatory compliance violations in strict industries, over-reliance that trades best practices for speed, reinforcement of problematic or insecure patterns absorbed from public code, and eventual erosion of creativity and even productivity if developers stop exercising their own ideas. For future work they call for long-term impact assessment on DORA metrics, code quality, and maintenance implications, plus study of effects on developer learning and skill development to inform enterprise adoption strategy, and they note open puzzles such as why acceptance figures align across companies and tools and why weekend rates and editor line-rates diverge.

## Authors & Institutions

The paper is authored by Gal Bakal, Ali Dasdan, Yaniv Katz, Michael Kaufman, and Guy Levin, listed in last-name alphabetical order, all affiliated with Zoominfo, the Go-To-Market Intelligence Platform company whose engineering organization served as the deployment site. The work appeared as arXiv:2501.13282v1 in Computer Science, Software Engineering, dated January 2025. The wiki notes a 37-entry reference list spanning Copilot evaluations, test generation, code quality, productivity frameworks such as DORA, SPACE, and DevEx, and related large-language-model-for-code studies, with the authors concluding their benefits-and-limitations findings are mostly in alignment with that literature apart from expected variation by task, language, and population.
