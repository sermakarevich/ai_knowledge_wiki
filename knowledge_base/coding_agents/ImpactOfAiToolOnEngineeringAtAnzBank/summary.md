# THE IMPACT OF AI TOOL ON ENGINEERING AT

**Paper:** [THE IMPACT OF AI TOOL ON ENGINEERING AT ANZ BANK: AN EMPIRICAL STUDY ON GITHUB COPILOT WITHIN CORPORATE ENVIRONMENT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05636v2)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine giving every bank software engineer a keen junior pair-programmer who types fast, knows the common patterns, and never gets tired — that is essentially what ANZ Bank tested when it handed GitHub Copilot to more than a hundred of its engineers for six weeks. Like timing two relay teams on the same obstacle course, the bank had one group code with Copilot and one without, then swapped who got the helper, and measured who finished faster and whose code held up better under inspection. The Copilot-assisted engineers finished roughly forty percent faster, spent less of their time debugging, and produced code with fewer bugs and untidy habits, while reporting that the tool was genuinely helpful for understanding old code, writing tests, and drafting documentation. The one thing the test could not really settle is whether Copilot makes code more or less secure, because the short puzzle-style tasks barely gave security flaws room to appear — so the bank's verdict was enthusiastic but cautious: roll it out, keep watching security, and keep measuring.

## TL;DR

ANZ Bank (5000+ engineers) ran a six-week controlled experiment on GitHub Copilot in mid-June to end-July 2023 to decide whether to adopt AI pair-programming at scale, combining a phase-1 exploratory use period with regular sentiment surveys and a phase-2 crossover A/B in which Control and Copilot groups solved the same twelve Python algorithmic challenges. Using GitHub usage metrics, per-challenge surveys, SonarQube static analysis, and team grading of correctness across 172 valid data points, the authors tested the null hypothesis of no Copilot effect on productivity or quality with one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at alpha 0.05. They found a statistically significant productivity gain of about 42.36 percent (mean 30.98 vs 17.86 minutes per problem), statistically significant reductions in bugs and code smells, a non-significant 12.86 percent uplift in unit-test success ratio, and insufficient vulnerability data to judge security, alongside uniformly positive but moderate engineer sentiment. Subject to further security analysis, the paper recommends productionising Copilot at ANZ, noting 1000+ engineers had already adopted it with a deeper productivity study underway.

## Problem & Motivation

Generative AI is framed as the next wave of productivity through operational efficiency and faster, better-informed decisions, with developers increasingly using tools like Copilot as pair-programmers that generate syntactically correct, contextually relevant snippets and explanations so engineers can spend more capacity on innovation rather than boilerplate. Against that promise stands a set of corporate anxieties that matter doubly inside a bank: intellectual property leakage, data security, privacy, and the risk of unintentionally shipping insecure or low-quality code at machine speed, all of which demand quantitative and qualitative evidence before a 5000-engineer organisation commits. Prior work offered suggestive but incomplete guidance — a Microsoft study of 95 engineers found Copilot users 55.8 percent faster on a JavaScript HTTP server, comparisons of Copilot against CodeWhisperer and ChatGPT favoured Copilot in engineering contexts, sentiment studies were positive, and correctness studies warned that Copilot writes plenty of lines but also plenty of deletable or subtly buggy ones whose danger depends heavily on the reviewer's expertise. ANZ therefore posed four direct questions: do its developers feel positive and empowered with Copilot, do they work faster, do they output better code, and is the suggested code secure, plus how often suggestions are accepted and whether they follow best practices. The study's stated purpose is thus twofold: to give a methodical playbook for taking AI pair-programming from experiment to large scale (including legal and security review, participant guidelines, and measurement design), and to supply statistical measures of productivity, quality, and security grounded in ANZ's own engineers rather than vendor benchmarks.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Corporate-scale crossover experiment design:** rather than a one-shot lab trial, the authors run a six-week, two-phase protocol inside a real bank — two weeks of preparation plus four weeks of active testing — with phase 1 devoted to proposed use-cases and every-second-day sentiment surveys, and phase 2 structured as a week-3/week-4 crossover in which the Control and Copilot groups are reversed so the same engineers appear on both sides, controlling for individual skill while the Control group retains internet and Stack Overflow access.
2. **Constrained, uniform measurement harness:** to keep comparison rigorous the team standardises on Visual Studio Code as the single IDE for Copilot telemetry, Python as the sole language for hypothesis testing, twelve short algorithmic challenges for time-boxed participation, and self-reported minutes per problem instead of intrusive monitoring, triangulated across four independent sources — GitHub Copilot metrics (suggestions shown, accepted, fully-accepted lines, language acceptance rates), surveys (baseline, week 1–2, and per-challenge week 3–4), SonarQube static analysis for smells and vulnerabilities, and team grading of correctness.
3. **Non-parametric hypothesis-testing pipeline for skewed engineering data:** after removing 6 duplicates and 22 unsolved attempts to leave 172 data points, and after boxplots and QQ-plots show skewed, non-normal time-spent distributions with retained outliers, the authors deliberately choose the Wilcoxon signed-rank test over Mann-Whitney U on the grounds that the samples are dependent (same users in both groups), run one-sided at alpha 0.05 across productivity (total time), quality (unit-test success ratio, bugs, code smells), and security (vulnerabilities) metrics.
4. **Proficiency- and difficulty-stratified productivity accounting:** beyond the headline average, the analysis breaks mean minutes-per-problem and percentage improvement down by self-reported Python proficiency (merging Novice into Beginner and Expert into Advanced for sparsity) and by challenge difficulty, revealing that beginners gained the most (52.27 percent, 20.07 to 9.58 minutes), intermediates and advanced engineers still gained around 41 and 40 percent, and the largest gains came on Hard tasks, supporting the claim that Copilot helps across the skill spectrum.
5. **Embedded security probes plus post-production validation framing:** recognising that atomic puzzles rarely surface flaws, the authors insert two targeted security questions — a password-hashing task expecting pbkdf2_hmac with random salt and a FastAPI command-execution task requiring injection guards — scanned under SonarQube Sonar Way rules, and they frame the experiment as the first stage of a larger adoption arc, citing early production data from around 1000 engineers as initial validation for the controlled findings.

## Key Findings

The headline result is a large, statistically significant productivity effect: the Copilot group averaged 17.86 minutes per problem against 30.98 for Control (medians 10 vs 20, quartiles shifted down across the board), yielding roughly 42.36 percent faster completion with a Wilcoxon p-value of 0.001 on total time spent, less debugging-time share, and benefits visible from Very Easy through Hard tasks. Code quality also improved on the measures that reached significance: bugs (p = 0.033) and code smells (p = 0.007) were significantly lower with Copilot, implying more maintainable code less likely to break in production, while the unit-test success ratio ran 12.86 percent higher for Copilot code but at p = 0.060 fell short of significance, with beginners seeing the largest quality uplift. Security, by contrast, produced essentially no verdict: SonarQube returned only one non-zero vulnerability data point, so the hypothesis on vulnerabilities could not be tested, though the authors note the available evidence showed no major security issues introduced by Copilot. Sentiment across phase 1 was uniformly positive yet consistently shy of strongly positive, with median responses of a bit less debugging time, a bit more time needed without Copilot, suggestions rated somewhat helpful and aligned well with coding standards, and a positive effect on reviewing and understanding code, creating unit tests, and writing documentation. The authors are candid about limits that temper generalisation: fluctuating participation among just over 100 engineers against a 5000-engineer population, lightweight tasks that left little room for bugs or vulnerabilities and motivate a future project-style task, and self-report biases such as Dunning-Kruger proficiency ratings, under-reported time, and volunteer enthusiasm, assumed to balance across randomly composed groups.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The paper's central recommendation is to productionise GitHub Copilot at ANZ Bank, conditional on further analysis of the security of Copilot-suggested code, on the grounds that the productivity and quality gains are both statistically and practically significant and the qualitative feedback is encouraging. For researchers and engineering leaders repeating the work, the authors suggest replacing atomic algorithmic puzzles with project-style tasks that give bugs, smells, and vulnerabilities realistic room to emerge, since only code smells yielded analysable data here and the password use-case in particular drew fewer attempts. They also point toward the adoption programme already in motion — more than a thousand engineers using Copilot in daily workflows at the time of writing and a detailed follow-up investigation into productivity, operational efficiency, and overall performance — as the vehicle for validating the lab effect in production. The broader direction is a shift in engineering work itself: with Copilot absorbing repetitive coding, debugging overhead, and documentation and test scaffolding, engineers are expected to redirect effort toward creative and design tasks, while tooling, guidelines from the legal and security review, and ongoing telemetry on acceptance rates and standards alignment mature to make that shift safe.

## Authors & Institutions

The paper is authored by Sayan Chatterjee, Ching Louis Liu, Gareth Rowland, and Tim Hogarth, all of Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Limited in Melbourne, writing as an Architecture and Engineering initiative rather than an academic lab. The institutional context is ANZ Bank's engineering organisation of more than 5000 engineers spanning the full software development life cycle, with the experiment's 100-plus participants drawn mainly from Software, Cloud, and Data Engineering roles. Legal and security teams at ANZ contributed pre-experiment risk assessment of intellectual property, data security, and privacy, producing the participant Playbook governing Copilot use. GitHub Copilot itself, built on GPT technology refined on public GitHub code and evaluated here via its Visual Studio Code extension, is the studied tool, chosen over alternatives such as Code Whisperer as the pioneer offering at the time of the mid-2023 experiment.
