# RQ1: Extensively Studied and Under-Explored Aspects of In-IDE HAX
**In one sentence:** Evidence is richest for professional coding and maintenance with growing task variety, but frequent omission of the SDLC stage plus a focus on adopters leaves early/late stages and non-adopters under-explored, so stage and task reporting must be standardized.
## Key points
- The corpus splits into 71/90 professional-use papers and 19/90 educational-use papers, making professional settings the dominant evidence base.
- Within professional settings, most papers examine practical coding and maintenance work, while a large share evaluates assistance without situating it in a specific SDLC stage.
- Educational papers focus on learners using assistance to solve programming problems, but likewise often omit stage information, limiting comparison across contexts.
- Missing stage information weakens interpretability because the same interface may have different effects at requirements, implementation, testing, or evolution.
- Task design shifted over time from small algorithmic exercises resembling games and interview-style problems to project-level workflow analysis plus documentation, testing, debugging, and migration tasks.
- Broadening task scope improved ecological validity but increased the number of papers labelling the task as unspecified, so clearer descriptions and stable taxonomies are needed.
- Several papers frame AI assistance as pair programming with the system as collaborator, creating needs for model-interaction training and oversight in professional teams and control-preserving scaffolds for learners.

## Contexts of use
**Covers:** RQ1 context split and SDLC-stage reporting gap

| Context | Count (of 90) | Focus observed |
|---|---|---|
| Professional | 71/90 | Practical use in coding and maintenance; large share with no specific SDLC stage |
| Educational | 19/90 | Learners solving programming problems; stage information often omitted |

Stage omission limits cross-context comparison: the same interface may behave differently at requirements, implementation, testing, or evolution. Stated remedy: report the stage explicitly and use a simple task taxonomy enabling cross-paper synthesis.

## Task design over time
**Covers:** RQ1 evolution of study tasks

- Early studies: small algorithmic exercises resembling simple games and interview-style problems.
- More recent work: project-level analysis of workflows, plus tasks in documentation, testing, debugging, and migration.
- Effect: broader scope improves ecological validity but raises the number of papers with the task labelled unspecified.
- Stated remedy: clearer task descriptions and stable taxonomies to preserve comparability as scope widens.

## Pair-programming framing and resulting needs
**Covers:** RQ1 collaborator view of AI assistance

Several papers frame AI assistance as pair programming in which the system acts as a collaborator rather than a tool, aligning with the move from isolated completions to co-creative workflows. Concrete needs named: training in model interaction and oversight in professional teams, and scaffolds that keep learners in control in educational settings. ## Synthesis and next steps
**Covers:** RQ1 summary (p. 25 in source pagination)

The evidence is richest for professional coding and maintenance, with growing task variety and a notable SDLC-stage reporting gap. Prescribed next steps: include non-adopters, expand coverage to early and late stages, and standardize task and stage reporting so results compare across settings and over time.
