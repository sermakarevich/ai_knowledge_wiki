# Human-AI Experience in Integrated Development Environments: A Systematic Literature Review

**Paper:** [Human-AI Experience in Integrated Development Environments: A Systematic Literature Review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06195v3)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine a carpenter who has just been handed a very eager apprentice who can hammer nails in seconds but sometimes uses the wrong nails and never cleans up. This paper is like a foreman's report after watching 90 different workshops try working with that apprentice: everyone gets faster at the routine sawing and hammering, especially the experienced carpenters, but they now spend up to half their day double-checking the apprentice's work, beginners can pick up bad habits by trusting it blindly, and the finished furniture is sometimes wobbly or unsafe. The report concludes that the best workshops are the ones that keep the apprentice in sight at the workbench, ask it to explain what it did, and stay firmly in charge of when it helps.

## TL;DR

This is a PRISMA-guided systematic literature review of 90 empirical studies (selected from 257 identified, covering first public availability from January 2022 to November 2024) on Human-AI Experience inside IDEs, extending an earlier non-systematic survey of 36 papers. It organizes the literature into three dimensions — Impact (74/90 studies), Design (28/90), and Quality (19/90) — and maps study contexts (71 professional, 19 educational) and SDLC coverage, which concentrates on implementation while requirements, testing, and deployment remain thin. Its central synthesis is that AI assistance raises productivity, particularly for experienced developers, while introducing verification overhead of up to 50% of developer time, over-reliance risks especially among novices, and correctness, maintainability, and security risks in generated code. It closes with an agenda calling for larger and longer evaluations, stronger audit and verification assets, broader lifecycle coverage, and adaptive, explainable assistance under user control, alongside methodological reforms such as justified sample sizes, preregistration, open sharing, and academic–industry collaboration.

## Problem & Motivation

The integration of AI into IDEs is reshaping software development and fundamentally altering how developers interact with their tools, with surveys cited in the review reporting that 76% of respondents use or plan to use AI tools and over half report reduced search time, faster coding, quicker repetitive tasks, and higher productivity. The authors frame this as a shift from human-computer interaction to Human-AI Experience, where AI acts as an active collaborator rather than just a tool, and they narrow the scope deliberately to what changes where code is written, inspected, or tested rather than AI in software engineering broadly. Existing LLM-for-SE reviews summarize model capabilities, task coverage, and evaluation techniques but do not analyze the interaction experience inside developer workspaces where the lifecycle actually takes place, and research on in-IDE HAX remains fragmented despite rapid adoption. The review therefore sets three goals: identifying common themes and patterns, merging the current state of knowledge, and surfacing critical gaps to guide future research, positioning itself as the first systematic review of in-IDE HAX and a foundational reference for cumulative, reproducible progress.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **In-IDE HAX as a distinct object of study** — The review defines in-IDE HAX around workspaces offering code editing, immediate execution or preview, and contextual AI feedback in the same window, including desktop IDEs such as IntelliJ and VS Code, notebook environments such as Jupyter and Databricks, and qualifying plug-ins, while excluding web explainers and offline static analysers. This scoping decision keeps the synthesis anchored to the developer workspace rather than diffusing into general AI-assisted software engineering.
2. **PRISMA-guided SLR protocol with recall-first search and expert supplementation** — The method searches eight libraries (ACM Digital Library, DBLP, IEEE, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Scopus, Springer Link, arXiv) in November 2024 with a two-part Score/Side query, supplements database hits with 35 studies from the authors' prior survey and 30 expert-known studies, and holds every addition to the same eligibility and quality criteria. Screening moves 223 records through duplicate removal and out-of-scope exclusion to 79 full texts, adds backward snowballing rounds yielding 18 further papers, and settles after revision re-verification at a final corpus of 90 included studies.
3. **Three-dimension taxonomy of Impact, Design, and Quality** — Built through two-author open coding, codebook piloting, full coding with third-author adjudication, and consolidation of low-support categories, the taxonomy structures the entire synthesis: Impact covers effects on developers, tasks, and workflows; Design covers how AI is integrated into and interacted with in the IDE; and Quality covers properties of AI outputs such as correctness, security, and readability. The expanded corpus confirms the adequacy of this framing while adding finer subscopes without new top-level dimensions.
4. **SDLC-stage and context mapping as an analytic lens** — The review maps each study to Waterfall-model SDLC stages and to professional versus educational contexts, later adding task-type classifications, which exposes that most professional work clusters in coding and maintenance or omits stage information entirely and that educational work focuses on pedagogy with similarly weak stage specificity. This mapping turns reporting gaps themselves into findings and motivates the call for standardized stage and task reporting.
5. **Future-work meta-analysis across 250 statements** — Beyond synthesizing findings, the review open-codes 250 future-work statements by topic (what) and method (how), yielding 17 topics and 10 methodological strategies, with productivity factors, design of AI assistance, and audit of AI-generated code leading the counts. This produces a second-order agenda that connects fragmented suggestions into a coherent program of larger evaluations, verification assets, lifecycle coverage, and adaptive assistance.

## Key Findings

Impact dominates the corpus at 74 of 90 studies, and the consistent result is that AI assistance accelerates development by reducing context switches and boilerplate and letting developers offload repetitive work toward higher-level logic, with gains strongest among experienced users but marginal on proprietary or highly complex logic where assistants struggle with context-specific nuances. The offsetting cost is large: verifying suggestions, refining prompts, and reworking generated code can consume up to half of developers' time, because output is often syntactically valid yet subtly flawed, and mitigations such as highlighting tokens by predicted edit likelihood help direct attention and counter automation bias. Novices solve assignments faster with lower perceived workload and steady self-efficacy when use is structured, and prompting proves teachable, yet beginners over-trust output, lose sight of foundational concepts, and drift from correct solutions when misled, while professionals sometimes under-rely by rejecting correct output that lacks rationale. Attitude research, in 11 of 74 impact studies, centers on trust calibrated by suggestion quality and task stakes: high-quality context-relevant suggestions and brief explanations raise trust, inconsistency and needless complexity lower it, and trust falls for high-stakes, production, complex, or open-ended work while rising for routine or proof-of-concept tasks.

Design is addressed in 28 of 90 studies, contrasting low-friction autocompletion that minimizes cognitive load against conversational agents that support higher-level reasoning and iterative refinement, with the literature converging on hybrid models that combine both plus context awareness, explainability and transparency, and user control and adaptability. Prompting appears in 7 of 28 design studies as a strategic, teachable collaboration skill where structured and well-contextualized prompts outperform both vague and excessively detailed ones, and educational design calls for staged AI contribution with small purposeful friction and quick checks before adoption. Tool coverage is heavily skewed, with GitHub Copilot studied in 36 of 90 works and alternatives such as IDA, Prompt Sapper, CoLadder, PairBuddy, Slide4N, and RealHumanEval appearing only sparsely, creating overgeneralization risk.

Quality is examined in 19 of 90 studies across correctness, readability and maintainability, and security, finding an efficiency–correctness trade-off in which acceleration comes with subtle errors, insecure patterns, and harder-to-maintain code marked by overly concise structures, unconventional naming, and missing comments. Security estimates report up to 36% of vulnerabilities in AI-assisted code originating from the models via replicated insecure training-data patterns, and remedies proposed include filtering suboptimal suggestions, improved contextual prompting, adaptive personalization of when and how much to suggest, automated verification and static analysis, and developer education on verification and debugging strategies. Methodologically, the corpus comprises 32 experimental, 38 qualitative, 12 survey, and 20 otherwise-classified studies with frequent mixed methods, but samples are small with a median of 17 participants and rarely justified, evaluations are short-term, and reporting of SDLC stage and task is inconsistent, with 46 of 71 professional studies and most educational studies omitting stage information entirely.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The authors synthesize the 250 future-work statements into a programmatic call for larger and longer evaluations with broader sampling, realistic professional settings, attention to students and novices, and comparative and longitudinal designs that track how practices and effects evolve. Audit and verification proposals seek wider vulnerability and language coverage over time, targeted test suites and benchmarks, more unit tests and quality metrics, automatic test generation, reference-example retrieval, post-processing and filtering layers, and multi-alternative comparison interfaces that keep tests, static analysis, and runtime evidence inside the editor. Design proposals call for hybrid autocompletion-plus-conversation assistants that share context, offer brief explanations and uncertainty highlighting, and provide scope and frequency controls, alongside prompting research on structured conversational refinement, comparative strategy evaluation, multi-modal channels, prompt sanitization, and reusable collaborative prompts. The agenda further asks for personalization keyed to profile, expertise, and task state; explainability work on rationales, annotations, and mental-model calibration; trust research on community formation and interface-driven shifts with diverse developer groups; IDE redesign toward AI-aware environments with non-linear input, edit propagation, and memory management; coverage of requirements, design, debugging, testing, deployment, and search beyond implementation; inclusion of non-users and stopped users; and underexplored governance, user control, and proactivity themes. Methodologically, it prescribes a priori power analysis, saturation arguments, recruitment-constraint reporting, versioned repositories with codebooks, prompts, tasks, scripts, and deidentified data, plus preregistration, blinding, randomization, transparency checklists, and academic–industry collaboration to enable ecologically valid longitudinal work.
