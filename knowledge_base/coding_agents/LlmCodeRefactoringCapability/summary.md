# An Empirical Study on the Code Refactoring Capability of Large Language Models

**Paper:** [An Empirical Study on the Code Refactoring Capability of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02320v1) — arXiv cs.SE, 4 Nov 2024

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine hiring a tireless tidy-up crew to clean houses alongside experienced homeowners, where the crew is brilliant at obvious chores like throwing out junk, relabeling boxes, and untangling long knotted cords, but gets lost when asked to knock down walls or rewire the floor plan. That is essentially what this study finds for StarCoder2 versus human developers across 30 Java projects: the model removes far more surface clutter and rule-based smells, while developers remain better at deep structural jobs that require knowing how the whole house fits together. The catch is that the crew sometimes breaks things while cleaning, so only a little over half of its best clean-ups keep everything working, whereas the homeowners never break anything. Giving the crew one worked example and asking it to try five times and keep the best attempt turns it from a sloppy helper into a genuinely useful partner.

## TL;DR

This paper empirically evaluates StarCoder2-15B-instruct on Java refactoring against human developers using 30 open-source Java projects (filtered to exclude anything in the Stack v2 training data) and 5,194 pure-refactoring commits where 100% of churn is refactoring operations detected by RMiner. For each commit the model refactors the pre-refactoring file content via a zero-shot prompt, and quality is judged by DesigniteJava code-smell reduction, Understand code-metric improvement (complexity, cohesion, coupling, modularity) with improvement rate IR = (A_before − A_after) / A_before × 100, and unit-test pass rates under Pass@1/3/5 with best-of-N selection by smell reduction then metric gain, tested with Mann-Whitney U and Cliff's delta. On test-passing outputs StarCoder2 reduces smells by 44.36% versus 24.27% for developers (about 20 points higher, p = 0.003) and averages 19.32% metric improvement versus 17.46%, but only reaches 57.15% Pass@5 against developers' 100%, so comprehensive testing is mandatory. The advantage splits cleanly by kind: StarCoder2 wins 10 of 16 smell types and dominates systematic, within-class, syntactic work such as Long Statement, Magic Number, Empty Catch Clause, renames, and annotation changes, while developers win Broken Modularization, Deficient Encapsulation, Multifaceted Abstraction, Missing Default, coupling metrics, and dependency-heavy moves like Move Attribute and Change Attribute Type. One-shot prompting (34.51% pass, 42.97% smell reduction) and chain-of-thought with refactoring-type hints (32.22%, 42.34%) both reach Scott-Knott Rank 1 over zero-shot (28.36%, 39.45% Rank 2), and generating five candidates per input beats generating one by roughly 29 points of pass rate, making one-shot plus multi-generation the recommended recipe.

## Problem & Motivation

Large language models promise to automate coding and lift code quality, yet because they are trained on vast piles of open-source code, any flaws, bad idioms, and technical debt in that training data can leak into what they generate, raising maintenance costs instead of lowering them. Code refactoring, defined here as improving internal structure, readability, and maintainability without changing external behavior, is the standard remedy, and its success is conventionally tracked by eliminating code smells that violate design principles. Although early studies fed prompts plus code to models like GPT-3 and reported encouraging anecdotes, the authors argue there was no systematic study of whether LLM-generated refactorings actually improve quality more or less than developer refactorings, which refactoring and smell types each side handles best, and whether prompting can close the gap. Without that, teams cannot know when to trust the model, when to keep humans in the loop, or how to prompt for safe clean-ups.

A second motivation is methodological honesty around data leakage and fair comparison. Popular closed models do not disclose training data, so a model evaluated on projects it already saw can look artificially good, and commits that mix features or bug fixes with refactoring muddy who improved what. The study therefore chooses StarCoder2-15B-instruct precisely because it is trained on the public Stack v2 (with a 46.3% HumanEval pass@1 anchor), filters from 765 Apache projects in 20-MAD down to 195 Java projects and finally to 30 leak-free projects with at least the median of 129 refactoring commits, and keeps only commits whose refactoring ratio equals 100% so the before/after contrast is pure refactoring. On that controlled ground it asks four questions: whether the LLM can outperform developers overall, which smell types each side reduces best, which refactoring types are most effective for each side, and whether one-shot and chain-of-thought prompting improve the LLM.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Leakage-controlled, developer-paired refactoring benchmark** — Starting from the 20-MAD Apache corpus, the study applies Java-dominance, commit-count, and lifespan filters, removes all 135 projects overlapping StarCoder2 training data, and balances to 30 projects, then extracts only pure-refactoring commits via RMiner (59 types, ~99.7% precision) so StarCoder2 and the developer refactor exactly the same pre-refactoring snippets and can be compared apples to apples.

2. **Three-axis quality framework with Pass@k validity gating** — Every refactoring is scored on unit-test survival (Pass@1 generating one candidate, Pass@3 three, Pass@5 five, succeeding if any candidate passes, with ties broken toward most smell reduction then best metric gain), DesigniteJava smell reduction across 46 smell types, and Understand metric improvement across coupling, cohesion, complexity, and modularity, with significance via Mann-Whitney U at p < 0.05 and magnitude via Cliff's delta, and only test-passing LLM outputs counted toward quality claims.

3. **Per-smell-type effectiveness split** — Rather than reporting a single reduction number, the study compares per-project reduction distributions for each of 16 observed smell types, revealing that the LLM sweeps syntactic, repetitive implementation smells (Long Statement, Long Parameter List, Long Identifier, Empty Catch Clause, Magic Number, Complex Conditional, Complex Method) and simple design clean-ups (Unutilized/Unnecessary Abstraction, Broken Hierarchy, Cyclic-Dependent Modularization), while developers dominate architecture-sensitive smells (Broken/ Insufficient Modularization, Deficient Encapsulation, Multifaceted Abstraction, Missing Default).

4. **Per-refactoring-type preference and payoff analysis** — Using RMiner 3.0 (up to 102 types) the study contrasts frequency distributions with per-type smell-reduction and metric-improvement payoffs, showing StarCoder2 favors and wins on frequent syntactic operations like Rename Method, Extract Method, Rename Class, annotation add/remove/modify, and Move-and-Rename variants, whereas developers favor and win on dependency-propagating operations like Move Method, Move Attribute, Change Attribute Type, Encapsulate Attribute, Change Attribute Access Modifier, Extract Superclass, and Pull Up Method.

5. **Prompt-and-sampling recipe for better refactorings** — The study contrasts the sparse zero-shot instruction prompt against a one-shot prompt that prepends a real unrefactored/refactored example pair and a chain-of-thought prompt that supplies the developer's refactoring types plus definitions and demands a step-by-step rationale, evaluated with Scott-Knott clustering, and crosses this with generating one versus three versus five candidates, establishing that examples plus repeated sampling materially raise both correctness and smell removal and even unlock seven refactoring types unseen under zero-shot.

## Key Findings

On overall effectiveness, StarCoder2 looks like a powerful but unreliable cleaner. Unrefactored code holds 17,429 smells and developers cut it to 13,199 for a 24.27% reduction, while StarCoder2's test-passing subset falls from 12,213 to 6,795 for a 44.36% reduction, roughly 20 points higher and significant at p = 0.003, with average metric improvement of 19.32% (median 18.2%) versus 17.46% (median 16.9%) for developers. The model leads on cohesion and complexity, notably PercentLackOfCohesionModified at 23.9% versus 21.5% (delta 0.42 medium), AvgCyclomatic 17.4% versus 14.6% (0.45 medium), and SumCyclomatic 18.6% versus 15.9% (0.47 medium), plus instance-variable and cohesion gains, while developers lead on coupling-sensitive CountClassCoupled (24.1% versus 21.4%) and CountDeclClassVariable (14.9% versus 12.5%), exactly where class-dependency judgment matters. Reliability tempers the win: developers pass 100% of unit tests as accepted pull requests, StarCoder2 manages only about 28.4% median (28.36% reported) at Pass@1, 48.5% at Pass@3, and 57.2% median (57.15% reported) at Pass@5, so moving from one to three to five generations adds roughly 20 then 9 points and lifts smell reduction by 4.91% via Rebellious Hierarchy, Long Method, and Long Statement, which is why the remaining analyses use the best-of-five and why the paper urges comprehensive testing.

The type-level splits explain where each side should be deployed. StarCoder2 wins 10 of 16 smell types, taking 7 of 8 implementation smells with large effects (deltas roughly 0.53 to 0.63) and splitting design 4–4, confirming it thrives where structured, rule-based corrections apply and struggles where architecture, encapsulation, and cross-class reasoning dominate. The refactoring-type payoffs mirror this: StarCoder2 wins 9 of 11 significant smell-reduction types (notably Remove/Modify/Add Method and Class Annotation with deltas above 0.85–0.96, Move-and-Rename Method 0.94, Rename Class 0.59) and 3 of 5 significant metric types (Add Class Annotation 0.85, Change Return Type 0.80, Rename Class 0.61), while developers win only Move Attribute (0.78) and Change Attribute Type (0.78) on smells and Encapsulate Attribute (0.68) plus Change Attribute Access Modifier (0.72) on metrics, all attribute-level work steeped in data dependencies. Qualitatively, StarCoder2 leans on renames, annotation edits, access-modifier tweaks, Change Type Declaration Kind, and high-level Move Package, whereas developers handle Extract Method, Inline Variable, Extract Superclass, Pull Up Method, and granular Move Source Folder, leading the authors to frame the pair as complementary rather than substitutable.

Prompting and sampling turn out to be first-order levers rather than polish. Zero-shot sits at 28.36% pass and 39.45% smell reduction (Rank 2), chain-of-thought rises to 32.22% and 42.34% (Rank 1), and one-shot peaks at 34.51% and 42.97% (Rank 1), translating to about +6.15 points of pass rate and +3.52 points of smell reduction for one-shot over zero-shot and +3.86/+2.89 for chain-of-thought. One-shot leads complexity metrics (Avg/Cyclomatic/MaxCyclomatic Rank 1) and CountClassCoupledModified at 20.1%, chain-of-thought leads CountClassCoupled at 23.4% and CountDeclClassVariable at 14.0% while adding seven new refactoring types (Extract Method, Rename Method, Extract Variable, Inline Method, Add Parameter, Extract Class, Parameterize Variable), with average metric gains of 20.15% one-shot and 19.99% chain-of-thought versus 19.32% zero-shot. The practical takeaway the abstract foregrounds is the combination: one-shot guidance plus five generations per input delivers the highest pass rate and deepest cleaning, far above single-shot zero-shot.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The paper's own prescription is to stop asking who wins and start combining strengths, using StarCoder2 for high-volume syntactic and within-class clean-ups like long statements, magic numbers, renames, and annotation hygiene while reserving developers for modularization, encapsulation, coupling, and hierarchy design where context and trade-offs dominate. Operationally that means always pairing the model with strong unit tests or other correctness gates because hallucinated yet plausible edits persist, and defaulting to one-shot prompts with concrete before/after examples plus best-of-five sampling (selecting by smell reduction then metric gain) rather than single zero-shot attempts, with chain-of-thought type hints as a close alternative when broadening refactoring variety or tackling coupling matters. For research, the conclusion and threats point to task-specific fine-tuning on refactoring data, prompt-design refinement including structured reasoning variants, and replication beyond the current envelope: more diverse open-source and industrial projects, languages beyond Java, newer StarCoder versions and other vendor models, multi-commit refactoring scopes, richer quality lenses such as readability and performance, and cross-checking RMiner, DesigniteJava, and Understand with alternative toolchains since similar-but-unseen training patterns and tool choice can shift results.
