# Andrew Southall | What Codex Actually Delivered in Truly Analytics

**Article:** [What Codex Actually Delivered in Truly Analytics](https://southall.solutions/articles/truly-analytics-ai-development/) — southall.solutions

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine hiring a tireless junior builder who can frame walls and hang drywall all day but cannot design the house, wire the electrics, or decide where the plumbing should go. That is what Codex was on this project: solo engineer Andrew Southall went from an empty repository to a working privacy-friendly analytics product in six weeks, with the AI pouring in over forty percent of all code written. But like cheap framing that later needs re-measuring, more than half of the AI's work was ripped out and replaced, while nearly three-quarters of the human's work survived to launch. The lesson is simple: the robot is a brilliant bricklayer for routine, well-known jobs, yet the architect with judgment about structure, safety, and what the customer actually needs still decides what stands.

## TL;DR

The article analyses the git history of Truly Analytics, a drop-in privacy-conscious Google Analytics replacement built solo by Andrew Southall from first commit to v1.0 in six weeks using OpenAI Codex (GPT 5.3-codex) as an engineering accelerator inside an isolated containerised pipeline. Across 137 commits and 21,031 committed lines, Codex wrote 8,886 lines (42.3%) in 30 commits while the human wrote 12,145 lines (57.7%) in 107 commits, yet only 46.5% of AI lines survived to v1.0 versus 72.1% of human lines, leaving v1.0 at 12,892 lines of which 67.9% is human and 32.1% is AI. AI contributions that survived concentrate in bounded pattern-driven work such as Go server routing scaffolding, SQL-row-to-struct boilerplate, caching, email integration, Kubernetes scaffolding, and admin-panel JSX, while human code dominates product-specific and production-sensitive areas including client-side analytics, enrichment logic, event handling, infrastructure, and final integration. The central thesis is that AI-assisted development is highly effective for bounded rote tasks but human judgment remains decisive where architecture, product context, security, and operational risk matter, so the useful metric is not how much code AI generates but how much of it survives review, replacement, and release.

## Problem & Motivation

The piece starts from a decade-old itch: while working at a publishing and media firm, Southall kept running into ad-blocked analytics and once patched the problem with a weekend proxy that died on geolocation, and after freelancing, shifting life circumstances, and a hiring market he wanted no part of, he decided to productionise the idea quickly without shipping garbage. The product answer is Truly Analytics, a drop-in Google Analytics replacement that sits on the customer's site, handles privacy and consent more carefully than stock GA, and feeds cleaned data back into the existing analytics stack so teams keep their tooling, with later additions such as Network Intelligence and Event Hooks deliberately excluded from this analysis. The methodological motivation is frustration with the unending stream of productivity claims, from vendors boasting that most of their code is now AI-generated to anonymous screeching about output, all issued without evidence from a finished crunch with a known shipped result. As a solo engineer working for months with the model as a reliable deckhand beside him, Southall realised he had no real sense of who was actually putting the code in, so he turned the v1.0 git history into the dataset: every committed line attributed to a human git user or the Codex git user, measured through to what survived in the release, complemented by the non-quantifiable experience of reviewing, rejecting, and reworking the agent's pull requests.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Survival rate, not generation volume, is the honest AI metric.** The article's signature move is to reject lines-generated leaderboard thinking and instead ask how much AI code survives real development, review, replacement, and release. By tracing 21,031 committed lines to the 12,892 that lived in v1.0, it converts vibes about acceleration into two durable numbers, 46.5% AI survival versus 72.1% human survival, plus the directional finding that 76% of removed AI code was overwritten by the human while only 4.5% of human code was overwritten by AI. The framing is memorable because it treats deletion and replacement as signal rather than noise, giving teams a replicable way to audit agent value instead of quoting commit counts.

2. **The isolated PR-levelled wrapper pipeline as a precondition for net gain.** Rather than letting the agent roam, Southall ran Codex GPT 5.3-codex inside an isolated containerised pipeline with a strict wrapper that provisioned the image, fed prompts from a configured library, captured output, and handled all branching, pushing, and PR creation, with the agent holding no direct access to git, secrets, databases, or internal services. The rationale is laid out bluntly: no time to clean up agent-scale messes, Codex sometimes forgot to commit and trashed work in transitory Kubernetes jobs, and secrets should never flow to an external model, so determinism and containment were designed in from the start. The verdict is that without this pipeline the work would have become a loss, and with it the agent's output became a gain, making the harness itself a first-class contribution of the piece.

3. **Bounded pattern-driven work versus judgment-driven work as the dividing line.** The analysis sorts the codebase by where each party's code lived and lasted, arguing that Codex excels exactly where tasks are rote, conventional, and abundantly exemplified on the open web, and fails exactly where the work is novel, product-specific, or load-bearing at 3 a.m. Server route handling, database access scaffolding including the hand-rolled SQL-to-struct layer that replaced an ORM, in-memory caching, SES email templates, Kubernetes API plumbing, and admin-panel JSX all persisted because each has one conventional good way of doing it that a model can memorise and emit on demand. Data enrichment, event handling, per-datum management, client analytics code shipped to every visitor's browser, and battle-tested infrastructure files copied from prior projects stayed human because nothing in the training data knows this product's answers, and correctness margins there are razor thin.

4. **The copy-paste machine theory of frontend and the JavaScript quality diagnosis.** A deliberately provocative idea is that frontend JSX success is unsurprising because copying component patterns from open-source libraries was already the norm before LLMs, so the model is just a sped-up customisable version of that habit, which explains why basically all admin-panel code and one-shot styling like the upgrade page survived untouched. The mirror claim is a self-described honest-jerk diagnosis of why `client/` analytics code failed horribly: the public JavaScript corpus over-represents lower-skill code that is none of performant, clear, modular, minimal, or dependency-free, JavaScript as a language enforces less correctness than Go or Rust so bad code leaks into training data, and the agent could verify Go builds but was winging it on the JS side, leaving the human to own 82.4% of surviving client lines. Together these turn a language-war rant into a testable account of where training-data quality and tooling feedback decide agent outcomes.

## Key Findings

Quantitatively, the six-week build produced 137 commits, of which the human made 107 (78.1%) and Codex made 30 (21.9%), and of 21,031 committed lines the AI wrote 8,886 (42.3%) against 12,145 human lines (57.7%). Only 12,892 lines (61.3%) survived to v1.0, splitting into 8,759 human lines (67.9% of the release) and 4,133 AI lines (32.1%), which yields the headline survival gap of 46.5% for AI against 72.1% for the human, with 4,753 AI lines replaced or deleted versus 3,386 human lines. Overwrite direction is strongly asymmetric: 3,610 of the removed AI lines (76%) were overwritten by the human, while just 546 human lines (4.5% of human output) were overwritten by AI, and of 30 Codex PRs, 25 were accepted (83.3%) with 5 rejected (16.7%). Location analysis sharpens the picture considerably: surviving `src` Go is near parity at 2,211 AI versus 2,671 human lines, JSX leans AI (607 vs 474) and JavaScript leans AI harder (683 vs 310), HTML leans human (870 vs 519, with Codex supplying the test framework the human tuned), and the browser-delivered `client/` analytics code is overwhelmingly human at 1,654 versus 353 AI lines after an effective post-release human refactor. Auxiliary ground is almost entirely human except for docs, with Ansible, project-root boilerplate, and simple Kubernetes manifests carried over as battle-tested copies described as the family AK-47 versus something 3D-printed, while docs are entirely AI-generated because the senior engineer never looks at them. Behavioural signatures also diverge: the human deletes with abandon including a zero-addition 900-line deletion commit and a 1,414-added / 1,000-deleted monster commit, AI dumps code in with an 800-added / 150-deleted outlier, all the largest files are AI-dominated while the human clusters around 300-line files and rarely exceeds 500, and early AI scaffolding commits over 1,000 lines give way to sustained human refinement through the first two-thirds of May plus a late AI return before release. Qualitatively, the five rejected PRs are instructive rather than incidental: a trivial Tailwind bump handled manually, a 400-addition debugging PR born of a wrong instruction, a stale-branch merge speedbump, and two prime examples of agentic over-engineering in client timing code dismissed as utter trash and in trial-restriction evaluation redone by the human in about ten minutes, both binned for comprehension, maintainability, and design reasons. The author is candid about bias, noting a sample of one engineer and one agent cannot wash out individual variation, yet argues the rarity of a complete blank-repo-to-release audit makes the study worth doing.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The explicit recommendation is to reuse AI in this manner again but never without the isolated pipeline and its process controls, keeping agent code away from production databases, desktops, and secrets because a single destructive action could undo all accumulated benefit. Within that harness, the advised placement is as a research and typing-automation tool and as the right hand for small throwaway tools, supporting software, isolated standalone pieces, dictation of half-formed thoughts, documentation, debugging help, and anywhere the output shape is already known, which at $20 a month is judged absolutely worth more than that for the mental energy it saves on tedium. The counter-advice is equally firm: do not assign novel, independent, sensible work where the expected output is unknown, because that remains a way off and not viable in his experience, and keep strict human ownership over the code that wakes you at 3 a.m., namely Go engineering, Ansible and automation, and build tooling that should just take input and produce output. On vendors, the stated end state is to stay on ChatGPT and Codex because prompt libraries are already tuned to them and Codex runs the way he wants, rejecting Gemini on working-relationship grounds, Anthropic on operational awkwardness plus distrust of its leadership and security claims, and Copilot with a one-line dismissal, while deferring open-source models to a later API-key swap currently blocked by missing image generation and weaker retrieval-augmented search. A concrete next step named is moving tedious private business processes such as GTM automation onto a local model that handles email and simple text without leaving the premises, and small projects like browser extensions are flagged as the sweet spot where bot output barely needs touching.
