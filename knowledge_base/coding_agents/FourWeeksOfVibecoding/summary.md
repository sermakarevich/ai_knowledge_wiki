# 4 Weeks of Vibecoding: What I Built, What I Learned, and… | Pokharna Talks

**Article:** [4 Weeks of Vibecoding: What I Built, What I Learned, and…](https://pokharnatalks.com/blog/4-weeks-of-vibecoding-what-i-built-what-i-learned-and-whats-next) — Pokharna Talks, March 2026

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine someone who has never cooked suddenly running a busy restaurant just by describing each dish in vivid detail while a team of expert chefs does all the chopping and cooking. That is what happened here: a complete non-coder went from not knowing how to set up a GitHub repo to shipping four live websites in four weeks simply by speaking exactly what he wanted into his phone. The lesson is like learning that the architect matters more than the bricklayer — if you know the house you want down to the last door handle and can explain it clearly, the AI builders will build it for you.

## TL;DR

The article argues that vibecoding is a misnomer: it is not coding in flow but specifying vision, domain knowledge, and exact desired behaviour in enough detail for AI coding tools such as Claude Code to execute. As evidence, the author — a zero-technical coliving veteran who on February 12, 2026 could not set up a GitHub repo or write a commit message — reports 197 commits and four shipped projects by March 12, including a full two-sided marketplace and a large content-platform rebuild. His process replaces Jira tickets with voice-memo product requirements transcribed, structured, and fed to AI, turning prompts into live features within hours. The closing takeaway is that the dividing line is no longer technical versus non-technical but clarity versus lack of clarity, and that intent plus precise description are the only things that matter.

## Problem & Motivation

The piece starts from intimidation and exclusion: for months the author avoided vibecoding because the word itself sounds technical, as if it requires syntax, frameworks, and flow-state coding skill. Behind that is a broader problem faced by domain experts with deep product understanding but no implementation path — people who know exactly what operators, tenants, and readers need yet cannot ship because they do not know how to code, set up a repository, or understand the technology stack. The motivation is therefore both personal and demonstrative: to show that a decade of domain experience in coliving, advising dozens of operators and running a large newsletter, can become shippable software once the bottleneck shifts from writing code to describing intent. The article wants to retire the excuses for not starting by proving, with live production systems rather than toy demos, that precise problem understanding now suffices to get things built.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Vibecoding is specification, not coding.** The central redefinition is stated verbatim as you do not write code but write the details of how you want things to be done and what exactly you want them to do, with AI tools acting purely as executors. The author stresses he still does not know JavaScript, TypeScript, Prisma, or why Next.js has an App Router, yet he succeeds because he knows listing fields, admin permissions, discovery-to-booking journeys, and SEO architecture. In this framing, domain clarity substitutes for technical skill, and the misnamed term should be understood as vision plus exact behaviour description.

2. **Speak in PRD: the voice-memo product pipeline.** The operational idea is a pipeline where product requirements are thought through while walking, driving, or between calls — user flows, data models, edge cases, and dislikes of existing solutions — then spoken into a voice memo app, transcribed and structured by AI into a specification, and handed to Claude Code for execution. Writing PRDs, described as one of the most significant product-manager tasks, becomes a spoken skill, and the deliverable changes from a Jira ticket lingering in backlog for weeks to a detailed prompt that becomes a live feature within hours or the same day.

3. **Intent over tools, clarity over technicality.** The closing thesis reframes the whole story as not a story about AI tools but a story about intent, where the only thing that matters is whether you know what you want to build and are willing to describe it precisely enough for it to get built. Technical versus non-technical is declared obsolete as a dividing line and replaced by people who have clarity on what needs to exist versus people who do not. The listed blockers — not knowing how to code, set up a repository, or understand the technology — are acknowledged as valid but declared irrelevant now.

## Key Findings

In one month the author moved from zero shipped code to a reported 197 commits and more shipped product than in the previous two years combined, and more than most funded startups ship in a quarter. The portfolio is presented as production-grade rather than landing pages: BookMyColiving.com as a full two-sided marketplace with tenant, operator, and admin portals, moderation workflows, messaging, reviews, lead management, CSV exports, and over 2,500 SEO pages on Next.js, TypeScript, Prisma, PostgreSQL, and Vercel; the EverythingColiving.com rebuild as a rethink from more than 170 pages toward over 300 pages with 15 interactive operator tools such as ROI, pricing, vacancy-cost, and readiness calculators plus directories and a templates library; JumboTiger as a complete PRD written as a Claude Code execution document covering architecture, module deep dives, design system, technical specs, and content strategy; and PokharnaTalks.com itself rebuilt as a first learning build with a full blog engine and a filterable portfolio About page. The process finding is that detailed spoken specifications are sufficient to drive this output without clarifying questions in at least the PRD case, compressing idea-to-live-feature cycles from weeks to hours. The conceptual finding is that deep domain knowledge — exact fields, permissions, journeys, and content structures — transfers directly into working software when expressed precisely, while lack of language or framework knowledge does not block progress.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The article's explicit direction is a call to action rather than a research roadmap: the hundred reasons for not starting are set aside, and readers who know what they want to build are urged to start now because the tools are ready and the open question is whether they are. Implicitly, the suggested practice is to invest effort in precise description — learning to speak in PRD, narrating user flows, data, edge cases, and acceptance details out loud, then systematising transcription-to-specification-to-execution as a daily habit. For domain experts, the future direction is to treat backlogs of known needs as prompt-ready work rather than hiring-dependent work, beginning with a small rebuild for fluency and scaling toward marketplaces, tool libraries, and full execution documents. No technical future work is proposed; the frontier named in the piece is personal clarity and willingness to do the descriptive work.
