# Do Influence Tactics Matter? Investigating Prompt Framing Effects in LLM Code Generation

**Paper:** [Do Influence Tactics Matter? Investigating Prompt Framing Effects in LLM Code Generation (Deaconu et al., 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11513v1)

## Human Readable TL;DR

Imagine asking two chefs to cook the same dish, but barking at one "you must finish now or else, I'm watching you" while simply handing the other the recipe. Both know how to cook, but the pressured chef rushes, skips seasoning notes, and is more likely to burn the dish. This paper ran that experiment on AI coding assistants at massive scale, wrapping identical programming tasks in different management styles borrowed from workplace psychology — friendly flattery, logical persuasion, formal rules, or urgent threats — and found that threatening, urgency-laden wording made the code more often wrong and less secure, while polite or formal framings changed the explanation style and documentation without reliably improving quality.

## TL;DR

This is the first large-scale empirical study of influence-induced prompt framing in software engineering code generation, operationalizing eight influence tactics from the Yukl and Falbe taxonomy into reproducible IBQ-G-grounded prompt templates and testing them across five open-weight LLMs on LiveCodeBench (1,055 structured algorithmic problems, release_v6) and SWE-bench Verified (485 real-world GitHub maintenance tasks), for roughly 123,000 and 57,000 generations respectively. The authors frame any effects as prompt-level distributional cues learned from human communication data rather than evidence that models understand persuasion, and they evaluate four quality dimensions — functional correctness, quality, maintainability, and security — with static metrics (cyclomatic complexity, Maintainability Index, PyLint, SLOC/comment density, Bandit) plus a qualitative codebook analysis of tone, structure, and reliability. On LiveCodeBench, tactic framing significantly affected only functional correctness and Bandit security warnings, with Neutral outperforming Pressure and Pressure-Alternative on correctness and Pressure framings associated with more security warnings, while most maintainability and style metrics were driven by model choice and task difficulty; on SWE-bench Verified, no tactic main effect was significant and model architecture dominated, with Pressure only increasing verbosity. Qualitatively, Legitimating produced more technical, better-commented, error-handling responses and Exchange and Personal Appeal produced more explanations, while Pressure and Rational Persuasion showed higher repeating-hallucination rates.

## Problem & Motivation

Large language models now support core software engineering work such as code synthesis, documentation, bug repair, and testing, and prior prompt-engineering research has shown that wording and structure matter, from in-context examples and chain-of-thought reasoning to decomposition, constrained decoding, multi-agent repair loops, and clarity principles. What remained unexplored was the pragmatic, social layer of prompting: developers naturally talk to models conversationally, using rational justification, deadline urgency, flattery, friendship appeals, or policy invocations, and organizational psychology shows these framings change human compliance and task performance, with rational and personal tactics generally helping and pressure reliably hurting. Because models are trained on vast corpora of human communication, they may have learned statistical associations between such pragmatic framings and response styles, so that coercive language co-occurs with short task-focused completions and formal language with structured documented ones, acting as distributional cues without any genuine understanding of intent. The paper therefore asks to what extent prompt framings inspired by influence tactics lead to measurable differences in LLM-generated code, with the practical goal of evidence-based guidance for transparent, interpretable human-AI interaction and of surfacing risks from psychologically framed prompts in real workflows.

## Main Original Ideas

1. **Influence-tactic prompt operationalization from IBQ-G** — Starting from the eleven-tactic Yukl framework, the authors exclude Apprising, Collaboration, Consultation, and Coalition as implausible or ambiguous for one-shot developer-LLM solving, then turn each remaining tactic's four Influence Behavior Questionnaire-General behavioural items into reproducible template sentences (with item indices tracked), yielding seven tactics plus a Pressure-Alternative phrasing and a Neutral baseline for nine conditions, each adapted to LiveCodeBench and SWE-bench Verified settings in a consistent semi-formal tone refined over three author rounds.

2. **Distributional-cue theoretical framing** — Rather than claiming models experience pressure or flattery, the study explicitly grounds hypotheses in surface lexical and pragmatic features and interprets any shifts as prompt-level steering signals interacting with training and decoding, expecting non-uniform effects concentrated where models have interpretive flexibility such as verbosity, commenting, and reasoning depth rather than where test suites tightly constrain correctness.

3. **Complementary two-benchmark, four-dimension design** — LiveCodeBench covers precise, low-ambiguity algorithmic problem solving across easy, medium, and hard levels to test RQ1 on framing effects under varying complexity, while SWE-bench Verified covers open-ended multi-file maintenance and debugging requiring context comprehension to test RQ2, jointly assessed on correctness, quality, maintainability, and security, with RQ3 adding a qualitative codebook analysis restricted to LiveCodeBench where outputs are consistently interpretable.

4. **Large-scale controlled execution with mixed-methods analysis** — Five open-weight models served via a single API (Llama 3.1 8B, Llama 3.3 70B, Llama 4 Maverick 17B 128e, DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 70B, Qwen 3 32B non-reasoning) each cover all nine prompt conditions with fixed decoding settings, scripted code and diff extraction, and official evaluation harnesses, analyzed with linear mixed models and negative-binomial GLMMs using ProblemID random intercepts and Neutral baselines with Bonferroni-adjusted post hocs, complemented by delta-based multi-file metric aggregation for SWE-bench and a 350-sample qualitative coding effort reaching inter-rater reliability above 0.90 across thirteen topics.

## Key Findings

On structured LiveCodeBench tasks, influence framing significantly affected functional correctness and low-severity Bandit security warnings but not Maintainability Index, complexity, SLOC, comment percentage, or PyLint as main effects, with Neutral achieving higher correctness than both Pressure and Pressure-Alternative and Pressure framings associated with more security warnings, at small effect sizes alongside much larger model and difficulty effects. A representative Llama 3.1 example makes this concrete: the Neutral prompt produced a correct, documented dynamic-programming solution with docstring, type hints, and comments, while the identical task under Pressure produced a compressed, minimally explained response with incorrect boolean-reachability logic and poor comments. Pairwise texture remains beneath the null main effects, with Neutral beating Exchange on Maintainability Index and comments, Legitimating beating Neutral on comment density, and Exchange showing fewer warnings than Pressure variants, while three-way tactic-by-model-by-difficulty interactions appear for comments and PyLint. On real-world SWE-bench Verified maintenance tasks the tactic signal largely vanishes, with no significant tactic main effect on correctness, maintainability, complexity, comments, PyLint, or security and only Pressure producing significantly more verbose code than Neutral, whereas model choice dominates every metric. Qualitatively, framing partially shaped completeness, reliability, and verbosity: Legitimating elicited more technical style, problem-description openings, good comments, and error handling, Exchange and Personal Appeal elicited more code explanations, Ingratiation and Inspirational Appeal skewed direct, and Rational Persuasion and Pressure showed notably higher repeating-hallucination rates against the lowest rates for Neutral and Exchange. The overall picture is therefore mixed and context-dependent: most static quality metrics resist superficial linguistic manipulation, model selection and task difficulty matter far more than wording, and the one robust caution concerns coercive urgency-based phrasing.

## Suggestions & Future Directions

The central practical recommendation is to avoid coercive or urgency-based prompt wording when correctness or security is valued, since pressure framings were linked to lower correctness and more security warnings on LiveCodeBench and to increased verbosity on SWE-bench even in these benign, non-adversarial tasks, a pattern the authors note aligns with prior work on coercive language and with very high success rates for persuasion-based jailbreaks in adversarial settings. Developers who are locked into a specific model API may cautiously use framing to nudge stylistic features such as verbosity, explanations, or documentation-heavy output for templates and onboarding materials, but they should prioritize model selection over prompt framing for reliability in high-stakes settings and should not rely on framing as a consistent lever for quality. For researchers, influence framing deserves attention as a dimension of human-LLM collaboration, alignment, and evaluation consistency, with open work needed to isolate causal mechanisms from interactions with temperature, dataset context, and architecture, to extend beyond Python-only tasks and the Llama-heavy open-weight pool, and to test whether framing effects grow stronger in open-ended or adversarial dialogue than in constrained code generation with strict correctness criteria.
